Three quantities, three units

Almost every electrical number you will read off a nameplate, a meter, or a schematic is one of three quantities. Each has a name, a quantity symbol used in formulas, and a unit symbol written after a value.

Current, I
The rate at which electric charge flows past a point. Its unit is the ampere, symbol A — one of the seven SI base units. One ampere is one coulomb of charge per second.
Voltage, V
Also called electric potential difference: the difference in electric potential between two points. Its unit is the volt, symbol V, defined as power per unit of current.
Resistance, R
How strongly something opposes the flow of current. Its unit is the ohm, symbol Ω, defined as voltage per unit of current.

Say “amp” if you like; write A. The unit name is the ampere, and the symbol is a capital A because the unit is named after a person. So is the volt (V) and the watt (W). Lower case changes the meaning of a prefix, not just the look of it: mA is a milliampere and MA is a megaampere, a billion times larger. See [[measurement-language]].

Why voltage and resistance are called derived units

Of the four units above, only the ampere is a base unit — one of the seven that the rest of SI is built from. The others are derived: their definitions are ratios of units already defined. NIST expresses the volt as W/A and the ohm as V/A, and traces both back to base units as m2·kg·s−3·A−1 and m2·kg·s−3·A−2.

That is why unit cancellation works on electrical problems exactly as it does on mechanical ones — the electrical units are made of metres, kilograms, seconds, and amperes underneath. The technique on [[dimensional-analysis]] does not need a separate electrical version.

Two equations, and everything else follows

The whole set is generated by two relationships:

V = IR  —  P = VI

Rearranging and substituting gives three expressions for each of the four quantities. There is nothing to memorise beyond the two above; the table is a lookup, not a list of new facts.

P = VI gives real power in watts on direct current or a purely resistive load. On alternating current, volts times amps is apparent power in volt-amperes, and the real power is that multiplied by the power factor — see [[power-and-efficiency]].

Worked example: what a lamp draws

How much current does a 100 W lamp draw on a 120 V supply?

  1. Pick the form with the two quantities you have: I = P/V.
  2. I = 100 W ÷ 120 V = 0.833 A.

Check the units rather than trusting the arithmetic. A volt is a watt per ampere, so watts divided by volts leaves amperes — which is what was asked for.

Worked example: voltage drop along a cord

A 100 ft extension cord has a resistance of 0.1588 Ω. What is the voltage drop across the cord when 10 A flows through it?

  1. V = IR = 10 A × 0.1588 Ω = 1.588 V.

About 1.6 V. That is 1.6 V that never reaches the tool: the supply is divided between the cord and the load, and the cord takes its share first.

The dropped voltage does not disappear

The power lost in the cord is P = I2R = (10 A)2 × 0.1588 Ω = 15.9 W. That power becomes heat in the conductor, spread along its whole length.

Notice the squared current. Doubling the load to 20 A does not double the loss, it quadruples it, to about 64 W. This is why the drop that is harmless on a light load becomes a real problem on a heavy one, and why the length and gauge of a cord matter more the more current the tool draws. The condition and routing of the cord itself are covered on [[electrical-safety]].

The same method keeps working

These are not the last units you will meet. Each new quantity arrives the same way: it is defined as a combination of quantities you already have, and its unit is that same combination.
