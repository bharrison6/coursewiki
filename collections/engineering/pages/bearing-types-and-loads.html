A bearing allows useful motion and restrains unwanted motion

A loose rigid part can move in six independent ways: translate along three axes and rotate about those three axes. These are its degrees of freedom. A bearing transfers load to a support while allowing the motion the machine needs, with low friction.

For a rotating shaft, the goal is usually one remaining freedom: rotation about its own axis. Two spaced radial supports resist sideways movement and tilting; axial location stops the shaft walking lengthwise. A single short bearing does not automatically provide that whole constraint system.

Bearings guide motion; motors and drives supply it. Belt, chain and gear drives load their supporting shafts. See [[torque]] for turning effort and [[shaft-support-and-adjustment]] for the complete support arrangement.

Radial load crosses the shaft; thrust acts along it

Radial load acts perpendicular to the shaft axis. Axial load, or thrust, acts parallel to it. Combined load has both components. A bearing's orientation and internal geometry determine which it can carry.

Do not turn “roller” into “radial only,” or “thrust” into a rule for every product bearing that name. Tapered rollers carry combined loads; some spherical roller thrust designs also accept radial load under specified conditions.

Plain and rolling bearings trade friction, upkeep and cost

A plain bearing supports sliding surfaces, often with a replaceable sleeve called a bushing. A rolling-element bearing places balls or rollers between rings. “Anti-friction” is a traditional name, not a promise of zero friction.

Rolling contact generally reduces resistance compared with sliding contact. It does not guarantee higher efficiency than every fluid-film bearing, nor does a cheap bushing guarantee lower lifetime cost. Include lubrication equipment, replacement, downtime and power loss. Plain bearings differ in both lubrication and load direction

Dry or self-lubricating
A suitable polymer, composite or lubricant-containing material supplies a low-friction sliding surface. Follow that material's instructions; adding grease is not automatically an improvement.
Hydrodynamic
Relative motion draws lubricant into a converging gap, forming a pressure-supported film. Starts and stops require attention because the running film depends on motion.
Hydrostatic
An external pressure supply supports the load on a fluid film, including at rest while the supply operates. The pump, flow control and loss-of-pressure behavior are part of the system.

Those are lubrication descriptions. Shape answers a different question: a journal surrounds a shaft to carry radial load; a footstep supports axial load at a shaft end; a thrust collar presents an annular face to carry axial load. An assembly can combine a journal and a collar to handle both components.

Rings carry the load; the cage spaces the rolling elements

The inner ring fits around the shaft; the outer ring fits in the housing. The rolling elements transfer load between their raceways. The cage maintains spacing and guides the elements.

In a common arrangement the inner ring turns with the shaft while the outer ring stays still. The balls spin about their own axes and orbit around the shaft. The rings do not have to rotate in opposite directions.

Balls have idealized point contact; rollers have idealized line contact. Under load these become finite contact patches. The longer roller contact generally supports more load in a similar overall size. Contact angle, not just element shape, determines thrust capability. Thrust ball and roller bearings place their contact geometry to support axial force.
