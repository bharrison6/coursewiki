The dial counts screw rotation; the indicator finds the carriage

A handwheel dial does not measure the carriage. It counts how far the screw has turned and multiplies by the lead. That is the carriage's travel only while the screw keeps pushing the nut in one direction.

A screw and its nut need some clearance between their thread flanks (a flank is the sloping side of a thread). After the screw changes direction, it must turn through that clearance before its other flank meets the nut and the carriage moves again. That clearance is backlash, the same idea defined for threads in [[screw-threads]] and for gear teeth in [[gear-drives]]. The turn spent crossing it is lost motion: input that produces no output.

  • Dial reading: travel predicted from screw rotation, turns × lead. See [[lead-screws]].
  • Carriage position: where the carriage really is, as an indicator touching it would show.
  • The difference is zero after a forward approach (the direction the dial was zeroed in) and one full clearance after a reverse approach.
Where the definition comes from

Modern Machine Shop's column Eliminate Backlash From Machining Centers (Mike Lynch, 2000) defines backlash as “any non-movement that occurs during axis reversals.” This page uses the CourseWiki definition on [[screw-threads]]: clearance that must be taken up after reversing before the load moves. In a real drive, elastic deflection can add lost motion as well; the lab below leaves it out.

Experiment: reverse the handwheel and watch the carriage wait

What the lab does: a handwheel turns an axially fixed lead screw. The dial reports travel calculated from screw rotation; an indicator on the carriage reports where the carriage really is. With a total axial clearance B between screw and nut flanks, the two agree only while the screw keeps pushing in the direction the dial was set in.

Dead band after a reversal = clearance ÷ lead, as a fraction of a turn:

angle = 360° × B ÷ L   dial divisions = B ÷ (L ÷ 100) on a dial with 100 divisions per turn.

Worked example: lead L = 5 mm/rev, so one division is 0.05 mm. Clearance B = 0.20 mm. Angle = 360° × 0.20 ÷ 5 = 14.4°; divisions = 0.20 ÷ 0.05 = 4. For four divisions after the reversal the dial changes and the carriage does not.

Landing on 25.00 mm after overshooting to 25.60 mm (lead 5 mm, clearance 0.20 mm). The dial was zeroed after a forward approach, so it equals the carriage position only for forward approaches:

Backing past the target by more than the clearance and finishing in the original direction puts the screw back on the flank the dial was set against.

Model limits: rigid parts and a kinematic dead band only. Friction is assumed to hold the carriage still inside the clearance, and nothing else pushes it along the axis; there is no force, stiffness, preload or stick-slip model. Lead, clearance and the 3D dimensions are illustrative and exaggerated, not a standard thread or a measured machine.

Predict the dead band: clearance divided by lead, as a fraction of a turn

Once the screw reverses, the carriage stays still until the screw's helix has moved one full clearance B. One turn moves the helix one lead L, so the dead band is B ÷ L of a turn.

dead-band angle = 360° × B ÷ L

dead-band divisions = B ÷ (value of one division); with 100 divisions per turn, one division is L ÷ 100.

Same clearance, two leads (illustrative values, 100-division dial).

The carriage error after a reverse approach is the same 0.20 mm in both rows. A finer lead only spreads that same clearance over more of the handwheel turn.

Land on a dimension: finish every approach in the direction the dial was set

If you overshoot a target, turning straight back to the number leaves the carriage one clearance away from it, because the screw is now pushing on the other flank. Instead:

  1. Back the handwheel past the target by more than the backlash.
  2. Turn in the original direction again and stop on the number.

The same reasoning applies when setting a dial. MIT's 2.670 milling notes say to “be certain that the backlash in the mechanism driving the table is taken up prior to setting the dial indicator”. A dial set with the clearance taken up in one direction reads true for approaches in that direction. For edge finding, [[mill-indicating-and-zero]] repeats the touch from the same direction for the same reason.

A readout that measures the slide, and when the clearance still matters

The MIT 2.670 milling machine notes (section “Using the Micrometer Dials”) also point out that a digital readout measures the bed position directly, so its reading is not thrown off by backlash. The clearance is still in the drive. A force that pushes the carriage along the screw can move it across the clearance without the handwheel turning; [[climb-and-conventional-milling]] explains when a cutting force does that.

What the lab model includes and leaves out

  • Included: a rigid screw that turns but never slides along its axis, a nut fixed to the carriage, and a total axial clearance B. The carriage position c stays within B/2 of the screw's helical phase s = −Lφ/360° (right-hand thread, φ the screw rotation); inside that band it does not move.
  • Assumed: friction holds the carriage still inside the clearance, and no outside force pushes it along the axis.
  • Left out: forces, friction values, stiffness and elastic wind-up, preload, stick-slip and wear. Drives designed to remove the clearance, such as preloaded ball nuts or adjustable split nuts, are beyond this model.
  • Illustrative: the leads, clearances and the 3D screw (5 mm lead, 1.0 mm clearance, 30° flanks) are chosen to be seen, not taken from a standard thread or a measured machine.

For drive design in depth, Alexander Slocum's Precision Machine Design (1992) covers actuators and transmissions in Chapter 10, including section 10.8, Linear Power Transmission Elements.

What the model means, and why it is not a prediction for every CNC

The coordinates describe the cutter relative to the workpiece, viewed from above: +X right, +Y up. A moving table travels in the opposite sense. The drive infers position from motor/screw motion, with no compensation or linear-position correction. The first approach seats both axes and calibrates the coordinates at the start, before the plunge. That reference is kept through the cut.

With positive initial seating, each actual coordinate c is updated as c = min(max(c, q), q + B), where q is the motor-inferred coordinate and B the total clearance. Negative seating uses the band [q − B, q]. These are shifted forms of the same play operator as the screw lab above. Each axis holds until a band edge reaches it. If the axes take up clearance at different moments within a command segment, the actual path has an extra bend; the cutter sweep includes it.

Every cut segment removes a disc swept along that actual segment, a capsule with the cutter's 2 mm radius. The area shading compares the actual and zero-backlash histories at the same command step. Span measurements intersect those capsules with the two centre sections. They are not a best-fit diameter, an overall size tolerance or a guarantee of the rest of the contour.

All other effects are idealized away: no runout, deflection, wear, servo lag, cutting-force pull, thermal error or compensation. Friction is assumed to hold a slide inside its clearance. The shapes, clearance settings and viewing speed are illustrative. Restart begins a fresh workpiece; changing a setting recomputes the experiment.

HEIDENHAIN describes direct linear-axis measurement as working “without intervening mechanical elements”. Such feedback changes what the controller can observe; this lab deliberately does not model that correction. Haas's ballbar interpretation guide distinguishes lost motion at reversal from controller-related reversal spikes. Only rigid lost motion is included here.
