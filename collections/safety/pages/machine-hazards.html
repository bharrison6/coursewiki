Rotating machinery

Rotating machinery injures you by catching hold of something and pulling it in. It does not have to catch your hand. It catches a sleeve, a glove, a lanyard or a strand of hair, and your hand goes wherever that goes.

Gears, pulleys and belts, chains and sprockets are extremely dangerous. Where a rotating part meets another surface there is a wedge that closes to nothing — an in-running nip point, the place the machine feeds things into itself. It is where the belt runs onto the pulley, where the chain runs onto the sprocket, where the gear teeth mesh.

The contact and the injury happen in different places. You touch the belt out in the open, where it looks like a moving strap; the belt carries you to the pulley, where there is no gap at all. That distance is why staying clear of the nip point is not enough on its own — anything the machine can grip is a handle it uses to bring you there.

What gets caught

  • Loose clothing, jewelry and unsecured hair — see [[ppe|clothing, jewelry and hair]].
  • Gloves. Gloves or rags are for handling sharp objects such as cutters, and only once the machine is shut down. NEVER wear gloves at a running machine — a glove catches where bare skin would pull free, and takes your hand with it. See [[metal-chips|chips and gloves]].
  • A long stringy chip still connected to the cut — see [[metal-chips]].

It does not take teeth, and it does not take speed. A plain smooth shaft turning slowly will still wind hair or cloth onto itself, and once it has a turn or two it does not let go.

The word for this, and where it comes from

“Nip point” is not shop slang. It is the term the machine-guarding standard uses. 29 CFR 1910.212(a)(1) requires guarding against “hazards such as those created by point of operation, ingoing nip points, rotating parts, flying chips and sparks.”

OSHA’s Machine Guarding eTool puts it plainly: “In-running nip point hazards are caused by the rotating parts on machinery.” Its longer treatment, Concepts and Techniques of Machine Safeguarding (OSHA 3067), gives three kinds:

  • “Parts can rotate in opposite directions while their axes are parallel to each other. These parts may be in contact (producing a nip point) or in close proximity.” — meshing gears.
  • “Nip points are also created between rotating and tangentially moving parts. Some examples would be: the point of contact between a power transmission belt and its pulley, a chain and a sprocket, and a rack and pinion.”
  • “Nip points can occur between rotating and fixed parts which create a shearing, crushing, or abrading action.”

So belts, chains, sprockets and gears are rotating hazards by name. What makes them dangerous is that they draw material in — not that two parts close on each other.

OSHA is just as direct about the plain shaft: “Rotating motion can be dangerous; even smooth, slowly rotating shafts can grip hair and clothing, and through minor contact force the hand and arm into a dangerous position.”

These parts are meant to be behind covers, and the standard is specific about it — 29 CFR 1910.219(f)(3): “All sprocket wheels and chains shall be enclosed unless they are more than seven (7) feet above the floor or platform.”

Moving machinery and pinch points

Moving machinery injures you without ever catching hold of you. Two surfaces close on each other with you in between. Nothing pulls you anywhere — you run out of room.

What makes a pinch point

A pinch point is a gap that is closing: two parts, at least one of them moving, with space between them that is on its way to nothing. That is the whole of it, and it is why pinch points are hard to spot. There are no teeth, no edge and often no noise. A wide gap and a narrow gap look the same until the last inch, and the gap does not care what is in it.

Two kinds of movement, and both make one:

Back and forth, up and down
A press ram, a drill head coming down, a saw guide being lowered, a clamp closing. You are caught between the part that moves and the part that does not.
Straight along
A table, a carriage or a saddle travelling. It closes the gap against whatever waits at the end of its travel — a stop, a column, the machine’s own frame, or the hand resting there.

Where they hide

  • Between the work and whatever holds it. A vise jaw, a clamp, a fixture: the hand steadying the workpiece is inside the gap that is closing on the workpiece.
  • At the end of a table’s travel. Wind it far enough and the space between the table and the frame goes to zero, with whatever was resting there in between.
  • Under anything that comes down. A head, a ram, a guard, a guide.
  • On hinges. Covers, doors and lids close on their own frames — at the free edge, where a hand is holding them, and again right at the hinge, where the gap closes over almost no distance at all.

A pinch point needs no power, no speed and no noise. A vise turned by hand multiplies what you put into the handle many times over, and it closes slowly and quietly enough that nothing warns you. Switching a machine off removes the part that would have closed the gap for you. It does not remove the gap.

The question to ask

Before moving anything — a handwheel, a lever, a clamp, a guard — what closes, and what is in the way? Follow the moving part to the end of its travel and find what it meets there. Then look at where your hands are, and where they will still be once you are watching the cut instead of watching your hands.

Why belts and chains count as rotating, not pinching

OSHA sorts machine hazards by the motion that causes them, and “pinch point” is the vocabulary it attaches to parts that travel, not to parts that turn. From Concepts and Techniques of Machine Safeguarding (OSHA 3067):

  • Reciprocating motion — “Reciprocating motions may be hazardous because, during the back-and-forth or up-and-down motion, a worker may be struck by or caught between a moving and a stationary part.”
  • Transverse motion, which means movement in a straight continuous line — “Transverse motion (movement in a straight, continuous line) creates a hazard because a worker may be struck or caught in a pinch or shear point by the moving part.”

A belt on a pulley is a rotating hazard that draws material in; a table running up against a column is a moving hazard that closes on it. Both can take a hand, and the vocabulary is worth keeping straight because the two do not have the same tell. You avoid the first by giving it nothing to grip. You avoid the second by knowing where the gap is going.

Lockout/Tagout — before anyone works on a machine

Everything on a machine that turns or travels does so because something is powering it. Lockout/Tagout is how that power is taken away and kept away while somebody works on the machine.

NEVER work on equipment with rotating or moving parts without first disconnecting power.

Lockout/Tagout procedures must be followed for maintenance.

The two words

Lockout
A lock on the device that isolates the energy — the disconnect, the breaker, the valve — so it physically cannot be switched back on.
Tagout
A tag on that same device saying who put it there and why, so nobody removes it wondering.

Off is not disconnected, and disconnected is not still

  • Off is not disconnected. A machine’s own on/off switch is a control. Someone can press it back on, and either way the machine is still connected to its supply. The disconnect is the thing that gets locked.
  • Disconnected is not still. A spindle coasts. A raised head or ram drops. A spring, a charged capacitor or air still in a line will all move something after the power is gone. Energy already inside the machine is part of what the procedure has to deal with.
What the standard actually covers

29 CFR 1910.147(a)(1)(i): “This standard covers the servicing and maintenance of machines and equipment in which the unexpected energization or start up of the machines or equipment, or release of stored energy could cause injury to employees.”

Two things are worth reading twice. Unexpected is the whole point — the hazard is not that the machine runs, it is that it runs while someone has a hand inside it. And stored energy is named alongside energization, because a machine that has been switched off and unplugged can still contain everything it needs to move once.

“Servicing and maintenance” is much broader than it sounds. The standard defines it as “constructing, installing, setting up, adjusting, inspecting, modifying, and maintaining and/or servicing machines or equipment”, and adds: “These activities include lubrication, cleaning or unjamming of machines or equipment and making adjustments or tool changes, where the employee may be exposed to the unexpected energization or startup of the equipment or release of hazardous energy.” Setting up, clearing a jam and changing a tool are all on that list.

Stored energy gets its own requirement — 1910.147(d)(5)(i): “Following the application of lockout or tagout devices to energy isolating devices, all potentially hazardous stored or residual energy shall be relieved, disconnected, restrained, and otherwise rendered safe.” The lock comes first, and then the energy already in the machine is dealt with. Both, in that order.

The environment around the machine

Slip hazards
Clean dust, metal chips, water and coolant off the floor immediately.
Lighting
Ensure lighting is adequate before operating any machine.
Walkways
Keep aisles and emergency exits unobstructed.

Operating habits

  • Stop machines completely before taking measurements or making adjustments.
  • Secure all workpieces before operation — use clamps, vises or fixtures.
  • Never leave tools on or near operating machinery.
