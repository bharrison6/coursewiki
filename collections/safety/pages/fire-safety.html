Decide before you reach for anything

An extinguisher is for a small fire you caught early. It is not for a fire that is already growing, and it is never worth your life.

Fight it only if all four are true

  • The fire is small and contained — a bin, a rag, a puddle. Not spreading.
  • You know what is burning, and you have the right extinguisher for it.
  • You have a clear exit behind you and the fire is not between you and it.
  • Someone else has already raised the alarm.

Otherwise: get out. Pull the alarm, leave, call 911 from outside, and tell the instructor and the fire crew what was burning.

If you try and it does not go out in the first few seconds, stop and leave. A hand extinguisher lasts roughly 10–20 seconds. That is your whole attempt.

Using an extinguisher — PASS

Four steps, in order. The one people get wrong is Aim: at the base of the flames, not at the flames themselves. Fire burns the fuel, so the fuel is what you have to smother.

Keep your back to your exit the whole time, and back away — never turn around and walk past a fire you have just knocked down.

Fire classes, and what puts them out

Fires are classed by what is burning. An extinguisher is rated for the classes it can handle, and using the wrong one ranges from useless to dangerous.

    Most extinguishers on a wall are ABC dry chemical — one unit covering the first three classes. Check the label; the classes it is rated for are printed on it.

    Metal fires are different — and we make the fuel

    A Class D fire needs a Class D extinguisher, and nothing else. “Dry powder” and “dry chemical” are not the same thing. An ordinary ABC dry chemical unit on a burning metal is at best useless and can make the fire worse.

    Never put water on a metal fire. It can react violently and throw burning material.

    Solid stock does not catch fire. Fine material does — swarf, chips, grinding dust and polishing lint have enormous surface area for their mass. Aluminium fines, magnesium and titanium all burn, and once alight they burn extremely hot and are very hard to put out.

    What that means day to day

    • Keep chip trays and vacuum contents emptied and separated, not left to accumulate. See [[machine-hazards]] for housekeeping, and the chip handling rules for aluminium.
    • Never grind or cut magnesium or titanium without asking first.
    • Do not mix hot chips or grinding debris into a general waste bin with paper or rag.
    • If fine metal debris does ignite, do not improvise. Alarm, evacuate, and tell the fire crew it is a metal fire — that changes how they fight it.

    Hot work and the things that start fires

    • Grinding throws sparks a surprising distance. Clear rag, sawdust, solvent and packaging out of the area before you start, not after it smoulders.
    • Oily rags can self-heat. Do not leave them in a pile; use the designated container.
    • Flammable liquids live in the fireproof cabinet when they are not in your hand — see [[chemicals-and-sds]].
    • A smouldering fire can restart long after you leave. If something got hot enough to smoke, tell the instructor before you go.

    Know these before you need them

    None of the above helps if you are looking for equipment while a fire grows. On your first day in any lab space, locate:

    • The nearest fire extinguisher, and what classes it is rated for.
    • The nearest alarm pull station.
    • Both ways out of the room, and the route that does not pass the machines.
    • Where your class gathers outside, so you can be accounted for.
    • The first aid kit and the emergency stops — see [[conduct-and-emergency]].
