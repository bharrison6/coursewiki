Five reasons a chip is dangerous

A chip is not scrap until it has cooled and stopped moving. Straight off the tool it is every one of these at once.

Hot
Chips can be upwards of 1000 °F the moment they are cut. They do not look it.
Sharp
The leading edge of a chip is always razor sharp. It is a freshly cut edge and it will open a hand.
Fast
Chips leaving a machine can travel at over 250 mph. That is why eye protection is not negotiable — see [[ppe]].
Strong
A long continuous chip is a steel ribbon. If it wraps a sleeve or a limb it can pull you into the machine.
Slippery
Chips on the floor roll underfoot. This is one of the most common ways people get hurt without the machine touching them.

Never clear chips with your hands

Do not reach into a machine to clear chips while it is running, and do not pick up a long stringy chip by hand at any time. Stop the spindle, then deal with it.

Gloves

  • Gloves may only be used for cleanup after the machine is shut down.
  • Never wear gloves at a running machine. A glove catches where bare skin would pull free, and takes your hand with it — see [[machine-hazards]].

Brush

  • A brush may be used while the machine is in operation, but with extreme care.
  • On a CNC machine, take particular care: the tool can move without warning, and the enclosure invites you to reach further in than you should.

Compressed air — there are limits on this

Compressed air is convenient and it is the option people reach for first. It is also regulated, because a chip in a moving air stream is a projectile and air forced against skin can be driven into the body.

The rule

OSHA permits compressed air for cleaning only where it is reduced to less than 30 psi, and only then with effective chip guarding and personal protective equipment (29 CFR 1910.242(b)). All three conditions, together — not the pressure alone.

In practice

  • Check the regulator is set for cleaning, not shop pressure, before you pull the trigger.
  • Eye protection, every time. Everyone nearby too — you do not get to decide the range.
  • Never at skin, yours or anyone else’s.
  • Never toward another person, and check who is behind the machine first.
  • Never into an enclosed space.
  • If air would just relocate the mess into the air or across the shop, use a brush or the vacuum instead.

Cleaning up

  • Use cleaning equipment or gloves to handle chips for disposal — the sharp edge does not dull because the machine stopped.
  • Clear the machine so only the residue in the bottom tray remains.
  • Sweep the floor, and mop if the floor is wet with coolant.
  • If cleaning equipment such as a vacuum gets wet, open it up and let it dry before it is put away.

Chips and fines do not go in the same place

  • Chips and swarf go in the recycling container. That container is for chips only.
  • Fines go in the trash. Dust from sanding, grinding, buffing and polishing, and what comes out of a vacuum or dust collector — never the recycling container.

Why: chips remelt back into usable metal. Fines largely do not — a fine particle has so much surface area for its mass that remelting oxidizes much of it to dross instead of recovering aluminum, and melt loss climbs steeply as the scrap gets finer. Fines also arrive contaminated with abrasive grit and with whatever else a shared belt or wheel has touched. So they do not merely add nothing to the bin, they degrade a clean chip stream. Keeping them out is what keeps the chip container worth recycling.

Fine dust burns; chips do not. Aluminum chips and swarf are hard to ignite. The fire hazard is fines — and then only when a cloud of them is thrown into the air and meets an ignition source, or when they are mixed with ferrous dust or sparks. Do not let fine dust accumulate, and do not raise a cloud of it cleaning up.

If fine metal debris does ignite, do not fight it. The ABC extinguisher on the wall is the wrong tool and will scatter it. Alarm, evacuate, and tell the fire crew it is a metal fire. See [[fire-safety]].
