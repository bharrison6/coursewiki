Feed direction decides whether each chip starts thick or thin

A milling cutter takes metal off in small slices, one slice per tooth. Whether each slice starts thick and ends thin, or starts thin and ends thick, comes down to one thing: which way the work is fed past the turning cutter.

Climb milling
Also called down milling. Where a tooth is cutting, it moves the same way the work is fed. Each chip starts thick and ends thin.
Conventional milling
Also called up milling. Where a tooth is cutting, it moves against the feed. Each chip starts thin and ends thick.
Feed per tooth
How far the work moves while the cutter turns from one tooth to the next. It sets how thick a chip can get.
Width of cut
How far the cutter reaches into the side of the work, measured across the cutter. Machinists also call it the radial depth of cut.

How to read the drawings on this page. You are looking along the cutter’s axis, end on. The cutter stays where it is and turns clockwise; the work is fed past it, the way a mill table carries the work. Every Work feed arrow is the work’s motion. On a machine that moves the cutter instead, nothing changes: the cutter simply travels the opposite way to that arrow.

The cutter is the same in both modes and spins the same way. What switches climb to conventional is the direction of feed relative to the side of the cutter that is doing the cutting.

Watch one tooth take its chip

The gold shape is the slice of metal the cutting tooth has taken so far. Play it, or drag the tooth through the cut, and watch where the chip is thick.

Five things to try in the demo

  • Choose Compare both and step until each tooth has only just entered the work. Which chip is already thick, and which is barely there?
  • Drag Tooth position in the cut from 0% to 100%, once in each mode. Watch the chip readout: in climb it runs down from the peak, in conventional it runs up to it.
  • Set the width of cut to 50%. The tooth now cuts through a quarter of a turn and the chip reaches the full feed per tooth. Drop it to 10%: the peak falls to 60% of the feed. At the same feed, a narrower cut makes a thinner chip.
  • Raise the feed per tooth. The chips get thicker but keep their shape: the direction of feed sets the shape, the feed sets the size.
  • Watch the red-orange arrow, the pull of the cut on the work. In climb it pulls the work the way it is already going and presses it down. In conventional it pushes back against the feed and lifts. Keep that in mind for the card on backlash.

Starting thin means rubbing; starting thick means a bite

A real cutting edge has a rounded tip. When the uncut layer is extremely thin, the edge can push and rub the metal before a chip forms. The peripheral cuts shown here make that difference visible.

  • Conventional milling starts every chip at zero, so every tooth rubs before it bites. Rubbing makes heat and wears the edge. Look at where the tooth enters, too: on the finished surface, the one you are keeping.
  • Climb milling starts the chips shown here at their thickest, so the tooth bites at once, with a knock each time a tooth enters.

That is the main reason climb milling is often the preferred direction when the machine can hold the work steady against it. Whether it can is the next card.

Which way the cut pulls on the work

  • Climb: the teeth drag the work along the way it is already being fed, and tend to push it down onto the table in the peripheral arrangement shown.
  • Conventional: the teeth push back against the feed and tend to lift the work in this arrangement, so whatever holds the work has to resist that lift.

Backlash: why climb milling depends on the machine

A feed screw moves the table by turning inside a nut. Unless the drive is built to take it out, there is some clearance between the screw’s thread and the nut. That clearance is called backlash, and it lets the table shift a little while the screw stands still.

  • Conventional: the cut pushes the table back, against the feed. That holds the nut against the flank of the thread the screw is driving with, so the gap stays on the far side and the table moves only as fast as the screw turns.
  • Climb: the cut pulls the table forward, the way it is already moving. If there is clearance, the table can slide across it ahead of the screw, and the cutter takes a bigger bite than the feed was set for, all at once. That sudden, oversized bite is hard on the cutter and on whatever is holding the work.

This is a question to ask about a machine, not a rule. How much clearance a feed drive has, and whether it is built to take it out, differs from machine to machine. This page does not tell you which direction to use on any machine you will meet. Ask the person responsible for that machine.

Why some machines climb mill without trouble

A feed drive can be designed to reduce backlash, for example with a preloaded ball nut. The important question is whether the machine controls that movement, not simply whether it is called CNC. The chip is the same shape either way; what changes is whether the machine can hold the table against the pull of the cut.

A slot is both at once

When the cutter is buried across its whole width, cutting a slot, each tooth enters at one wall and leaves at the other. Its chip starts thin, is thickest halfway across, and ends thin. The first half of the sweep moves against the feed, like conventional milling, and the second half moves with it, like climb milling, whichever way the work is fed. That is why the demo stops at half the cutter’s diameter: past that point the two can no longer be pulled apart.
