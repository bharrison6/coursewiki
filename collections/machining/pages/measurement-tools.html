Choose from the tolerance and the shape of the feature

Start with what must be measured: an outside size, an opening, a depth, a height, or a small change from a reference.

As a rule of thumb, choose a tool that reads about ten times finer than the total tolerance. This is a starting point, not a standard or a guarantee that the measurement is good enough. Check its specified accuracy, condition, measuring range, and the way its contacts reach the part.

For a size of 0.625 ± 0.001 in, the total tolerance is 0.002 in. A starting resolution target is 0.002 / 10 = 0.0002 in. An outside micrometer reading 0.0001 in is a candidate for an accessible outside diameter. A 0.001 in caliper is too coarse for that rule of thumb.

See [[tolerances-and-fits]] for the allowed size range, [[reading-measuring-scales]] for the numbers, and [[reliable-shop-measurement]] for the technique.

Resolution is the increment; accuracy is agreement with a reference

Resolution
The smallest indicated change or scale increment. A display that changes by 0.0005 in has that resolution; its last digit is not an accuracy certificate.
Accuracy
How closely a measurement agrees with the reference value. Use the instrument's error specification and checks against suitable standards to judge its capability.
Precision
How closely repeated measurements agree under stated conditions. A dirty measuring face can give repeatable readings that are all wrong.

Extra digits cannot remove jaw wear, a tilted contact, dirt, or heat. Record the instrument and units along with the result when the measurement matters.

Use a steel rule for layout and quick size checks

A steel rule is useful for stock lengths, approximate sizes, and setting a divider. Read the graduation pattern on the edge you are using: fractional edges may divide an inch into 8, 16, 32, or 64 parts; decimal edges may use 0.1, 0.05, 0.02, or 0.01 in increments. A decimal rule does not ordinarily have 0.001 in graduations.

Place the rule along the distance being measured and look straight at the marks. A damaged end is a poor zero. If you start at the 1 in line instead, subtract 1 in from the far-end reading. Do not use a rule to accept a feature with a tight size tolerance.

Calipers cover outside, inside, depth, and step measurements

Dial, digital, and vernier calipers use a sliding jaw. The larger jaws measure outside sizes; the smaller jaws measure openings. A depth rod reaches down from the end of the beam. On tools with step faces, the back shoulders measure a step.

Use light contact and keep the jaws square. Measure near the beam where access allows. Long jaws can flex; small inside jaws may not reach the true diameter of a small hole. A caliper's versatility does not make it the best tool for every tolerance.

Micrometers specialize in outside sizes, inside sizes, or depths

An outside micrometer measures between a fixed anvil and a moving spindle. Choose a range that contains the dimension: a 1–2 in instrument does not start from zero like a 0–1 in instrument. A conventional inch scale reads 0.001 in; a sleeve vernier can add 0.0001 in readings. A conventional metric scale reads 0.01 mm. Digital models differ.

An inside micrometer spans an opening using internal contacts. Rod and two-point types require careful alignment; three-point types have a different contact arrangement. Check the reference and any extension length for the actual tool.

A depth micrometer rests its flat base on the reference face and extends a rod to the lower surface. Keep the base seated. Check the installed rod's range and the scale direction: many depth scales run opposite to an outside micrometer. Do not add a rod length without checking how that instrument is marked.

Indicators show change from a reference

A dial indicator commonly has a plunger moving along its axis. A test indicator has a pivoting lever stylus and usually a shorter measuring range. Both are useful for alignment, comparing heights, and observing runout as a part turns.

Read the marked graduation and count revolutions where needed. Zeroing the bezel sets a reference; it does not turn the indicator into an absolute diameter tool. Total indicator reading, or TIR, is the maximum indication minus the minimum during a sweep. From −0.001 to +0.002 in, TIR is 0.003 in.

Use a rigid stand. A magnetic base needs a suitable magnetic support; it will not attach magnetically to granite. For plunger indicators, align motion with the plunger. For lever test indicators, follow the maker's stylus-angle instructions; the lever is normally close to parallel with the surface. These are different geometries.

What a sweep can and cannot establish

A sweep combines the part's shape with its orientation and support. Runout is not a direct measurement of formal concentricity. A height sweep relative to a plate can reveal variation, but calling it a flatness inspection requires the proper setup and evaluation. Follow the drawing's specified inspection method.

Gauge blocks supply a reference; transfer gauges carry a size

Gauge blocks have accurately finished opposing faces and a known length. Use suitable blocks to set a height, compare a measurement, or check an instrument. Clean and inspect the faces before wringing blocks together; follow their care instructions. A zero check alone is not a full calibration.

Build stacks from sizes actually present in the set. For example, 1.0000 + 0.1340 + 0.1005 = 1.2345 in uses three blocks if those sizes are available. Do not assume a set contains separate 0.0040 or 0.0005 in blocks.

A telescoping gauge transfers a bore size to an outside micrometer. With light locking friction, set the contacts slightly across the bore at an angle and sweep gently through the diameter; withdraw without changing the setting, then measure across the contacts. Follow the tool's instructions for locking and repeat until the technique is consistent.

A small-hole gauge expands rounded contacts inside a smaller opening. Adjust for light drag, withdraw as its design permits, and measure across the contacts with a micrometer. Neither transfer tool has its own numerical readout. Take readings at several depths and directions; keep them separate to reveal taper or out-of-roundness rather than averaging the shape away.
