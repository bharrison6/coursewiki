Look for oil, odor, solids and foam—not just a low level

The sump is the reservoir that collects coolant for reuse. It also collects material that does not belong in the cutting fluid.

  • Tramp oil: unwanted lubricant leaking into the coolant, such as hydraulic oil or oil from sliding machine surfaces. Skim the floating layer and investigate its source; it can interfere with fluid condition and concentration readings.
  • Bacteria and odor: water-mixed fluids can support microbial growth. New or persistent bad odor is a reason to investigate, not a test that identifies the organism. Lack of odor does not prove the fluid is healthy.
  • Concentration drift: evaporation tends to make the tank richer; repeated water additions can make it lean. Measure using [[coolant-concentration|Brix and the product factor]].
  • Chips and fines: chips settle and fine particles collect as sludge. They occupy tank space and can obstruct screens, filters and flow paths.
  • Foam: check concentration, water quality, contamination and air entering the delivery system. Foam is not a reliable concentration measurement.
  • Skin irritation: an overly strong mix can irritate skin; an overly weak mix can lose protection against microbial growth. Irritation is a reason to report exposure and investigate, not to guess a dilution.
Why inspection and measurement belong together

Concentration alone does not describe contamination or biological condition. HSE's metalworking-fluid guidance calls for maintaining fluid quality and checking bacterial contamination. A supplier-directed fluid-management program may include pH and microbial checks as well as concentration; use its methods and action limits. Master Fluid Solutions' FAQ explains odor, residue and concentration-related skin irritation.

Top up a serviceable tank; replace fluid that cannot be restored

Use a top-up when the existing coolant is fit for continued use and the problem is level or a correctable concentration. Choose the addition from the measured condition, not from the fresh-fill recipe. A rich tank may need a weaker makeup mix; a lean tank may need a stronger one.

Arrange a cleanout and replacement when contamination, unstable mixing or persistent odor cannot be brought under control by the approved maintenance process, or when the machine/fluid maintenance instructions require it. Investigate leaks and other causes so the fresh charge does not immediately inherit the problem.

Straining removes larger solids; it does not remove dissolved contamination or establish that drained fluid is reusable. Do not return rejected fluid just because it looks cleaner. Do not add household disinfectant or an unapproved chemical to mask odor.

Use the machine and fluid suppliers' maintenance intervals and condition checks. There is no universal calendar changeout interval or guaranteed sump life.

Clean the tank and its return path before refilling

Use this as a general sequence with the machine's approved maintenance procedure. Stop the machine and follow its isolation requirements before accessing the tank, pumps or chip-handling equipment. Only run pumps for a cleaning or circulation step when that procedure calls for it and the system is safely reassembled.

  • Identify the fluid, approved cleaner and disposal route. Review [[chemicals-and-sds]], [[ppe]] and [[metal-chips]] before starting.
  • Stop and isolate the equipment as required by its maintenance procedure. Prepare suitable labeled receiving containers before opening or draining the system.
  • Skim accessible floating tramp oil. Drain or transfer coolant through a suitable strainer to catch larger chips, keeping the destination consistent with the reuse or waste decision.
  • Remove accumulated sludge and chips with the approved tools. Clean accessible screens, filters and return passages; inspect for blocked flow and leaks.
  • Clean dried coolant residue with a compatible cleaner diluted to its labeled instructions. Use the specified contact time and cleaning method; do not invent a stronger mixture.
  • Remove the spent cleaning solution. Rinse and drain as the machine, cleaner and fluid suppliers specify; capture those liquids for the approved waste route. Account for retained liquid before refilling.
  • Reinstall components and guards. Prepare fresh coolant by adding concentrate to water at the product's specified concentration, then fill to the machine's operating level.
  • Restore and circulate the system according to its procedure. Check flow and leaks, measure concentration after mixing, and record the fluid condition, result and service performed.

For a public demonstration, watch Haas's Machine Tool Coolant: Cleaning Your Tank. Its examples distinguish a routine tank cleanout from cleaning an extensively contaminated coolant system. Haas's Machine Tool Coolant: Tools of Coolant is a companion resource.

Supplier instructions determine the cleaning treatment

HSE's sump-cleaning guidance emphasizes supplier-selected cleaner and circulation times. An extensively contaminated system may need treatment before draining and additional cleaning cycles; the general checklist is not a microbial decontamination recipe.

Use the safety pages for contact, PPE and waste handling

Minimize skin contact with coolant and cleaning liquid. Use [[chemicals-and-sds|the SDS]] for the actual products and [[ppe|the PPE guidance]] for exposure control. Use [[metal-chips]] for removing and handling solids; this maintenance page does not replace those rules.

Manage spent coolant, removed oil, sludge and cleaning liquid through the approved regulated-waste process. Do not pour them down a drain or onto the ground. The responsible waste handler must determine the applicable classification and disposal route from the fluid, contaminants and local requirements. “Water-based” or “biodegradable” is not disposal permission.

Why the waste route depends on what is in the tank

Washington State Department of Ecology's metalworking-fluid guide, pages 12–13, illustrates how formulation and contamination change waste management. Its state-specific rules are not a disposal authorization elsewhere. EPA's used-oil guidance also distinguishes qualifying used oil from other wastes and notes that state requirements can be stricter. The product SDS is a starting point; used coolant also contains material picked up during machining.
