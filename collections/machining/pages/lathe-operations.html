Identify the parts that rotate, guide and feed

Headstock and spindle
The headstock supports the rotating spindle and its drive. The spindle carries the chuck, faceplate or other workholding. Its axis is the reference for turning.
Bed and ways
The bed supports the assemblies. Its guide surfaces, called ways, guide carriage travel and locate the tailstock.
Tailstock
Supports the far end of work with a center, or holds axial tools such as drills and machine reamers. Its quill extends along the spindle axis when aligned.
Carriage, cross slide and compound rest
The carriage moves along the bed. The cross slide moves across the axis. The compound sits above it and swivels to guide short angled movements. The tool post holds the cutting tool on the compound.
Apron
The assembly on the front of the carriage contains feed mechanisms and controls.
Feed rod and leadscrew
On machines with separate drives, the feed rod powers ordinary turning or facing feeds; the leadscrew synchronizes threading. Some lathes combine functions, so identify the actual drive arrangement before selecting a feed.
Half-nuts and quick-change gearbox
The half-nuts close around the leadscrew to drive the carriage during threading. The gearbox selects ratios for feeds or thread pitches; its chart tells you which control combinations produce each ratio.

Choose workholding for the shape and the surface that must run true

Running true means the reference surface is centered on the spindle axis, with little measured runout. Gripping a part does not automatically establish that alignment.

Three-jaw scroll chuck
Moves its jaws together for quick gripping of round or suitable hexagonal stock. Self-centering is convenient, but does not promise zero runout, especially after removing and replacing a part.
Four-jaw independent chuck
Moves each jaw separately. Useful for square or irregular work, intentional offsets, or indicating an existing surface true. Adjustment takes more time.
Collet
Grips around a matching stock shape over a limited size range. Useful for repeated work and small diameters; choose the correct collet and its permitted clamping range.
Centers and drive dog
Centers locate work through prepared center holes. A dog clamped to the work transmits torque from a drive plate; the centers provide support. Useful when several surfaces must share the center-hole axis or the work must be removed and returned.
Faceplate
Provides slots or mounting features for clamping work that does not suit chuck jaws. The fixture must locate the intended axis and account for the off-center mass.
Steady rest
Mounts on the bed and supports a long workpiece at a fixed position. It can support work when the end must remain accessible for drilling or boring.
Follow rest
Mounts to the carriage and travels with the tool. It supports slender work near the cut to reduce bending under cutting force. Its support contacts must suit the diameter being supported.

For why a chuck key must never remain in the chuck, and for rotating-work precautions, see [[machine-hazards]]. See [[ppe]] and [[metal-chips]] for protection and chip handling.

Match tool travel to the feature, and set the edge on center

Facing feeds across an end to establish a plane perpendicular to the axis. Straight turning feeds parallel to the axis to make a cylinder. Shoulder turning joins a cylindrical diameter to a step face; the tool needs clearance to reach both surfaces without its holder or unused edge contacting the work.

Center drilling prepares the seat for a supporting center. It is not automatically the best starting method for every drill. Tailstock drilling makes an axial hole; reaming removes a controlled small allowance to improve hole size and finish. Select that allowance from the reamer maker. A reamer generally follows the existing hole, so it is not a reliable way to correct a misplaced hole. Boring uses a single-point tool to enlarge an existing hole and establish its surface relative to the spindle axis.

Grooving makes a recess; parting continues a similar radial cut until the part separates. Chamfering makes a bevel at an edge. Form knurling presses a pattern into the surface and increases the diameter over the raised points; cut knurling is a different process.

Use spindle center height as the starting reference for ordinary turning and threading tools, then follow any tool-specific instruction. An off-center edge changes the effective rake and clearance. In facing, a tool that cannot reach the axis leaves a small central nub. Boring also needs clearance between the tool and the curved hole wall.

Rake and chipbreakers depend on the whole cutting setup

Rake describes the slope of the surface the chip flows over. Clearance keeps the tool below the edge from rubbing. Material matters, but hardness alone does not dictate positive, neutral or negative rake: tool material, edge strength, holder geometry and rigidity also matter. Use the tool maker's cutting geometry and range for the application.

A chipbreaker guides a chip into a tighter curl so it can break. It may be molded into an insert or ground into a tool. It works within a range of feeds and depths; a very thin chip can curl without breaking. A chipbreaker is not a guarantee of short chips at every setting.

A lathe spins the work and moves the tool on two slides

In ordinary turning the work rotates while the tool feeds along it or toward its center. This simulator models parts that are round about the spindle’s axis. The shape comes from where the tool is put against the turning work, and in what order.

Chuck
Grips the bar and turns with the spindle. The jaws hold one end; the tool works on the part of the bar that sticks out.
Carriage (Z)
Carries the tool along the bed, parallel to the axis. On this page Z minus is toward the chuck and Z plus toward the tailstock.
Cross slide (X)
Moves the tool straight toward or away from the axis. The simulator’s X readout is the diameter the tool would cut. On a real lathe, find out whether a dial or readout shows diameter or radius before you trust it.
Tailstock
At the far end of the bed, on the centerline. It holds the center drill, the drills and the countersink, which feed straight along the axis.
Z0
Where the drawing’s finished front face belongs. Every length on the drawings is measured from the front face.

How to read the simulator’s picture. It is the lathe seen from the front, with the whole work in one of two views; the switch under the picture changes view at any time without changing the work. Section cuts the work in half along its centerline, the way a section drawing shows a part: metal is hatched, a hole shows open, and a thread or knurl shows as its profile. Normal shows the outside, as you would see the part on the machine, with its thread and knurl; a hole is drawn as a dashed hidden line, and a tool working inside the part is hidden by it. Tools on the cross slide come in from above, in the plane the section is cut on, so a tool and the metal it cuts are in the same plane.

The dashed blue outline is the drawing, placed at Z0. Tinted metal is still to come off. A heavy red-orange dashed line marks work that is already smaller than the drawing allows. On each tool, the gold edge is the part that cuts and the small ring is the point the readout follows.

Before using a real lathe, read [[machine-hazards]]. The chuck key stays in your hand until it is out of the chuck.

Lathe simulator: make the part on the drawing

Each target is shown in a complete Normal outside view and a complete Section through its centerline. Dashed lines in Normal identify hidden internal features. Section thread bands are schematic crest/root envelopes; the knurl remains a surface callout. Dimensions are nominal inches; the simulator’s feature table gives the limits.

Pick a part and read its drawing (Drawing opens it). Then make it from bar: choose a tool, start the spindle, and move the tool against the work. Measure the part against the drawing whenever you stop.

  • Move the tool by dragging it in the picture, one slide at a time as if you were turning one handwheel; with the handwheel buttons, which repeat while held; with the arrow keys once the picture is selected; or by typing a position and pressing Feed to Z or Feed to X. Stop feed, or Escape, stops a feed part-way.
  • The spindle has to be running for anything to cut, and stopped before you change a tool, change the drill size or thread pitch, or measure the part. A caliper or micrometer never goes on turning work.
  • Each tool does only its own kind of work. When a move would ask a tool for something it cannot do, the simulator stops the tool there and says why. What the tool in use can and cannot do is listed under the tool rack. Turn / face offers two actual tools in its Cutting tool selector; stop the spindle before changing between them.
  • Undo takes back one cut at a time. New bar starts the part again, and undo brings the old bar back.
  • Measure compares every feature with the drawing. The list under the simulator says what each feature still needs, or whether it has already gone too far to save.

Each simulator tool has a defined job; its form knurl displaces metal

Facing, turning, drilling, threading and knurling are not the same action with a different shape of tool. Each tool reaches different places and moves in different ways.

Turning tool
The roughing tool supplied here has a 10° lead and a 0.008 in nose radius. Its angled edge reaches ahead of its readout point. Stopping an axial pass leaves an angled shoulder; leave allowance instead of running the nose to the finished shoulder plane.
Facing tool
A square, 1/16 in wide finishing blade set for radial feed. Put its left corner at the finished Z and feed X across the end or shoulder. Its actual sweep removes the allowance and leaves the face flat. Retract clear before moving Z. These are the simulator’s selected tools; other turning tools and settings can both turn and face.
Chamfer tool
A 90° point. Plunged at an edge, each side cuts a 45° chamfer.
Grooving and parting blade
Cuts only on its front edge. Plunged part of the way it makes a groove; plunged to the center, or into the hole, it parts the work off.
Center drill, drill, countersink
Fed from the tailstock along the axis. A center drill makes a small pilot and a 60° seat for a lathe center; a spotting drill has different starting geometry. The twist drill makes the hole and the countersink chamfers its mouth. Choose a real drill’s starting method and spot angle from its maker’s guidance.
Boring bar
Turns the inside of a hole that is already there, the way the turning tool works on the outside. Its bar has to fit in the hole.
Threading tools
Form tools with a 60° point, one for outside threads and one on a bar for inside threads. For a threading pass the carriage is driven by the leadscrew, so the tool moves one thread lead for every turn of the work; for the single-start threads here, lead equals pitch.
Knurling tool
A straddle head with two opposed forming wheels, with left-hand and right-hand diagonal teeth. Their axles lie parallel to Z; together they roll a diamond pattern. Gap Ø is a simulated gap/contact-diameter control. A real tool’s arm adjustment and locking are separate from cross-slide infeed. The picture shows the barrels and axles in side projection; the highlighted rims are the forming contact.

What makes the simulator stop a move. Each of these is something the real tool cannot do, or should not be asked to:

  • The spindle is stopped, so the tool would only rub.
  • More of the cutting edge would be in the work than the tool takes in one pass. Take it in more passes.
  • A part of the tool that does not cut would touch the work: the holder, the top of an insert, the bar of a boring tool, the side of a blade.
  • The tool would hit the chuck jaws.
  • A threading tool is still in the thread when the carriage moves. Back it out first.
  • A knurl is pressed past full depth, or into a shoulder.
  • A drill is three of its own diameters into a cut without being backed out to clear its chips.

The simulator’s limits are teaching values chosen for these four parts. A real tool’s limits come from its maker and depend on the machine, the material and the setup.

Tool shape and setting matter: Sherline’s tool-grinding guide describes cutting edges, clearance and form tools. The particular blade widths, nose radius and operating limits used here are declared teaching choices, not specifications for every lathe tool.

Most parts start with a facing cut and end with parting

More than one order can make the same part, and the simulator accepts any order that gets there. Some orders are still better than others, for reasons you can see in the work.

  • Face first. The faced end is the datum: every length on the drawing is measured from it. A sawn end is neither flat nor square to the axis.
  • Rough, then finish. A large step comes off in several passes, and only the last one goes to the size on the drawing. On the stepped pin, stop the roughing passes 0.040 in short of each shoulder, retract clear and change to the facing tool. With its left corner at the final shoulder Z, feed X to the smaller diameter. A deeper shoulder needs more clearance for the angled edge; the facing blade can remove its allowance in slices up to 0.0625 in wide.
  • Drill, then bore to size. The boring bar needs a hole its bar fits in. A center drill is available here to explore its pilot and center-seat shape, but it is not a universal prerequisite for drilling. On a real machine, use the starting method recommended for the following drill.
  • Heavy work before thin sections. Knurling pushes hard on the work. Knurling before the thin sections next to it are turned keeps the work stiff while it happens.
  • Make room for the threading tool. A thread needs somewhere for the tool to stop: a relief groove, or a stop short of the shoulder. Chamfer the end first so the thread starts on a clean edge.
  • Chamfer the back edge before parting. Once the part is cut off, its back edge is out of reach without a second setup.
  • Part off last. The part falls free, and anything not done to it by then needs another setup.

The small Chuck end circle is a separate axial view, looking from the tailstock toward the chuck. Its forward-rotation arrow and animation run counterclockwise in that view. The main picture is longitudinal, not a view along the spindle. See the viewpoint diagram in Haas’s spindle-direction training, page 16.

A thread is cut in several passes along one helix

For a threading pass the carriage is coupled to the leadscrew, so the tool advances exactly one lead for every turn of the work. For the single-start threads here, that is one pitch: 1/16 in for 16 threads per inch. Every pass follows the same helix, so every pass deepens the same groove.

The section uses a schematic thread envelope. Its unhatched bands lie between the surviving crests and dashed roots; they are not solid annular thread teeth. A real single-start helix intersects opposite sides half a pitch apart. The model samples a thread profile for depth checks and preserves its material envelope for later cuts and parting. The carriage still stops at the requested Z, even between full turns. Sandvik explains the lead/pitch relation for single- and multi-start threads.

  • A little deeper each pass. The simulator allows 0.015 in of infeed per pass outside and 0.012 in inside. The thread is done when its roots reach the root diameter on the drawing.
  • Back out before you come back. At the end of a pass the tool is still in the groove. Bringing the carriage back without backing the tool out would cut the crests off.
  • Keep the pitch. A pass at another pitch cuts a second helix across the first: a crossed thread.
  • Leave somewhere to stop. The tool runs into a shoulder unless a relief groove gives it room, which is why the thumb screw has one.
How a manual lathe keeps every pass on one helix

The carriage is coupled to the leadscrew by closing the half-nut. On a lathe with an inch leadscrew cutting an inch thread, the thread dial shows when to close it so that each pass drops into the same groove. Many machinists also feed the tool in with the compound travel set near 30° from the radial cross-feed direction, so that most of the cut is taken by one flank of the tool.

The simulator simplifies all of this. It keeps every pass on one helix for you, feeds the tool straight in, and checks a simplified 60° thread by its crest and root diameters. A real thread is checked against a thread table with thread gauges or measuring wires.

A form knurl moves metal instead of cutting it

The knurling tool makes no chips. Its wheels press a pattern into the turning work, and the metal they push aside has to go somewhere: it rises between their teeth. The diameter over the points ends up larger than the blank was.

Blank
The diameter turned before knurling. On these drawings it is not given: the knurled size is, and you work back to the blank.
Full points
The wheels pressed deep enough that the diamond points are fully formed. Too shallow leaves flat tops; pressing past full depth crushes and flakes the points.
Over the points
The knurled diameter, measured across the crests.

In the simulator the roots sink exactly as far as the crests rise, 0.010 in each at full depth, so a full knurl adds 0.020 in to the diameter. A real knurl’s growth depends on its pitch, the material and how hard it is pressed.

This paired-wheel forming arrangement supports feeding along Z after engagement. Dorian’s straddle setup instructions (H-19) and paired diagonal-wheel example (H-48) describe that combination. The drawing is a generic teaching head, not a dimensioned catalog tool or a setup instruction for a particular machine.

Things to try in the simulator

  • On the stepped pin, try to turn the body from 1.000 to 0.750 in in one pass. Read why the tool stops, then do it in two.
  • Stop a roughing pass short of a shoulder and Measure. Choose Face without cutting and measure again. Then make the radial facing cut: which geometry actually changed?
  • Face the end but stop the cut at X 0.200. Measure: what is left at the center?
  • Face 0.010 in past Z0 and make the rest of the pin to the readout. Measure the lengths. Which ones moved, and why?
  • On the bushing, bring the boring bar to the end before there is a hole. Then drill a 1/4 in hole and try again. Which drill gives the bar room?
  • Drill the bushing without the center drill and read the note under the inspection.
  • On the thumb screw, try a threading pass up to the head before cutting the relief groove.
  • Take one threading pass at 16 per inch, change to 13 and take another. Measure the thread.
  • Knurl the head straight on the 1.000 in bar, without turning a blank first. Measure it against the drawing.
  • On the knurled nut, part the ring off and watch where the blade breaks through.

What this simulator leaves out

The simulator is about which tool does what, in what order, to what size. It leaves out:

  • Speed and feed. The spindle is on or off. Spindle speed, feed rate and what they do to the cut are on [[feeds-and-speeds]].
  • The cut itself. No tool wear, heat, chatter, deflection or surface finish. A drill cannot wander, so a hole started without a center drill is only noted, not drawn off center.
  • Setups. One setup only: no turning the part around, no second operation, no tailstock center supporting long work.
  • Threads and knurls, simplified. Thread pitch, crests and roots are checked along the required length; pitch diameter and functional thread fit are not verified. The form knurl illustrates displaced material and diameter growth. Its patterned section is schematic, not an exact volume-conservation calculation. Form and cut knurling use different mechanisms.
  • Its numbers. Inch only. The tolerances, the tool limits and the parts are teaching values, not tool-maker data and not a standard.

Tolerances on the drawings mean the same as on [[tolerances-and-fits]]: a size on a limit passes.

The starting method for a real hole depends on the drill. For example, Haas distinguishes center drilling from spotting for carbide twist drills; this model does not predict drill wandering.
