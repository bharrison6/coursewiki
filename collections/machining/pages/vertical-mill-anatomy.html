The cutter rotates; feed changes its position relative to the work

A vertical mill has a spindle whose axis is vertical in its normal setup. The spindle rotates the tool. The head carries the spindle and its drive; the column supports and guides the machine assemblies. The table carries the workholding.

Do not picture the workpiece as fixed in space. Depending on the machine, the table, head, or both move to feed the rotating cutter through the work. On a typical three-axis vertical mill, X is left–right, Y is front–back, and Z is along the vertical spindle axis.

A CNC position describes the tool relative to the work. A table moving left can therefore produce tool motion to the right relative to the part. Confirm the machine's jog directions and readout before locating a feature. Continue with [[milling-operations]] for what those movements cut.

Identify the actual moving assemblies. “Bed mill” does not mean that every machine assigns Y travel to the head. CNC describes how motion is controlled, not whether the machine has a knee.

The [[mill-setup-checklist|setup checklist]] follows a Tormach workflow. Use the manual for the installed machine to identify controls and travel; a familiar brand name does not establish its spindle interface, options or limits.

Head tram and vise squareness check two different alignments

Tram is the spindle axis's perpendicular alignment to the table in both left–right and front–back directions. An indicator carried around the spindle axis compares points on the table at the same sweep radius. This is an alignment check with spindle power off; head adjustment belongs to the machine-specific procedure.

Squaring the vise makes its fixed jaw parallel to an axis of table travel. It uses a straight sweep along the jaw, not a circular sweep over the table. A square vise does not prove the head is in tram, and a trammed head does not prove the vise is square. See [[milling-workholding]].

Why facing can reveal a tram problem

A tilted spindle puts opposite sides of a face cutter at different heights. Adjacent passes may leave steps even with the vise correctly aligned. Surface finish alone does not identify the cause: check the geometry before changing cutting data.
