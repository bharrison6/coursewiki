Inspect before a setup error becomes a batch of parts

Quality control uses evidence to keep production meeting requirements. Inspection checks what was made; it does not repair a process or make a bad part good.

First-article inspection
Check the first part from a new or changed setup against the required features before releasing continued production. A good first part supports the setup decision; it does not prove that later parts will stay good.
In-process checks
Check selected features while production continues. Record results in production order so tool wear or a shifting setup is visible before many parts are affected.
Final inspection
Check the completed part or lot against the acceptance plan, including requirements that earlier operations could not verify. Keep its identity connected to its results.

If a check fails, identify and hold the affected material, report the result, and investigate before continuing under the applicable procedure. Consider parts made since the last known good check; checking only the next part leaves that interval unresolved.

A gauge gives a decision; a measurement gives a value

Attribute gauging reports a category such as pass/fail. Variable measurement reports a number, such as 18.012 mm. A number can show how close the process is getting to a limit; a passing gauge alone cannot.

For a plain hole, the GO plug checks the small-hole limit and should enter over the specified inspection length. The NO-GO plug checks the large-hole limit and should not enter as defined by the gauging procedure. Do not force either end. For a plain shaft, a GO ring should pass over the specified length and a NO-GO ring should not pass: the GO ring checks the large-shaft limit.

A GO plug that will not enter is a failed check, not proof of one particular cause: an undersize hole, burr, dirt, poor alignment or form error can obstruct it. Clean and investigate using the approved method. A NO-GO plug that enters beyond the permitted engagement is also a failed check.

Use the correctly identified gauge with current calibration status. Do not derive gauge sizes directly from workpiece limits. This plain-gauge explanation does not supply the engagement rules for threaded gauges.

Why the gauge itself needs limits and wear checks

Gauges cannot be manufactured to an infinitely exact size. Their own tolerances and wear allowances are selected under the applicable gauging specification. Repeated use can shrink a plug or enlarge a ring and change its decision.

Build a clean, repeatable surface-plate setup

A surface plate provides a reference plane. A height gauge measures from that plane; an indicator can compare the part with a reference or show variation as a surface is swept. Neither tool corrects a rocking part or dirt under a support.

  • Read the required feature and reference surfaces on the drawing; select a suitable instrument and range.
  • Clean the plate, supports and contact surfaces. Address burrs under the approved preparation method without altering the feature being inspected.
  • Support the part stably on the intended reference surfaces and allow temperature differences to settle as the measurement procedure requires.
  • Check the instrument against the appropriate reference, set zero or reference height, and use consistent contact force and alignment.
  • Repeat the reading, record the value and units, and investigate disagreement before accepting the result.

Instrument handling belongs in [[measurement-tools]] and [[reading-measuring-scales]]. Machine alignment is covered in [[mill-indicating-and-zero]]. Size acceptance and surface finish are separate requirements: see [[tolerances-and-fits]] and [[surface-texture]].

A CMM relates measured points to the part's references

A coordinate measuring machine (CMM) records positions in three dimensions using a probe or sensor. Software uses sampled points to evaluate features such as a plane, bore or hole location relative to the chosen part alignment.

The operator still needs a suitable setup, probe qualification, measurement strategy and drawing interpretation. A few points cannot reveal every possible surface defect. A CMM is a measurement system, not an automatic guarantee that every requirement has been checked.

Increase checks when the process is new or changing

Sampling means inspecting some parts to learn about a larger group or process. It reduces inspection effort but leaves a chance that unmeasured defects will escape. Select samples by a defined plan, not by choosing the nicest-looking parts.

A new setup, tool replacement, unexpected variation or a trend toward a limit calls for closer attention and potentially more frequent checks under the inspection plan. A demonstrated stable process may justify fewer checks; feature consequences and customer requirements still matter. Some features require every part to be inspected.

Keep the readings in order and compare their trend and spread, not just their pass/fail status. [[process-capability]] explains that distinction. Do not repeatedly adjust a stable process just because its readings differ slightly.

Trust a result only as far as the measurement supports it

Accuracy concerns agreement with a reference or true value; precision concerns agreement among repeated measurements under stated conditions. Readings can cluster tightly and still be consistently wrong.

Calibration compares instrument indications with reference values under specified conditions and establishes their relationship and uncertainty. It is not merely setting zero, and adjustment is a separate action. Traceability belongs to a measurement result supported by a documented chain of calibrations, each contributing uncertainty.

For classroom limit comparisons, treat the stated numbers as exact and include both endpoints. In real inspection, uncertainty matters near a limit: apply the specified acceptance decision rule rather than assuming the last displayed digit is exact.
