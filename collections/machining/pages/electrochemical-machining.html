ECM removes metal by electrochemical dissolution

In electrochemical machining (ECM), the metal workpiece is the positive electrode (anode), and the tool is the negative electrode (cathode). Direct current passes through a flowing, electrically conductive liquid called an electrolyte. Metal dissolves from the work surface while the tool advances without cutting contact.

Consider ECM for conductive metals, including hard alloys and complex contours. The tool shape, gap and electrolyte flow govern the resulting feature. A suitable electrolyte must be matched to the metal; a generic salt solution is not a universal process recipe.

ECM uses a conducting liquid; EDM uses controlled breakdown

What removes material?Electrochemical dissolutionHeat from discharges
What does the liquid do?Conducts current and carries reaction products awayInsulates between discharges; cools and flushes
Does the tool wear?No removal-related tool wear in principle; real tooling still needs maintenanceElectrode wear occurs; wire is renewed

ECM is not a melting process and normally avoids a heat-affected zone. That does not mean the electrolyte stays cold: current flow heats it. Flow, temperature, chemistry and gap control all matter.

Why hardness alone does not set ECM performance

The reaction removes metal without requiring a harder cutting edge. Metal chemistry and current conditions still affect dissolution, so “all metals machine at the same rate” is not a valid selection rule. Tooling and electrolyte control can make ECM attractive for repeated parts, while adding development effort for a one-off feature.
