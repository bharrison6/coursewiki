End Mill Anatomy

An end mill has cutting teeth separated by recessed flutes. Read the side and the cutting end together: the flute hollow, the cutting edge and the relieved face behind it are different features.

Original Blender-authored representative geometry. Use the lab below to orbit the same model, locate the named features and inspect the end. These are teaching dimensions, not a manufacturer part specification or setup recommendation.

  • Flutes are grooves beside the teeth that provide room for chips. The sharp edges bordering them do the cutting.
  • Helix angle describes the spiral relative to the tool axis. It affects how the edge enters the cut and the direction of cutting forces; it is not the spindle's rotation direction.
  • Cutting diameter is the diameter swept by the cutting edges. Shank diameter fits the holder and may differ from cutting diameter.
  • Overall length (OAL) runs from tool tip to shank end. Length of cut (LOC) is the axial cutting-edge length, not permission to cut that entire depth in one pass.
  • Length below shank (LBS) runs from the start of a reduced neck to the tip, so it includes both relieved neck and LOC. It is not the neck-only length.
  • Square end makes a flat-bottom form with a sharp nominal corner. A corner-radius or bull-nose end rounds that corner. A ball end has a rounded tip for curved surfaces.
  • Center-cutting geometry can cut at the tool center, allowing axial entry when the manufacturer permits it. Flute count alone does not prove a tool can plunge; check its entry method and limits.
Peripheral and end cutting edges
The sharp intersections of the rake and relieved faces cut on the side and the end respectively. An edge is not the same thing as the whole flute groove.
Rake face
The face ahead of the cutting edge, along which the newly formed chip flows.
Primary relief and land
The narrow ground surface just behind an edge clears the newly cut surface. Further relief behind it provides additional clearance; the exact grind varies with the tool.
Flute or gullet
The hollow beside a tooth provides chip space. The material between the flutes forms the teeth and the central core.
End gash and end relief
A gash is a recessed pocket at the end near the lips; the relieved face behind an end edge provides clearance. Neither is a decorative line drawn on a flat cap.
Core, runout and reduced neck
The core is the central material remaining between the flutes. Runout is where a groove blends into the shank. A reduced neck is a separate relieved reach region; the displayed four-flute model does not have one.
Helix and radial pitch
The helix angle is measured relative to the tool axis on its cylindrical surface. Radial pitch is angular spacing between teeth: four equally spaced flutes give 90°. These describe different directions.

Holder-face projection is the mounted distance from holder face to tip. Pulling the same tool farther out changes projection and insertion, not OAL, LOC or LBS. Follow the tool and holder makers' gripping requirements. A side cut and a full-width slot load the same cutter differently. Select the actual tool's data for the operation, then use [[feeds-and-speeds]] for the calculations and [[climb-and-conventional-milling]] for feed direction.

Why the two-for-aluminum, four-for-steel rule is only a starting point

Tool designers trade chip space against the core and cutting-edge arrangement. Purpose-built tools and controlled engagement can make other flute counts appropriate. See Harvey Performance: Why Flute Count Matters.

Match HSS, carbide and coating to the job and machine

High-speed steel (HSS) is relatively tough and often economical. It can be a useful choice at lower cutting speeds, especially where the setup cannot take full advantage of carbide. Cobalt HSS is an HSS family with improved hot hardness.

Cemented carbide retains hardness at higher cutting temperatures and is stiffer than HSS, but is less tolerant of impact and poor support. A carbide tool does not make a loose setup rigid. Select its grade and geometry as well as its material.

Coatings change wear, friction and heat behavior. TiN and TiCN are familiar options; AlTiN/TiAlN are common on tools for hot-running ferrous applications. For aluminum, choose an uncoated polished tool or a coating explicitly recommended for that alloy, such as TiB2 where specified. Color is not a reliable selection method, and no coating guarantees a fixed multiple of tool life.

Job examples: for an aluminum slot, start by comparing aluminum-specific two- and three-flute tools with enough chip space. For steel side milling on a rigid setup, a steel-rated four-flute carbide tool may be a good starting point. For a modest-speed, interrupted job, a suitable HSS tool may be more forgiving. Check the manufacturer's recommendations before selecting the cutting data.

Face mills and fly cutters cover broad flat surfaces

A face mill uses several cutting edges, often replaceable inserts, to machine a broad face. Match its diameter, insert geometry, holder and power demand to the machine and part.

A fly cutter carries a single cutting point that sweeps a circle. It can make a broad finishing pass, but its single edge and interrupted loading need suitable rigidity, balance and cutting data. It is not simply a face mill that can use the same feed setting.
