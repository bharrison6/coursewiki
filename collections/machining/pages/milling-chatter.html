Chatter is vibration of the cutting system that leaves a repeating pattern

The tool, holder, spindle, workpiece and fixture can all vibrate. Chatter often produces a strong sound and repeated ripples on the surface. Ordinary tooth marks, runout and a loose part can also spoil a finish; a sound alone does not prove which component is responsible.

End the cut using the machine's operating procedure before inspecting or changing the setup. Use [[equipment-operating-procedure]] and [[machine-hazards]] for those rules.

How one tooth can leave trouble for the next

A vibrating edge leaves a wavy surface. The next edge encounters changing chip thickness, which changes the force and can reinforce the vibration. This feedback is called regenerative chatter. Tooth impacts can also excite a flexible setup. Speed changes the timing; stiffness and engagement change how strongly the system responds.

Fix seating and rigidity before tuning the cut

  1. Check what changed. Look for a loose or lifted workpiece, incomplete holder seating, a damaged edge, packed chips or excessive runout. Correct the cause before another trial.
  2. Shorten and support. Reduce unnecessary tool and holder reach, improve the part's support and grip, and minimize quill extension where applicable. Keep the required shank engagement. See [[tool-deflection]] and [[milling-workholding]].
  3. Reduce or smooth engagement. Review width and depth of cut, especially at inside corners where more of the cutter enters the material. A smaller radial engagement or revised path may remove the trigger. See [[milling-operations]].
  4. Change spindle speed deliberately. Test a different permitted speed to change the excitation timing. A stable speed may be higher or lower; “always slow down” is not a complete rule.
  5. Recheck feed per tooth. When changing speed, review feed with it. Too little feed can rub, while excessive feed overloads the edge. Use the tool maker's range and [[feeds-and-speeds]].
  6. Reconsider the tool. A different diameter, flute spacing, helix, or cutter geometry may suit the remaining reach and engagement better. Change one factor at a time and record the result.

This is a diagnostic order, not a guarantee that every case needs every step. If the part moved, speed tuning is not the next experiment.

Use the location of the symptom to choose the next check

ObservationFirst useful check
Chatter began after fitting a longer tool.Compare unsupported reach and holder engagement with the previous setup.
Straight walls cut cleanly; inside corners chatter.Check the increase in radial engagement and the programmed corner path.
Only the unsupported end of a thin part vibrates.Review part support and clamping without distorting the part.
A rigid, correctly seated setup chatters at one speed.Try a permitted speed change and keep the intended chip load by reviewing feed.

Public demonstration and application reference

Haas: Avoid Chatter on Your Mill is a public demonstration. Sandvik Coromant: Long overhang—optimizing machining conditions discusses reach, force direction and tool choices. Neither supplies a universal speed for a different setup.

Tormach's chatter troubleshooting reference covers wear, toolholding, workholding and toolpath checks. Machine-specific settings in that reference apply only to the configuration it describes.
