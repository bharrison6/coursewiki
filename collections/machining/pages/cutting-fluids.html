Cool the cut, reduce friction and carry chips away

Coolant does more than cool. A cutting fluid removes heat from the cutting zone, lubricates contact between the tool, chip and workpiece, flushes loose chips away, and helps protect the machine and work against corrosion.

Lubrication can reduce material sticking to the cutting edge, called built-up edge. Together with cooling and chip removal, that can improve surface finish and dimensional control. Fluid cannot make up for a dull tool, poor workholding or unsuitable [[feeds-and-speeds|feeds and speeds]].

The fluid must reach the cutting zone to do its job. A full tank with a blocked filter or a nozzle aimed away from the cut is not effective delivery.

Choose a fluid family for the operation

Use a product approved for the machine, work material and tooling. These are general tendencies, not a product selection chart for a particular shop. Check compatibility with seals, coatings and the metals being cut.

Semi-syntheticA water-diluted formulation with less mineral oil than a conventional soluble oil. Often balances cooling, lubrication and cleanliness in general machining.Still needs water-quality and sump maintenance. Foam, residue and compatibility depend on the formulation.

Concentrate and working fluid are different. A supplier's oil-content figure for the concentrate is not the percentage to put in the tank. The working concentration comes from that product's technical data sheet.

Why the family name does not settle performance

Base fluid, additives, dilution and water quality all affect the result. A neat oil can use mineral, vegetable or other base oils; “straight” describes using it undiluted. A synthetic is not automatically harmless or maintenance-free. See the fluid-family comparison on page 7 of Washington State Department of Ecology's metalworking-fluid guide and HSE's exposure overview.

Run dry, mist or flood according to the cutting setup

  • Dry: use when the tool supplier's guidance for that material and operation calls for it. Some carbide milling benefits from avoiding repeated heating and sudden cooling of the edge. Dry does not mean chips or dust need no control.
  • Mist or minimum-quantity lubrication: a designed system meters a small amount of fluid toward the cut when lubrication and access matter more than bulk cooling. It does not provide flood-level heat removal or washing. Use the specified enclosure and mist control.
  • Flood: a continuous flow can remove heat and wash chips from the cut when the setup calls for wet machining. Supply enough flow to reach the edge consistently; a weak, intermittent splash is not the same thing.

Oil mist is liquid droplets carried by air, not a residue-free gas. Neither “all carbide runs dry” nor “more coolant is always better” is a useful rule. Follow the tool and machine guidance for the actual cut.

Why some milling runs dry while other milling needs fluid

A milling tooth repeatedly enters and leaves the material. Coolant can increase its temperature swing and promote thermal cracking in susceptible edges. Other operations need lubrication to limit adhesion, cooling to control distortion, or flow to clear chips. Sandvik Coromant's dry and wet milling guidance explains these competing demands.
