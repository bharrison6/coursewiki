Use certification as a goal for building dependable CAD skills

A CAD certification gives you a way to demonstrate skills assessed by its provider. Preparing for one can help you become more productive, build models that survive changes, and describe your skills on a resume. Passing does not guarantee a job or prove that every design you make will work.

The Certified SOLIDWORKS Associate (CSWA) is the worked case here, offered through Dassault Systèmes' SOLIDWORKS certification program. CourseWiki is an independent learning resource and is not affiliated with or endorsed by Dassault Systèmes.

Find the current exam rules before you register

Start at the official SOLIDWORKS certification program, then select the CSWA Design Associate page. Check the exact credential name: similarly named certifications can assess different tools.

  • Read the current exam description, skill coverage, format, length, question count, passing rules, and any section weights on the official page.
  • Follow the official registration or purchase route; confirm the current price and any voucher conditions before paying.
  • Check software, computer, account, and testing-client requirements, plus the rules for permitted resources and retakes.
  • Find the official sample or practice resources linked from the certification page and read their use instructions.

For current numerical exam details, see the official page. This guide does not reproduce an exam specification or sample exam. The official certification FAQ is another starting point for registration and testing questions.

Practise reading, modeling, assembling, and checking

Use these general skill areas to organize practice; use the official description to confirm what your chosen certification assesses.

  • Read a drawing: identify views, dimensions, and feature notes before building. Start with [[drawing-terminology-review]].
  • Sketch deliberately: use dimensions and geometric relations to control size and shape. Review [[sketch-geometric-relations]].
  • Build and revise parts: choose features that express the shape, then change a dimension and check the result. Review [[feature-based-modeling]].
  • Assemble components: place parts and constrain their intended positions or motion with mates. Review [[assembly-files-and-mates]].
  • Inspect the result: measure dimensions, check mass properties, and confirm the correct model state. Review [[cad-model-views-and-checks]].

Check geometry, units, and material before trusting mass

For a uniform solid, mass = density × volume. Use compatible units: density in g/cm³ multiplied by volume in cm³ gives mass in g. To convert mm³ to cm³, divide by 1,000 because 1 cm³ = 10 × 10 × 10 mm³.

Changing only the displayed units changes the number and unit label, not the physical size or mass. Entering a dimension in the wrong units changes the geometry. Choosing a different density changes mass even when the shape stays the same.

  • Confirm the drawing's units and the units used when entering dimensions.
  • Check overall dimensions, holes, and removed material against all relevant views.
  • Assign the specified material or density to the intended bodies or components.
  • Rebuild after edits; confirm the intended configuration and which bodies or components are included.
  • Read the requested quantity and its output units before recording an answer.
Why matching mass does not prove the shape is right

Different shapes can have the same volume and density, and therefore the same mass. A missing cut and extra material removed elsewhere can cancel numerically. Treat mass as one independent check alongside dimensions, feature locations, and drawing views.

Build accuracy first, then practise with a time limit

Choose an original, ungraded drawing you have permission to use. Build the part without following a click-by-click demonstration. Explain how each view supports the geometry, then check the dimensions and material. Repeat with a changed dimension to see whether the model updates as intended.

Once that process is reliable, set a practice time limit. Include time to read, model, check, and save. Record where you lose time and practise that skill next. During an actual attempt, follow the provider's rules about navigation, returning to questions, and saving work.

Keep a short error log: drawing interpretation, sketch control, feature choice, assembly position, units, or material. Rebuild after correcting the cause instead of memorizing the previous answer.
