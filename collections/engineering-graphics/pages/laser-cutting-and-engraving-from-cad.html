Use vector paths for outlines and raster scans for filled marks

A vector operation follows lines and curves. A raster operation scans across an area to make a surface image or filled mark.

A vector path can cut through a sheet or make a shallow score, depending on the material and settings. Raster engraving typically changes the surface without cutting out the part. A bitmap picture of an outline is not automatically a usable vector cutting path.

[[img:engineering-graphics/fabrication/vector-and-raster.svg|Vector motion follows a continuous rectangular boundary. Raster motion scans parallel rows across a filled rectangular area.]]

Practice choosing cut or engrave

Prepare a full-size profile and assign each operation deliberately

  • From CAD, export the intended planar face or sketch as a supported vector format such as DXF. View the face normal to its plane; a perspective picture is not a true-size profile.
  • In the vector editor, set units and page/piece size. Import the file and measure a known feature to confirm 1:1 scale.
  • Remove unwanted dimensions, notes, borders, and duplicate paths from the fabrication copy. Check for gaps in outlines that should be closed.
  • Assign cut paths, score paths if used, and raster areas to the correct operations. Inspect fills as well as strokes.
  • Match page/piece size and placement in the artwork and machine software. Preview the job and check its extent against the actual sheet.

CorelDraw is one possible vector editor. Line weight and color can tell a particular driver how to treat an object, but those conventions are not universal. Follow the posted procedure for that driver and confirm the operation in its preview.

Why a hairline is a workflow setting, not a cutting guarantee

The teaching deck uses hairline strokes for vector cuts and strokes of at least 0.5 pt for raster content. Preserve those values only when the specified driver procedure calls for them. Stroke width alone does not establish cut depth, material suitability, or a safe setting.

Offset the path toward the waste by half the kerf

Kerf is the width of material removed by a cut. In the simple model below, the laser follows the center of a constant-width kerf. Half the removed width lies on each side of the path.

For an outside part boundary, move the path outward by kerf / 2. For an opening, move the path inward toward the material being removed. Without compensation, a cutout part becomes smaller and a hole becomes larger.

[[img:engineering-graphics/fabrication/kerf.svg|An outside part has its cutting path outside the desired edge, while a hole has its cutting path inside the desired edge. Each path is half a kerf into the waste.]]

For a desired outside width W, the path-to-path width is W + k, where k is kerf. For a desired opening width H, it is H − k. These are whole-width changes; the offset at each edge is only k / 2.

Measure a test cut in the same material and setup. Kerf can vary with thickness, focus, and settings, and cut edges may taper. If software already compensates the path, do not apply the same allowance again. See [[tolerances-and-fits]] for fit requirements.

Treat power, speed, and pulse settings as a matched setup

Power controls laser output; speed controls how quickly the spot travels. With other conditions held fixed, higher power or slower travel generally puts more energy into a length of cut. That can also increase scorching or melting rather than improve the edge.

Frequency or pulse settings control how pulses are delivered. Controls may use pulses per second, pulses per distance, or a machine-specific scale; read the manual's definition. A percentage from one machine is not a transferable recipe for another.

Use the posted procedure and the manufacturer's starting settings for the exact machine, material, and thickness. Confirm the result on an approved test piece. Check that the cut separates and that engraving is readable before running the complete job.

Manufacturer guidance for selecting a starting setup

Epilog's material settings guidance treats its recommendations as starting points. A settings table cannot identify an unknown sheet or replace approval for a material.

Confirm the material and stay with the operating laser

Only process materials approved for that machine. Suitable grades of wood and acrylic are common CO₂-laser examples, but coatings, adhesives, thickness, and machine capability matter. A material that can be marked is not necessarily one the machine can cut through.

Do not cut or engrave PVC or vinyl containing PVC. Do not guess the identity of an unknown plastic. Never leave an operating laser unattended. Use the required extraction and follow the machine procedure for a flare-up or other abnormal condition.
