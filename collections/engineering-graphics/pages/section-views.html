Use an imaginary cut to expose the interior

A section view shows what is inside a part without making you untangle a group of hidden lines. Imagine cutting the part, removing the material between you and the cutting plane, and looking at what remains.

The cut changes the drawing representation, not the manufactured part. Hatch the material crossed by the plane and show the useful visible edges beyond it. Hidden lines are usually omitted in sections unless needed to make the shape clear.

Start with [[alphabet-of-lines]] and [[orthographic-projection]] if the line types or viewing directions are unfamiliar. For a sloping face or a small feature, see [[auxiliary-and-detail-views]].

Follow the cutting-plane arrows and match the letters

The cutting-plane line locates the imaginary cut in a parent view. Its arrows point in the direction you look into the remaining part. Matching letters connect that line to a label such as SECTION A–A; the letters do not specify a dimension.

Use a prominent cutting-plane line that extends beyond the part, with thin section lining in the resulting view. Line weight, dash pattern, and arrow size follow the drawing standard and template. These teaching diagrams use blue for cutting planes; color is not required to interpret a drawing.

  • Find both cutting-plane ends and the matching section label.
  • Follow the arrows to decide which side is removed from sight.
  • Trace solid material and voids separately across the plane.
  • Compare the resulting section with the parent view.

Slide a cutting plane and predict the hatch

Practise reading what a cut exposes. Move plane A–A through a part, decide which regions of SECTION A–A are cut material, then check. Only material the plane passes through is hatched. A hole or slot that the plane crosses stays blank, and so do the surfaces you can see beyond the plane.

Worked example: a full section through the flanged bushing. Plane A–A runs through the axis. Its arrows point toward the back of the part, so the front half is imagined removed and you look at the cut face from the front. Only material the plane passes through is hatched: the bore and counterbore are empty, and the surfaces you see through them lie beyond the plane.

Regions of the bushing section, top to bottom and left to right:

  1. Region 1: Not cut: you see the far wall of the counterbore, about 15.0 mm behind the plane. Draw its edges, but do not hatch it.
  2. Region 2: Cut material: the plane passes through the solid body and flange here, so it is hatched.
  3. Region 3: Not cut: you see the far wall of the through bore, about 10.0 mm behind the plane. Draw its edges, but do not hatch it.
  4. Region 4: Cut material: the plane passes through the solid body and flange here, so it is hatched.

Check the hatched area: each side of the bore contributes wall thickness × height, so 2 × [(20 − 10) × 30 + (30 − 10) × 2 + (30 − 15) × 8] = 920 mm². A hole crossed by the plane removes area from the cut face; it is never hatched.

The three parts are simplified teaching shapes, not dimensioned drawings. Round surfaces are modelled as 96-sided polygons, so every edge lies within 0.03 mm of a true circle at these sizes, and the hatched areas are exact for those polygons. The blind hole has a flat bottom; a drilled hole would end in a cone. Hidden lines are left out, as is usual in sections, and the explorer does not draw tangent edges where a flat face runs smoothly into a curve. It does not apply the rib, spoke and assembly exceptions described in “Hatch cut material, with conventional exceptions” below.

How the explorer decides what is hatched, and its sources

Each part is built from boxes and round features with vertical axes. The explorer cuts that solid exactly along the plane: the cut face shown on the 3D part and the hatching in SECTION A–A come from the same computed region, and holes inside it remain gaps. The view then shows the first surface you meet looking in the arrow direction. A line is drawn where that surface changes, and where the cut face meets a surface beyond the plane. In an offset section, the cut faces at the two legs are drawn as one face, with no line at the jog. A half section shows the outside on one side of the center line and the section on the other.

The conventions follow the U.S. Navy training course Blueprint Reading and Sketching, NAVEDTRA 14040, chapter 3, “Section Views” and its full, offset and half sections (pages 3-6 and 3-7), and Linn-Benton Community College’s open textbook Basic Blueprint Reading, “Sectional Views” (CC BY 4.0). For formal drawings, the edition of ASME Y14.3, Orthographic and Pictorial Views invoked by the drawing governs; the explorer is a reading exercise, not a statement of that standard.

Full sections cut all the way through the part

A full section uses one plane through the entire part. For this hollow cylinder, a plane through the axis exposes both walls and the bore. The bore is empty space and receives no hatching.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/full-section.svg|Top of a hollow cylinder with a straight A–A cut and upward viewing arrows; below, two hatched walls flank a blank bore.]]

Half sections combine the exterior and interior

A half section is useful for a symmetric part. Imagine removing one quarter with two perpendicular cutting planes. The resulting view shows one half in section and the other half as an exterior view, usually divided by a center line.

It is not a full section with half its hatch erased. The unsectioned half shows the outside, and symmetry supplies the connection between the two halves.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/half-section.svg|An L-shaped cut removes a quarter of a hollow cylinder; the resulting view shows exterior at left and a hatched wall at right.]]

Offset sections reach features that are not in one straight line

An offset section jogs the cutting plane to pass through several features. Project the selected parallel portions into one section. The jogs help explain the cut location; they do not create physical edges on the part.

Read the parent view for the holes’ actual locations. Their appearance together in the section does not mean they lie in one straight row.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/offset-section.svg|A jogged A–A cutting plane passes through three staggered holes in a plate; the section shows all three holes without false edges at the jogs.]]

Broken-out sections expose only a local area

A broken-out section removes a limited patch to a chosen depth in an existing view. An irregular break line marks the boundary. Use it to expose a small passage or wall without adding a complete section view.

A separate cutting-plane line is normally unnecessary: the local boundary identifies the area. Depth still matters; a cut that stops before a bore will not reveal it. This differs from a broken view, which merely shortens a long part on the sheet.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/broken-out-section.svg|A housing with a hidden horizontal passage, followed by a local irregular cutaway exposing the passage between hatched walls.]]

Revolved sections show a cross section in place

A revolved section turns a cross-sectional slice 90° into the plane of the drawing and places it on the parent view. It is useful for the shape of a bar, spoke, or arm. Show the slice itself, rather than the features behind the cut.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/revolved-section.svg|A transverse cut through a rectangular bar is rotated into the drawing and overlaid as a hatched rectangle on the bar.]]

Removed sections place the slice clear of the parent view

A removed section places the cross section away from the parent view to keep it readable. Match its identifying letters to the cutting plane. Several removed sections can describe changes along a long part; keep each location unambiguous.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/removed-section.svg|The same transverse cut through a rectangular bar is shown separately as section A–A rather than overlaid on the bar.]]

Aligned sections rotate an angled portion into the section plane

An aligned section uses intersecting cutting planes through features arranged around an axis. An angled portion is rotated into alignment before it is shown in the section. This makes radial features easier to read.

Do not infer angular spacing from the aligned result. In this disk, one small hole is rotated into the section plane; its original angular position is established by the top view.

[[img:engineering-graphics/section-views/aligned-section.svg|A disk has a central bore and two radial holes at different angles; the angled cutting-plane portion rotates into alignment to show the holes in section.]]

Hatch cut material, with conventional exceptions

Use thin, evenly spaced diagonal lines, commonly near 45° to the main outline. Change the angle when the hatch would be confused with an edge. Keep a part’s hatch consistent; distinguish adjacent assembly parts by changing hatch angle or spacing.

  • Voids: holes and open spaces are never hatched as solid material.
  • Ribs, webs, and spokes: a cut along their length generally leaves them unhatched so they do not look like a thick solid wall. A transverse cut through their thickness is hatched.
  • Shafts and fasteners in assembly sections: when cut lengthwise through their axes, solid shafts, bolts, nuts, washers, pins, and similar standard parts are commonly shown unsectioned. A transverse shaft section can be hatched; “never hatch a shaft” is too broad.

An unhatched region may be empty space, an uncut surface, or a conventionally unsectioned component. Use its outline and the other views to decide which.

Drawing standards and scope

These are introductory conventions, not clause quotations. The current edition of ASME Y14.3 and related drawing standards governs formal work, subject to the edition invoked by the drawing or contract. Consult that edition for exceptions and exact line requirements. Do not identify a material solely from generic diagonal hatching.

Create the section from a CAD drawing view, then inspect it

Start with a model-based parent drawing view. Choose a section command, define the cut, set its viewing direction, and place the result. These drawing operations do not remove material from the design model.

Full, half, and offset
Use a section-view command with a straight, half-section, or jogged cutting path. SOLIDWORKS calls the main tool Section View; Onshape uses Section view. Available half-section controls differ by application.
Aligned
Choose the aligned section option and define the intersection and angled portions. SOLIDWORKS uses its Section View PropertyManager; Onshape names the tool Aligned section view.
Broken-out
Define a closed local profile and a cut depth. The tools are Broken-out Section in SOLIDWORKS and Broken-out section view in Onshape.
Removed and revolved
Create a slice-only section, then place it apart or rotate and position it in the parent view. SOLIDWORKS documents Removed Section and a rotated-section workflow combining a break with section views. Onshape’s Show cut geometry only option can produce a slice without background geometry; check placement and rotation support in the installed version rather than assuming a dedicated revolved-section button.
  • Check that the plane crosses the intended features and the arrows face the intended direction.
  • Check the label, view scale, hidden-edge visibility, and hatch regions.
  • Exclude applicable assembly components from the cut and inspect ribs separately.
  • After a model change, update the drawing and recheck cut references and annotations.
