Rebuild a part by combining shared dimensions

A multiview drawing gives several descriptions of one fixed part. Read them together: front and top share width, front and side share height, and top and side share depth. Review [[orthographic-projection]] and confirm [[first-and-third-angle-projection|the projection convention]] before interpreting positions on the sheet.

  • Identify the front direction and the overall width, height, and depth.
  • Sketch a light enclosing box using [[pictorial-sketching|isometric construction]].
  • Choose a recognizable profile and locate its corners in the box.
  • Use the other views to decide which features continue, stop, or change along the depth.
  • Account for every visible and hidden edge before darkening the pictorial.

Do not assume that a front profile extends all the way through. The top or side view must support that interpretation. If two shapes still fit the information, identify the missing view or dimension rather than inventing a feature.

Match a surface by its boundaries, not its apparent shape

Face A in this original example is the front face. Face B is the sloping face made by removing a corner through the full depth. Follow their corner positions and shared boundaries across the views.

A appears in true shape in the front view. From above, all its points have the same front-to-back position, so it collapses to the front boundary line. From the right, it also appears edge-on. B appears as a sloping edge in front, but as a foreshortened area in top and right.

One drawn line can represent several boundaries

The edge view of a surface can coincide with the outline of another surface. A line is therefore not a unique surface label. Match its endpoints and the surfaces meeting there using another view. The colored areas and heavy highlighted segments here are teaching aids, not extra physical edges.

Normal, inclined, and oblique describe a flat surface relative to the view planes

Normal surface
Parallel to one principal projection plane and perpendicular to the other two. It shows true shape in one principal view and an edge in the other two. Face A is an example.
Inclined surface
Perpendicular to one principal plane and tilted to the other two. It shows an edge in one principal view and a foreshortened area in the other two. Face B is an example.
Oblique surface
Tilted to all three principal planes, with none parallel or perpendicular to it. It shows a foreshortened area in all three principal views; none shows true shape or an edge view.

Here “oblique surface” describes a face orientation. “Oblique sketch” describes a pictorial drawing method. A part with an inclined face can be drawn with either isometric or oblique sketching.

Turn a pictorial back into views one direction at a time

Keep the front direction fixed. For the front view, discard depth and plot width against height. For the top, discard height and plot width against depth. For the side, discard width and plot depth against height. Include changes of surface inside the outside outline.

Before checking, explain which boundary remains visible from above and where it lies relative to the width. A matching outside rectangle alone is not enough.

Predict and reveal views in the projection explorer

Choose a part, predict the missing view on paper, then reveal it. Change the part and repeat. Use the alignment guides to check shared dimensions. Changing the sheet layout changes view placement, not the part.

On paper: cover the top view, predict its outer rectangle and the line where the sloping face meets the flat top, then uncover it.
