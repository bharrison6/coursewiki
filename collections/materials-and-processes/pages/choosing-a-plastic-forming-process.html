Check geometry and material before comparing prices

A low quoted price is irrelevant if the process cannot make the required shape or use the required material. Start with [[plastics-as-materials]] and the capability cards in [[plastic-forming-processes]].

  • Describe the geometry: hollow container, open sheet-like shell, constant cross-section or discrete solid part.
  • Choose a material type and candidate grade that meet the load, temperature and environment.
  • State the quantity, expected repeat orders and required delivery rate.
  • Check how the part leaves the tool: draft, undercuts and access to internal spaces.
  • Compare tooling, per-part cost, achievable detail and likely defects for the remaining processes.

“Solid” does not mean “make every region thick.” An injection-molded housing is a discrete part, yet hollowing out bulky sections often improves it. Several processes can make a tank; size, quantity, resin and detail determine the better candidate.

Practice choosing a process

Spread tooling cost over the number of acceptable parts

A simple comparison is average cost per part = tooling cost / quantity + variable cost per part. Variable cost includes the costs that grow with production, such as material and processing time. Tooling cost is paid even if only a few parts are made.

Illustrative estimates, not supplier prices: suppose two processes can both meet a cover's specification. Route A costs $900 for tooling and $14 per part. Route B costs $18,000 for tooling and $2 per part.

Average cost per acceptable part, assuming no scrap or extra charges
QuantityRoute ARoute B
100$900 / 100 + $14 = $23.00$18,000 / 100 + $2 = $182.00
5,000$900 / 5,000 + $14 = $14.18$18,000 / 5,000 + $2 = $5.60
Find the quantity where the two estimates are equal

Set total costs equal: 900 + 14N = 18000 + 2N. Then 12N = 17100, so N = 1,425 parts. Below that quantity A costs less; above it B costs less under these assumptions.

This model leaves out financing, mold replacement, setup changes, inspection, shipping and rejects. A flexible mold that must be replaced during a run adds another tooling cost. Recalculate when the assumptions change.

Cycle time and cavity count set the ideal production rate

A cavity makes one part per cycle. With cycle time in seconds, ideal parts per hour = 3600 / cycle time × cavities.

For a 30-second cycle and two identical cavities: 3600 / 30 × 2 = 240 parts/h. That assumes uninterrupted operation and every part acceptable. Use the complete repeating cycle time, including opening and ejection; do not add overlapping cooling time twice.

Actual useful output is lower when setup, stoppages or rejects occur. More cavities may increase tooling cost and require a larger machine, so a higher ideal rate does not by itself settle the process choice.

Explain the choice with both a benefit and a limitation

A useful selection sounds like this: “For 300 large hollow PE garden reservoirs, investigate rotational molding because the shape fits and tooling can be economical at modest quantities. Confirm wall control and cycle time before committing.”

The quantity alone does not prove that choice. A narrow neck, precision sealing face, delivery deadline or different resin could change it. State the assumptions, identify a credible alternative, and compare the features that matter.
