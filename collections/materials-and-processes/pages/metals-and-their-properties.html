Metal selection starts with the job the part must do

A metal can carry a load, conduct current, transfer heat or resist wear. Those are different jobs. Start with the required behavior, then compare a particular alloy in a particular condition. An alloy combines a base metal with other elements to change its behavior.

Metals belong to the material-family foundations topic. Use [[material-properties]] for the general property vocabulary; this page applies it to metals. Continue with [[types-of-steel]], [[nonferrous-metals]], [[metal-stock-forms]] and [[heat-treating-metals]].

Metallic bonding helps explain conduction and forming

In a metal, some electrons are shared throughout the structure rather than tied to one bond between two atoms. Their mobility helps metals conduct electricity and heat. Metallic bonding can remain intact while atoms rearrange during permanent deformation, which helps explain why many metals can be rolled or drawn.

Most engineering metals contain many tiny crystals called grains. Atoms have an ordered arrangement inside each grain; adjacent grains have different orientations. Grain size, alloy chemistry and processing history all influence properties.

Why two pieces of the same alloy can behave differently

Permanent deformation involves movement of crystal defects called dislocations. Grain boundaries, dissolved alloying atoms and small particles can obstruct that movement. Cold working and heat treatment change these obstacles. Composition alone therefore cannot tell you the strength of a finished piece.

Strength, hardness, ductility and toughness answer different questions

Mechanical properties used when comparing metals
PropertyQuestion it answersSelection example
StrengthHow much stress can the material withstand? Yield strength concerns the start of permanent deformation; tensile strength concerns the maximum stress in a tensile test.A loaded bracket must resist permanent bending.
HardnessHow strongly does the surface resist local indentation?A wear surface may need hardness, but hardness alone does not predict every kind of wear.
DuctilityHow much permanent deformation can occur before fracture?A formed part needs enough ductility to survive bending.
ToughnessHow much energy can the material absorb before fracture?A part struck repeatedly needs resistance to fracture, not simply a hard surface.

Strong does not mean stiff. Stiffness describes elastic deflection. Hardening a steel can raise its strength substantially without a comparable change in its elastic stiffness. Geometry also affects how far a part bends.

Density, conductivity and corrosion can decide the choice

Density is mass per volume. Compare equal volumes when comparing densities; a large aluminum part can still weigh more than a small steel part. Use [[geometry-mass-and-force]] and [[dimensional-analysis]] for mass, weight and unit calculations.

Conductivity describes how readily heat or current travels through a material. Metals vary widely: choosing a good conductor is different from choosing a high-strength alloy.

Corrosion resistance depends on the metal and its surroundings. Stainless steel and aluminum can corrode. Water, salts, trapped moisture and contact with other metals can change performance. A protective surface film helps only while the environment allows it to remain effective.

Why connecting two different metals can change corrosion

When dissimilar metals are electrically connected in a conducting liquid, such as salty water, galvanic corrosion can accelerate attack on the more active metal. Material pairing, exposed areas and the environment matter. Isolation and drainage can be as important as the alloy choice.

Ferrous means iron-based; a magnet does not identify the grade

Ferrous metals have iron as their base: steels and cast irons belong here. Nonferrous metals have another base, such as aluminum, copper or titanium. Nonferrous does not mean that no trace of iron can be present.

Magnetic response is a clue, not a material certificate. Some stainless steels are weakly magnetic or effectively nonmagnetic in certain conditions. Appearance and a magnet cannot establish an alloy, its temper or its strength.
