Choose the required geometry before choosing the machine

A machine name does not describe a finished part. First state the result: a straight edge, a curved outline, a flat face, a hole or a round profile. Then consider the workpiece dimensions, accuracy, surface quality and available tooling. The machines below remove material and belong to Separating.

This is a selection guide, not an operating procedure. Machine-specific use belongs with [[machine-hazards]] and [[equipment-operating-procedure]]. Grain and moisture are covered in the wood-as-a-material topic; the operation selected here depends on those properties.

  • Describe the surface or cut that must exist when the operation is complete.
  • Identify which existing face or edge can locate the workpiece.
  • Check machine capacity and whether the cutter can reach the feature.
  • Decide whether the cut is final size or leaves material for another operation.

Router bits make profiles and channels; templates repeat shapes

A router spins a router bit. Straight or spiral bits make channels and pockets; roundover and chamfer bits change an edge's profile. A bearing-guided bit or guide bushing can follow a template, which is a pattern used to reproduce an outline.

Routers may be handheld, mounted in a table or moved by a CNC machine. A shaper commonly carries profile cutters on a table-mounted spindle. Both shape wood, but their cutters and mounting systems are not interchangeable merely because the resulting profile looks similar.

Why “router bit” is more useful than “end mill” here

CNC routing can use end-mill-like spiral cutters, but routing also includes bearing-guided and shaped profile bits. “End mill” alone does not describe the tooling a beginning woodworker will encounter. Whiteside's template-bit range illustrates tooling for following patterns; its catalog also includes straight bits, flush-trim spiral bits and roundover profiles.

Spindle and faceplate turning put grain differently relative to the axis

In typical spindle turning, grain runs mainly parallel to the rotation axis, as in a handle or table leg. In typical faceplate or bowl turning, grain runs mainly across the axis, as in a bowl blank. Faceplates and chucks are workholding methods; the name alone does not guarantee the grain orientation.

Turning removes material to make circular features. It does not bend the wood. [[lathe-operations]] explains metal-lathe operations as a separate topic; wood turning has different tools and workholding practices.
