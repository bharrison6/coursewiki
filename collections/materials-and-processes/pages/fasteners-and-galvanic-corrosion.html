Match the fastener to the assembly and its service access

Common mechanical joining components
ComponentPurposeDesign check
Bolt and nutProvide a removable threaded connection that can clamp parts together.Provide tool access, suitable thread engagement and a compatible strength and material specification.
WasherProvides a bearing surface or distributes load where the design requires it.A plain washer does not by itself prevent loosening or electrically isolate an entire joint.

Standard fasteners often reduce dedicated tooling for short runs, but every fastener adds parts and assembly time. Riveting or automated fastening may suit long runs. Removability, inspection and repair can justify a threaded joint even when another method is faster.

A thread size alone does not specify a complete fastener

The mating threads must agree in diameter, spacing and thread system. Similar-looking inch and metric parts are not interchangeable. Pitch describes axial spacing between adjacent thread forms; threads per inch describes a count over a unit length.

A purchase or drawing specification also needs the relevant length, head style, material or strength grade, coating and thread-fit requirements. The wrench size is not the thread diameter. Use the applicable thread reference and supplier drawing to decode a designation before choosing mating parts.

Galvanic corrosion needs a metal pair, electrical contact and an electrolyte

Dissimilar metals can form a corrosion cell when they are electrically connected and share a conductive liquid, called an electrolyte. Moisture containing salts can provide that liquid. The more active member of the pair acts as the anode and may corrode faster; the other acts as the cathode.

[[img:materials-and-processes/metal-processes/galvanic-pair.svg|A wet joint connects a more active metal to a more noble metal, completing a corrosion cell. A second sketch shows an insulating sleeve and washers separating a fastener from the surrounding plate.]]

A galvanic series ranks metals by their behavior in a specified environment. It helps screen combinations; it does not predict a service life. Alloy condition, surface films, wetting and exposed areas matter. A dry pair is not automatically a working galvanic cell.

Why the wetted areas matter

A small active area coupled to a large noble area can suffer concentrated attack when both areas contact the electrolyte. That is why coating only the active member is not automatically a cure: a small defect in that coating can leave a small wetted anode coupled to a large cathode. The relevant areas are those participating in the wet corrosion cell, which can change as a joint dries. A compatibility decision must consider the complete joint and environment.

Sources for galvanic contact, coatings and area effects

ASSDA Technical FAQ 1 (PDF), pages 1–2, explains electrical contact, conductive liquid, coating defects and relative wetted areas. Hilti's Corrosion Handbook (PDF), printed pages 12 and 26–27, illustrates the cell, separation measures and environment-specific material pairing.

Review the complete joint before calling a fastener compatible

“Stainless” does not mean compatible with every metal, and a coating is not a permanent guarantee of isolation. Consider material pairing, durable electrical separation, drainage and a suitable protective finish together. An insulating washer under a head does not isolate a shank that still touches the hole wall.

  • Identify the base metal, fastener, washer and their coatings.
  • Identify where electrical contact and moisture can occur.
  • Check the material pairing for the actual service environment.
  • Review sleeves, washers or gaskets for complete isolation and mechanical suitability.
  • Provide drainage and an inspectable, maintainable finish.

Compatibility review does not replace structural sizing or joint design.
