Manufacturing turns materials into useful products

Manufacturing changes materials into parts and products people can use. A board becomes a shelf, plastic pellets become a bottle, and metal sheet becomes a bracket. Shape, properties, joints and surfaces all contribute to the result.

A process is an operation that makes one of those changes. Most products need several processes. A useful plan states what each operation must achieve and how the result will be checked.

Two organizing questions keep the subject manageable: What is the material? Use [[material-families|the four material families]]. What change are we making? Use [[manufacturing-process-categories|the five process categories]].

Primary processing makes usable material; secondary processing makes parts

Primary processing converts raw resources into usable materials and common supply forms. Secondary processing turns those materials into parts or products. Stock means material supplied for further work, such as sheet, rod, boards or pellets.

Metal
Ore is refined into metal and made into stock such as sheet; sheet is then cut and bent into a bracket.
Plastic
Chemical feedstocks are made into polymer resin, often supplied as pellets; pellets are then molded into a bottle cap.
Wood
Logs are sawn into lumber; boards are then cut, joined and finished as furniture.
Glass
Mineral ingredients are melted and made into sheet glass; sheets are then cut and edge-finished for a window.

These terms describe a material's place in the supply chain. They do not replace the five process categories: sawing a log and sawing a furniture part are both separating.

Why the boundary needs context

Different industries draw the primary/secondary boundary differently. Here, use the raw-resource-to-stock and stock-to-part distinction. Extraction supplies the raw resource; mining alone does not turn ore into an ingot. A supplier's finished product can also be the next manufacturer's starting material.

Choose a process that fits material, quantity, cost and quality

Material
Can it tolerate the heat, force or chemical exposure? A process that bends metal sheet may crack a ceramic plate.
Quantity
A few parts favor flexible methods with little dedicated tooling. Repeated production can justify a mold or other tool made specifically for that part.
Cost
Count material, setup, tooling, labor, processing time, scrap and inspection. Cheap starting material can be expensive to turn into an acceptable part.
Required quality
Specify dimensions, allowable variation, strength and surface condition. Choose a route that can meet them consistently, then verify it.

For a small plastic cover, 3D printing may be useful for a few trial pieces; molding may suit repeated production after the design settles. Neither route wins from quantity alone: material behavior, geometry and acceptance requirements still matter.

Prototype, batch and mass production describe production situations, not universal numerical cutoffs. Manufacturing economics and design for manufacturing develop this comparison further.

Read every material unit through the same six-stage map

Characteristics → Forming → Separating → Fabricating → Conditioning → Finishing

  1. Characteristics: What is the material like? Compare [[material-properties|properties]], useful forms and limitations.
  2. Forming: How can it take a shape?
  3. Separating: How can material be removed or cut apart?
  4. Fabricating: How can parts be joined?
  5. Conditioning: How can its internal properties be changed?
  6. Finishing: How can its surface be protected or improved?

This is the collection's learning map for the later plastics, wood and metals topics. Characteristics is the starting description, not a sixth process category.

The map is not a mandatory factory sequence. Wood may be dried before cutting; a metal part may be annealed between forming steps; some surfaces are finished before assembly. A product may repeat a category or omit one.
