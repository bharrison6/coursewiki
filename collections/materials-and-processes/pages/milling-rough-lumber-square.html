A jointer establishes flatness; a planer establishes thickness

Stock is the material a part starts from. Rough lumber may have uneven surfaces or warp. A jointer removes material to establish a flat face. With that face as a reference against a square fence, it can establish a straight edge at 90° to the face.

A thickness planer cuts the upper face relative to the face supported by its bed. With a flat reference face down, it makes the opposite face parallel and brings the board to thickness. Jointing both faces separately does not ensure they are parallel.

[[img:materials-and-processes/wood-shaping/jointer-versus-planer.svg|A jointer makes one face flat while the opposite face remains uneven; a planer references that flat face to make the opposite face parallel.]]

Why a planer alone can preserve a bow

Feed rollers can press a bowed board flatter during the cut. When the pressure is released, the board can spring back. Uniform thickness and flatness are different requirements. Specialized fixtures can provide a reference for other methods, but the sequence here assumes an ordinary jointer and thickness planer.

Build reference surfaces in order: face, edge, thickness, width, length

This is the geometry sequence for preparing rough stock. It assumes enough material remains for the finished dimensions and that the stock fits the equipment. It is not a machine setup guide; use [[equipment-operating-procedure]] and [[machine-hazards]] for operation.

[[img:materials-and-processes/wood-shaping/milling-sequence.svg|Five stages: face-joint a flat face, edge-joint square to that face, plane the opposite face parallel, rip the opposite edge parallel, then crosscut ends to length.]]

  1. Face-joint: establish a flat face and mark it as the reference.
  2. Edge-joint: use the reference face to establish one straight edge square to it.
  3. Plane: place the reference face against the planer bed and bring the opposite face parallel to it at the required thickness.
  4. Rip: use the straight reference edge to locate the cut; make the opposite edge parallel at the required width.
  5. Crosscut: establish a square end, then cut the other end to the required length.

Any preliminary breakdown into shorter, oversized blanks comes before final sizing. Leave allowance for the later cuts; an early rough crosscut is not the final crosscut in this sequence.

Practice the milling order Practice the reference face

Check geometry instead of counting machine passes

One pass is not proof that a surface is ready. The result of each stage is the reference for the next. Flatness means a surface lies in a plane; parallel faces maintain a constant separation; square surfaces meet at 90°.

  • Check the reference face for flatness with a straightedge and for twist with winding sticks, a matched pair of sighting bars.
  • Check the reference edge for straightness and squareness to the face.
  • Measure thickness at several positions after planing.
  • Check width, parallel edges, end squareness and final length.
  • Recheck dimensions and geometry if material removal releases stresses and the board moves.

Stop and reconsider the intended dimensions if flattening would make the board too thin or too narrow. No sequence can recover material already removed. Use [[how-saws-cut-wood]] to account for kerf in the width and length layout.
