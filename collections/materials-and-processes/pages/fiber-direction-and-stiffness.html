Fiber direction changes elastic response

A fiber-reinforced composite combines a reinforcement with a surrounding matrix. In a unidirectional layer, the reinforcement is continuous and parallel. The layer is much stiffer along the fibers than across them, so its elastic response depends on direction.

Stress is force divided by loaded area. This lesson compares patches under the same uniform axial stress, so each square millimeter carries the same applied force. Strain is deformation divided by original dimension. Elastic stiffness connects stress to strain in the reversible small-strain model.

The illustrative material uses E1 = 40 GPa along the fibers, E2 = 10 GPa across them, G12 = 5 GPa, and ν12 = 0.30. These round teaching values do not describe a named composite and are not design allowables.

Predict the least stretch, then turn the fibers

Read the directions: the fiber angle is the starting, unloaded direction measured from the horizontal load axis. In this drawing, +45° slopes up to the right and −45° slopes down to the right. Axial means along the load; transverse means across it. The drawn fibers follow the exaggerated deformation, so their visible slope can differ from the starting angle on the control.

Predict first: identical free material patches carry uniform axial stress σx = 10 MPa, with no applied transverse or shear stress. Which orientation stretches least: 0°, 45°, or 90°?

Result: 0° stretches least because the stiff fiber direction is aligned with the load. Opposite 45° angles have the same axial stiffness and stretch, but their shear strain has opposite sign.

This is a freely deforming material patch loaded by uniform edge traction. It is not a tensile-test coupon held in rigid grips: grips would restrain the off-axis skew and create another boundary-value problem.

Off-axis extension can also create shear strain

At 0° and 90°, the loading direction matches a principal material direction. The patch stretches and narrows without extension–shear coupling. At an off-axis angle, an axial stress can also make the free patch skew. Turning +45° to −45° mirrors that skew while leaving the axial stiffness unchanged.

The interactive drawing exaggerates every strain by the same factor of 120. A larger drawn change therefore means a larger calculated strain; the display does not secretly rescale each angle to fill the frame.

How the axial stiffness is calculated

Let m = cos θ and n = sin θ. For this plane-stress orthotropic layer, the compliance in the load direction is S̄11 = m⁴/E1 + n⁴/E2 + m²n²(1/G12 − 2ν12/E1). Then Ex = 1/S̄11 and εx = σx/Ex. The full stress and strain transformation supplies transverse and shear strain.

Stiffness does not predict strength

A smaller strain under the same stress means greater elastic stiffness. It does not say when the material fails, which direction is safest, or which laminate should be built. Strength needs failure data and criteria; a laminate also needs its complete stacking sequence, ply thicknesses, bonding, environment, loading, and boundary conditions.

This lesson models one thin, continuous-fiber lamina in small-strain linear elasticity and plane stress. It omits failure, damage, buckling, bending, temperature, moisture, manufacturing defects, grips, and interaction between multiple plies.

Primary mechanics source and model boundary

NASA Reference Publication 1351, Basic Mechanics of Laminated Composite Plates, sections I and II, defines isotropic and orthotropic behavior, develops plane-stress compliance and off-axis transformation, and identifies extension–shear coupling. Its stated scope separates elastic response from strength. The constants and examples on this page are original illustrative teaching inputs, not values from that report.
