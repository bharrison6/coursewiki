Prove the parts fit before dividing areas

Stock is material purchased for further work. Nesting arranges part outlines on stock to reduce waste. An area ratio cannot prove a layout fits. Check dimensions, orientation, grain direction where relevant, edge margins and spacing.

Kerf is the width removed by a cut. An offcut is leftover stock; a usable offcut may be saved. Include trim cuts and holding allowances before promising a part count.

Illustrative layout: a 1,000 mm bar supplies 98 mm blanks. Each blank requires one 2 mm separating cut, including the last blank, with no end trim. Ten blanks consume 10 × (98 + 2) = 1,000 mm. A different last-cut arrangement would need a different cut count.

Practice kerf allowance

Test the next-blank threshold

Predict first: can the same 1,000 mm stock still supply ten 98 mm blanks if separating kerf increases from 2 to 3 mm? What happens to stock cost per blank when the stock price stays $48?

This one-dimensional lab reserves a full separating kerf after every blank, including the last. Left and right excluded trim lengths already include their own cut losses. The layout does not use the far stock edge as a finished blank edge; it is a count under this policy, not a claim of globally maximum yield.

Usable interval U = stock length L − left excluded trim − right excluded trim
Blank count n = floor[U / (blank length b + separating kerf k)]. Round down to a whole number. There are n separating cuts, plus any preparation already accounted for in excluded trim.

Each outlined block is an actual 98 mm blank; the narrow filled band after each is its full 2 mm kerf. Ten blanks plus ten kerfs fill this stock exactly. On narrow screens, scroll sideways to inspect the full bar.

Material balance: 1,000 mm = 0 excluded trim + 980 acceptable blanks + 20 separating kerfs + 0 offcut. Utilization = 980 / 1,000 × 100 = 98.0%.

Change the kerf to 3 mm: ten blanks would need 10 × (98 + 3) = 1,010 mm, so only nine fit. The new balance is 1,000 = 0 + 882 + 27 + 91 mm. The 91 mm offcut is 10 mm short of another 101 mm blank-and-kerf interval.

Allocate the whole stock charge: $48 / 10 = $4.80 per acceptable blank in the original layout; $48 / 9 ≈ $5.33 after the kerf change. All equal-cross-section blanks are assumed acceptable. No remnant credit or processing cost is included. If zero blanks fit, cost per blank is undefined.

Another threshold: at 2 mm kerf, 999.99 mm stock fits nine blanks, while 1,000.00 mm fits ten. This lab uses hundredths of a millimeter and currency cents; its bounded illustrative values are not machine settings. It is not a two-dimensional nesting tool.

Use a consistent physical basis for utilization

Utilization = material in acceptable parts / material purchased × 100%. Compare length for the same cross-section, area for the same thickness, or mass on a consistent basis.

The bar example gives 980/1,000 × 100 = 98.0%. The remaining 2% is kerf. Distinguish reusable offcuts from material discarded.

Why 20% loss means a 25% increase per useful amount

If 80% of purchased material reaches the product, one useful unit needs 1/0.80 = 1.25 purchased units, not 1.20. State whether a waste percentage is based on purchased material or on the useful amount.

Practice utilization

Charge each part its share of stock and waste

Part material cost = share of stock charged to the part × stock price. A $48 sheet yielding eight identical acceptable blanks gives $48 × 1/8 = $6.00 per blank when the whole sheet is charged to those blanks. This includes their share of kerf and unusable offcuts.

Finished-part area divided by stock area can understate cost by leaving waste unassigned. State any credit for reusable remnants. Add delivery only if it is not already in the stock price.
