Separate the bending step from the cutting and bonding steps

Bending changes the shape of wood and belongs to Forming. A complete route can use several of the five process categories: cutting thin strips or kerfs is Separating; bonding layers is Fabricating. Veneering bonds a thin wood facing to a supporting base, so it is not automatically a bending operation.

Wood remains in the Wood & Composites material family. See [[manufacturing-process-categories]] for the process model. Wood structure, moisture and engineered-wood products belong to the wood-as-a-material topic; here the question is how to obtain a shape.

Steam makes suitable wood more bendable; the form holds the curve

Heat and moisture soften the wood's lignin-rich matrix, allowing the piece to deform more readily. The wood does not become a liquid. It is bent around a form, a shaped support, and held while it cools and dries. Clear, straight-grained white oak and ash are common candidates; species alone does not guarantee a successful bend.

The outside of a bend tends to stretch while the inside compresses. Too much stretching can split the outer fibers; too much compression can wrinkle the inner face. Grain deviations, drying history, moisture, thickness and bend radius all matter. A larger radius is a gentler bend. There is no single radius-to-thickness ratio that works for every piece.

Why heat and moisture work together

Moisture lowers the temperature at which the wood's matrix softens. This is why moist heat can make wood bendable without melting it. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's Wood Handbook chapter on specialty treatments discusses plasticizing wood. Its Bending Solid Wood to Form compares species and bending conditions.

These are process concepts, not steaming-equipment instructions. Equipment use belongs with [[equipment-operating-procedure]] and [[machine-hazards]].

Bent lamination uses thin strips and adhesive to retain a curve

Thin strips bend more easily than one solid piece of the same total thickness. Bent lamination brings prepared strips together with adhesive over a form, then restrains them until the bond has developed as specified for that adhesive. The strips commonly run with their grain along the curve. Alternating grain direction is not a requirement of bent lamination.

Springback is the tendency for a bend to open when restraint is removed. It can occur in both steam-bent and laminated parts. Thinner laminations can reduce springback for a given total thickness and curve, but adhesive behavior, layer count and material also matter. A trial bend establishes any form compensation; there is no universal correction percentage.

[[img:materials-and-processes/wood-shaping/steam-versus-lamination.svg|Steam bending curves one softened piece around a form; bent lamination curves several thin strips and bonds them together over a form. Both can spring back when released.]]

Do not rank every “hot/wet” part above every “cold/dry” part in strength. Compare the actual grain path, remaining section, bond quality, loading and service conditions.

Practice bending-route selection

Kerf bending trades an intact section for easier curvature

A series of partial-depth slots on the inside of the intended curve leaves a thin continuous web on the outside. The slots narrow as the piece bends. Slot depth and spacing, the remaining web and the desired radius determine how it deforms.

This method can suit curved coverings where the slotted side is concealed. It also removes load-carrying material. Filling or bonding the slots does not automatically restore the properties of the original uncut board. A backing or supporting structure may be part of the design; structural performance needs its own justification.

Veneer follows a prepared substrate; it does not replace it

Veneer is a thin wood layer. A substrate is the base it is bonded to. Veneering puts a chosen grain appearance over that base and can cover a flat surface or an appropriate curved form. The substrate must have the intended shape and a sound, even bonding surface.

In book matching, adjacent veneer leaves are opened like book pages to make a mirrored pattern. In slip matching, leaves are placed beside one another in the same orientation for a repeated pattern. Neither arrangement is the same as alternating structural grain directions through a panel's thickness.

Unequal face and back construction can encourage a panel to warp. Veneer thickness, grain, adhesive and the balancing back layer belong in the layup design. Glue selection and detailed bonding methods belong to wood fabrication; engineered panels belong to the material topic.
