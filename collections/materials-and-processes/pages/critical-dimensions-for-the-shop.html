A critical dimension controls fit, function or an interface

A critical dimension is one whose variation can stop the part doing its job. Start with what touches another part, what must move or clear, and what locates the part. A hole's diameter may control whether a pin enters; its position may control whether two parts line up.

Critical does not mean “make every tolerance tight.” A tolerance is the permitted variation in a dimension. Choose it from the function, then check that the process and inspection method can achieve it. See [[tolerances-and-fits]] for limits and fits, [[measurement-language]] for units and measurement language, and [[surface-texture]] for surface requirements.

Give hole locations from named reference edges

The original DL-01 display plate below illustrates a shop drawing. Its two suspension holes must stay at the intended locations; length and width mainly establish the display area. These are invented teaching limits, not a general specification for plywood or hanging hardware.

How to check a center when a caliper touches the hole edge

Measure from the reference edge to the nearest hole edge, then add half the measured hole diameter to obtain that center coordinate. Use the same reference and direction as the drawing. This example does not specify a precision pin fit; a more demanding interface needs a more capable measurement method and a reviewed tolerancing scheme.

The nominal hole spacing is 70 mm, derived from 85 − 15. The separate ±0.5 mm x-coordinate limits can allow spacing from 69 to 71 mm. If an interface needs a tighter spacing, the designer must change the requirements; the inspector must not invent a tighter limit.

Dimension the part so it can be made without measuring the picture

Include the part identity, revision, material, units, overall dimensions, feature sizes and locations, thickness, and required finish. Supply enough views to remove ambiguity. Call a hole “through” when it passes through the material; otherwise specify depth.

Locate related features from a common reference where that matches their function. Do not give multiple independent dimensions for the same feature unless their relationship is clear. A redundant dimension can contradict the dimensions used to make the part.

  • Identify every mating surface, clearance and locating feature.
  • Choose references the maker can establish and the inspector can reach.
  • Check that each feature has a size, location and acceptance limit.
  • Remove conflicting or missing dimensions; do not rely on drawing scale.
  • Match the drawing revision to the method sheet and BOM.
