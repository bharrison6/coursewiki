A printed pattern defines the cavity in the silicone mold

A pattern is the original shape copied by a mold. For a simple one-part, open-face mold, use a shape with a flat back and features that can be withdrawn through the opening. An original example is a small decorative relief tile with a flat back, gently sloping sides and no hooked details.

Draft is a slight taper that helps a part leave the mold. Let the shape widen toward the opening. A flat parting face, where the pattern meets the mold-box base, defines the open back of this mold. Avoid undercuts that hook behind the silicone; flexibility does not make every trapped shape removable.

A wide mouth does not rule out an interior hook. This illustrative model uses an exact-fit, centered, symmetric rigid 2D pattern and rigid cavity. Opening depth is 0; positive depth points down into the mold. The only permitted motion is outward translation upward at fixed orientation, into unobstructed space. Units are arbitrary consistent diagram units.

At cavity depth d, an outward pull t places pattern half-width w(d+t) against cavity half-width w(d). Positive Δ(d) = w(d+t) − w(d) means overlap. For the local shoulder at t = 5, the positive interval is (50/3, 520/23), approximately (16.6667, 22.6087); endpoints are zero-contact boundaries.

The continuous path requires width to be nonincreasing with depth. A deeper section wider than a shallower section provides an obstruction. For each pose, Δ is linear between original and translated knots; checking these knots and solving zero crossings is analytical, not an animation-frame test. At t = 0 every profile is in initial contact. At t = 40 there is no interior overlap, but the reverse taper and local shoulder still have blocked paths: a clear endpoint does not prove a reachable endpoint.

Comparisons use a numerical threshold of 64 × machine epsilon × maximum half-width, separate from physical clearance and manufacturing tolerance. Values within that floating-point threshold are treated as contact. This is geometric interference only: it omits friction, adhesion, shrinkage, forces, damage, cure, rotation, lateral motion, deformation and unshown 3D features. No-interference does not establish practical release; a rigid obstruction does not establish flexible-silicone failure.

Remove unwanted layer ridges and support marks without losing important dimensions. Choose pattern preparation, any sealer and any release agent for the specific print, silicone and casting material. Follow the manufacturers' compatibility instructions and test a small prepared sample before committing the full mold.

Read the technical data sheet before choosing a mix or cure schedule

The technical data sheet (TDS) supplies processing instructions. The safety data sheet (SDS) supplies handling information; use [[chemicals-and-sds]] for that guidance. Neither a generic lesson nor a familiar-looking container replaces the instructions for the actual product.

  • Record the silicone product, batch identification and technical-sheet revision.
  • Record the A:B ratio and whether it is by mass (weight) or by volume.
  • Record working time, mixing procedure, bubble-removal requirements and cure conditions.
  • Check compatibility with the printed pattern, coatings, release agent and intended casting material.
  • Complete a small compatibility trial and inspect its cure before the full pour.

Working time is the available time to mix and place the material under stated conditions. It is not the demolding time. Cure instructions may distinguish demolding from full properties or further treatment. Record the actual temperature and times; do not substitute a universal overnight wait.

Where to find product-specific instructions

Start with the manufacturer's product documentation, such as Smooth-On's technical bulletin and SDS directory. Its printed-pattern compatibility FAQ is a further-reading lead when investigating an uncured surface against a resin print. Check the complete instructions for the particular materials before selecting a treatment.

A ratio by weight is not automatically a ratio by volume

Read what the given mass represents. These invented A/B examples teach arithmetic for two-component materials; they are not silicone or resin recipes. For A:B = a:b by mass and target total material mass M, mA = M a/(a+b) and mB = M b/(a+b). If A is already measured, mB = mA b/a.

Density connects mass and each component’s own volume: VA = mA/ρA and VB = mB/ρB. For a:b by volume, divide target total mass using weights aρA:bρB. For given A mass, VA = mA/ρA, VB = VA b/a, then mB = VBρB.

Use illustrative densities ρA = 1.2 g/mL and ρB = 0.8 g/mL with a 300 g target total:

The first row has a 4:3 component-volume ratio; the second has a 3:1 mass ratio. Those are equivalent descriptions of their respective batches. Converting 300 g to 0.3 kg keeps the same physical batch. Switching 2:1 from mass to volume does not. Individual component volumes do not establish mixed/cured volume, yield or cavity fill.

Ideal cumulative scale example: for 200 g A + 20 g B in a 35 g container, readings with no tare are 35 g empty → 235 g after A → 255 g after B. Tare once with the empty container on the scale and the readings are 0 g → 200 g → 220 g. The final reading includes A and B; B alone is 20 g. Container mass changes the gross reading, not the material ratio. This comparison does not re-tare after A.

Weigh with a scale suitable for the actual quantities and follow the product’s specified ratio basis, measurement and mixing procedure. The arithmetic assumes no material loss; it predicts no cure, exotherm, working time, compatibility or actual mixed/cured volume. The actual product’s TDS/SDS remain authoritative.

Document pattern preparation, pouring and cure as separate operations

  1. Prepare the pattern and box. Inspect the print, complete the qualified surface treatment, secure its flat back to the box base, and check that the box contains the liquid. Set wall and backing thickness from the mold material and geometry requirements.
  2. Measure and mix. Record actual A and B quantities, the ratio basis and the start time. Follow the product's mixing instructions, including its directions for reaching material at the container walls and bottom.
  3. Reduce trapped air. Follow the product's bubble-removal and pouring instructions. A controlled pour into a low point can let the material rise around the pattern instead of repeatedly trapping air over its details. Vacuum degassing is product- and equipment-dependent; do not assume every silicone requires it or has enough working time for it.
  4. Cure and remove the pattern. Record temperature, elapsed time and the specified demolding condition. Inspect the mold surface and details after removal before using the cavity.
  5. Make a first casting. Follow the casting material's own mixing, release and cure instructions. Its ratio and timing need not match the silicone's. Inspect the first piece before repeating the batch.

Use [[operation-method-sheets]] to give each operation equipment, settings, timing and a pass/fail check. Record what actually happened, including a changed pour method or a delayed demold, rather than copying the planned values into the result column.

Inspect each casting for voids, dimensions and consistency

A void is an unintended empty region. Inspect visible faces, edges and small details for pits, missing material and incomplete filling. A good exterior does not prove that the interior has no voids; choose further inspection according to the part's function.
