Choose the shape-making route before comparing production cost

Metal parts can begin as liquid poured into a mold, solid stock plastically deformed into a new shape, or powder pressed into a compact. Plastic deformation means a permanent shape change after the force is removed. Each route limits the shapes it can make and the tools it needs. A die is a tool that controls shape; a mold contains material while it takes shape.

These shape-making routes sit under Forming in the classification used by [[manufacturing-process-categories]]. In metalworking references, forming often means solid-metal deformation specifically, with casting listed separately. Cutting is Separating; joining is Fabricating; property-changing treatment is Conditioning; surface protection or appearance is Finishing. Metals are one of the four families in [[material-families]], alongside Plastics, Wood & Composites, and Ceramics & Glass. Metal properties and heat treatment are separate topics.

  • Identify the alloy, overall shape, holes and thin regions.
  • State the quantity and whether the design will change.
  • Check tool access and how the part will leave the mold or die.
  • Compare tooling cost, repeatability and production rate.
  • Allow for trimming, machining, joining and finishing afterward.

Casting makes a shape by solidifying metal inside a mold

Three casting routes and their trade-offs
RouteHow it worksTypical products and cost trade-off
Sand castingA pattern makes a cavity in bonded sand. Remove the pattern, assemble the mold, pour metal, then break away the mold after solidification.Pump housings and large machine bases. Relatively flexible tooling can suit small batches; making a new mold for every part adds work.
Investment castingA disposable pattern, often wax, receives a ceramic shell. Remove the pattern and fire the shell before pouring metal. Break away the shell afterward.Detailed brackets, valve parts and turbine components. Fine detail can reduce later machining, but pattern and shell preparation add time and cost.
Pressure die castingInject molten metal into a reusable metal die, let it solidify, then open the die and eject the part.Repeated aluminum or zinc housings. Expensive dies and equipment can pay off over long runs; alloy, size and geometry must suit the process.

There is no universal quantity at which die casting becomes cheapest. Obtain estimates for the actual geometry, alloy, tool life and required secondary operations.

A casting needs room to release, shrink and feed

[[img:materials-and-processes/metal-processes/sand-mold.svg|Schematic sand mold with the cope above the drag, a sprue and runner feeding the cavity, a riser above it and a supported core forming an internal opening.]]

The cope is the upper mold half; the drag is the lower half. The sprue leads downward from the pouring opening, and a runner and gate lead into the cavity. A riser supplies liquid metal as the casting solidifies and contracts. A core occupies space that must remain hollow.

Draft is a slight taper that helps a pattern or casting leave its tooling. A shrinkage allowance changes tooling dimensions to account for contraction. Neither is a universal number: alloy, geometry and process matter. Critical faces or holes may need machining afterward.

Why a larger pattern does not replace a riser

Pattern allowance addresses final size. Feeding addresses a different problem: a solidifying region may need more liquid metal to avoid an internal shrinkage void. A riser must remain able to feed that region. The diagram identifies parts of a mold, not a production gating design.

SFSA explains riser feeding and shrinkage voids.

Rolling squeezes, forging compresses and extrusion pushes through a die

[[img:materials-and-processes/metal-processes/bulk-forming.svg|Side-by-side sketches: rolling reduces stock thickness between rolls; forging compresses stock between dies; extrusion pushes a billet through an opening into a long profile.]]

Solid-metal forming routes
RouteShape and examplesTooling and quantity
RollingRotating rolls reduce thickness or produce a section. Sheet, plate and structural shapes are common products.Large equipment investment suits substantial throughput; a designer can often buy standard rolled stock.
Open-die forgingDies compress a solid workpiece without enclosing its whole outline. Examples include large shafts and rings.Relatively simple dies accommodate varied sizes; repeated positioning and later machining may be needed.
Closed-die forgingShaped die cavities guide metal flow into repeated parts such as connecting rods and wrenches.Dedicated tooling favors repeated production. Excess metal may form flash that is trimmed away.
ExtrusionA ram pushes a billet through a die opening. Long channels, rails and heat-sink profiles have a substantially constant cross-section.A dedicated profile die can serve many lengths. Cut ends, cross holes and local pockets require later operations.
Wire drawingPull wire through a reducing die to make it thinner and longer. This is different from pushing stock through an extrusion die.Die sets and handling equipment suit repeated lengths; several reductions may be needed.

Hot working occurs at temperatures and deformation rates that allow recrystallization, the formation of new grains, during deformation. Cold working occurs where that process does not remove the work hardening as deformation proceeds. The boundary depends on the metal and processing conditions; it is not one temperature for every alloy. Hot rolling permits large shape changes, while cold rolling commonly improves dimensional control and surface quality and can increase strength through work hardening.

Cold forming does not guarantee an unchanged dimension

Cold forming generally requires greater force than comparable hot forming. It avoids a separate hot-working step, but that does not prove every production cycle is faster. Springback, residual stress and later processing can change dimensions. Forging can also be hot, warm or cold; heat is not part of its definition.

Sheet forming changes shape; blanking and shearing separate material

Bending changes the angle of sheet, as in a bracket or enclosure. A press brake uses a punch and die to make bends; adaptable tooling suits prototypes and short runs. Roll forming progressively bends strip through roll stations into a long profile, such as a channel. Its dedicated roll tooling favors repeated lengths. It is different from rolling sheet thinner.

Deep drawing pulls sheet from a flat blank into a die cavity to make a cup or shell. Tooling must control material flow to limit tearing and wrinkling. Dedicated tools suit repeat production. This is different from pulling wire through a reducing die.

Stamping names a press-based production route that can combine several operations. Bending and drawing are Forming; shearing and blanking are Separating. In blanking, the cut-out piece is the wanted blank; in punching a hole, the removed slug is usually scrap. A stamping line can therefore perform more than one process category.

Bend allowance accounts for the material length within a bend when developing a flat pattern. The sheet stretches on the outside and compresses on the inside. Use a validated bend table or supplier data for the alloy, thickness, radius and tooling; adding outside leg dimensions alone does not give the correct blank length.

Powder metallurgy presses particles, then bonds them by sintering

In conventional press-and-sinter powder metallurgy, powder fills a die and is compressed into a green compact: a shaped part that is not yet fully bonded. Heating then bonds the particles during sintering, generally without melting the main metal throughout. Some systems include a liquid phase.

Small gears and porous bearings are typical products. Dedicated dies and presses favor repeat quantities and can produce a near-net shape, close to the finished dimensions. Tool access, ejection and density variation limit geometry; critical features may still require sizing or machining.

Porosity can be useful or unwanted

Oil impregnation fills connected pores in a bearing so it can retain lubricant. Porite describes this use of porous sintered metal. That does not mean every powder-metal part is porous by design or that every part needs impregnation.
