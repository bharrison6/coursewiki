Engineered wood changes the arrangement of the wood

Engineered products combine wood pieces with binders to make sheets or members that a single log could not supply as consistently. They belong to Wood & Composites: the wood supplies much of the structure, while the binder holds the selected arrangement together.

Engineering redistributes natural variability; it does not make all products equally strong, waterproof, or free of manufacturing defects. Select the product grade, thickness, orientation, exposure suitability, and fastening details for the job.

Plywood cross-laminates veneers to restrain movement

A veneer is a thin sheet of wood. In plywood, adjacent veneer layers commonly have grain at right angles. Each layer restrains the cross-grain movement of neighboring layers, making the panel more stable in its plane than a comparable wide solid-wood board.

Conventional plywood commonly has an odd number of plies in a balanced layup, with matching face-grain directions. Odd ply count is not a universal rule for every panel construction. The grain direction of the faces still matters to panel stiffness and strength; cross-lamination does not mean equal performance in every direction.

Plywood serves cabinet boxes, furniture panels, sheathing, and subfloors when the appropriate product is selected. Face appearance, core voids, bond durability, and panel rating vary. A decorative face veneer does not establish a structural rating.

Glulam and LVL put wood along the member

Glulam bonds lumber laminations with their grain generally parallel to the member's length. It is used for structural beams and columns, including large members and curved forms.

LVL (laminated veneer lumber) bonds thin veneers with grain predominantly along the member. It is part of the structural composite lumber group and is commonly used for beams and headers over openings. It is not simply thick plywood: its layup favors lengthwise performance.

Use the manufacturer's design information for a particular structural product. Product names alone do not give allowable loads, spans, or permission to cut holes. Lamination and bending methods belong to the later wood forming and fabricating topics.
