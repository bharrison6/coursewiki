Choose a joint around its load, access and need for disassembly

Joining parts is Fabricating. First identify the metals, thicknesses, loading, service environment and whether the assembly must come apart. Then check access for the joining equipment and for inspection.

A welded frame can avoid a large casting tool, but fit-up, fixtures and inspection still cost money. A bolt-on cover allows service access, but needs clearance for the fasteners and tools. See [[fasteners-and-galvanic-corrosion]] for mechanical assembly.

This is a process-selection overview. For safety requirements, use [[ppe]], [[fire-safety]], [[chemicals-and-sds]] and [[equipment-operating-procedure]].

Welding joins the base metal; melting is not required in every process

Fusion welding melts base metal at the joint, with or without added filler metal. The pool solidifies to form the joint. The common arc, oxy-fuel and resistance spot processes below are fusion processes.

Solid-state welding forms a bond without bulk melting of the base metals. Friction welding is one example. A solid-state process can still heat and alter nearby metal; “not melted” does not mean “unchanged.”

The heat-affected zone is nearby base metal whose structure or properties change because of the thermal cycle, without itself becoming weld metal. Selecting a process includes checking those changes and distortion, not merely whether a bead looks smooth.

Why MIG is a familiar name but GMAW is the broader term

MIG refers to metal inert gas welding. GMAW also covers processes using active shielding gases or mixtures, often called MAG. Gas and filler selection depend on the alloy and process; the familiar shop name is not a complete welding specification.

Practice choosing a joining method

Brazing and soldering melt filler while the base parts stay solid

In brazing and soldering, a molten filler wets the solid base parts. In a suitable close-fitting joint, capillary action draws filler into the gap. The usual dividing temperature is 450°C: the American Welding Society describes brazing fillers as having a liquidus above 450°C and soldering fillers below it. Liquidus is the temperature above which the filler is fully liquid. This distinction concerns the filler, not the flame temperature or the machine's setting. In both processes the base parts remain solid. Neither is simply a cooler version of fusion welding.

Brazing can join suitable dissimilar metals or attach a carbide tip to a steel tool body. Soldering is common in electrical connections. Choose filler and joint design for compatibility, temperature and loads; a soldered electrical connection is not automatically suitable for a structural load.

Joint clearance, surface condition and heating method matter. Brazing can be manual or performed in production equipment such as a furnace. Mechanization changes throughput and tooling costs; the process name alone does not identify the cheapest route.

Sources for the filler-temperature distinction and capillary joint

The AWS Brazing and Soldering Manufacturers Committee defines the liquidus distinction and explains wetting and applications. TWI's vacuum-brazing overview describes filler flow by capillary action while the parent materials stay below their solidus, the temperature at which melting begins.

Specify the required joint, not just the name of the machine

  • Identify both base materials, thicknesses and any coatings.
  • Describe the joint location, overlap or preparation and equipment access.
  • State load, leak-tightness and appearance requirements.
  • Allow for distortion, inspection and any later machining.
  • Coordinate the finish and any assembly sequence constraints.

A drawing and its referenced specification communicate the required weld and acceptance criteria. A process abbreviation alone does not specify weld size, extent or quality. Likewise, a visually neat joint does not establish internal soundness. Heat-treatment selection is a separate topic.
