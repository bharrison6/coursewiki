Wood belongs to the Wood & Composites family

Wood is a cellular material whose properties depend on direction. Long cells run mainly along the trunk; their arrangement helps explain why a board carries loads and splits differently along and across its grain.

In the material-family model, wood belongs to Wood & Composites. Engineered wood products combine veneers, strands, particles, or fibers with binders. These products are why composites share this family. See [[engineered-wood-products]] for the product choices; the material families and manufacturing process categories are foundation topics.

What makes the cell walls work together?

Cellulose reinforces the cell wall, while lignin and hemicelluloses help form its surrounding structure. This is a useful natural-composite analogy, but a tree is more complex than fibers suspended in glue. Cell arrangement, moisture, knots, and grain direction all affect a real board.

Read a trunk from bark to heartwood

Bark
Outer bark protects the tree. Inner bark carries sugars made by the leaves.
Cambium
A thin growing layer produces new wood toward the inside and new inner bark toward the outside.
Sapwood
The outer wood conducts water and contains living storage cells. Many of its conducting cells are already dead at maturity.
Heartwood
Older inner wood no longer conducts water. Deposits can change its color and decay resistance; heartwood is not automatically stronger or decay-resistant in every species.
Pith and rays
The pith marks the central stem tissue. Rays run radially across the wood and contribute to storage and transport across the trunk.

A growth ring records a growth cycle. In many temperate trees, earlywood develops earlier in the season and latewood later. Their cells differ in size and wall thickness, creating visible bands. Ring contrast varies by species; ring width alone is not a strength grade.

Hardwood and softwood describe the tree, not its hardness

Hardwoods come from angiosperm trees; softwoods come from gymnosperm trees, commonly conifers. Leaf shedding is not the defining test: some hardwood trees are evergreen, and some conifers shed their needles.

Balsa is a very soft hardwood. Southern yellow pine is a softwood but can be much harder than balsa. Ask about the species and the property you need, rather than treating the category name as a test result.

How the cell structure differs

Most commercial hardwoods have vessels, visible as pores in end grain, along with supporting fibers. Softwoods rely mainly on long cells called tracheids for support and water transport. These differences influence appearance and processing, but do not make every member of either group behave alike.
