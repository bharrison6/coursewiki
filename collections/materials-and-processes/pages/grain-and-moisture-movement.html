Follow the grain through the face, edge, and end

Grain describes the direction of wood cells. Figure is the visible pattern created by growth structure and the way the wood is cut. A board's broad surface is its face, its narrow long surface is its edge, and its cut end exposes end grain. Face and edge grain usually run generally along the board; they are not separate botanical structures.

Wood is generally stronger in tension along the grain than across it. A short projection with grain running across its narrow base can split off even if the rest of the part is thick. Knots divert nearby grain, and sloping or interlocked grain makes a board less predictable.

During machining, a cutting edge can lift fibers ahead of the cut and tear them out. Grain direction helps predict that result, but does not replace the machine's operating procedure. See [[machine-hazards]] and [[equipment-operating-procedure]] for safety; wood separating and finishing are later topics.

Sawing orientation changes figure and movement

Read the growth rings on the end of a board. Plain-sawn boards have rings more nearly parallel to the broad face and often show cathedral-shaped face figure. Quarter-sawn boards have rings more nearly perpendicular to the face; their width follows the radial direction more closely. Rift-sawn boards have an intermediate, oblique ring angle and commonly show straight face grain.

Quarter-sawn oak can expose conspicuous ray fleck. Rift-sawn oak is often selected for straight grain with less prominent fleck. Trade descriptions and angle boundaries vary, so inspect the end grain as well as the sales label. The diagram shows selected orientations, not a mill's complete cutting plan.

Moisture content uses oven-dry wood mass as its reference

MC (%) = (wet mass − oven-dry mass) / oven-dry mass × 100. A sample weighing 150 g before drying and 100 g after oven drying has 50% MC. MC can exceed 100% because the reference is dry wood mass, not the original wet mass.

Water occupies cell cavities and is also held in cell walls. At the fiber saturation point, the walls are saturated while the cavities contain no free liquid water. Below this region, loss of water from the cell walls causes appreciable shrinkage. It is not one exact threshold for every species and condition.

Equilibrium moisture content (EMC) is the moisture content wood approaches in a particular surrounding humidity and temperature. A board can be dry today and absorb moisture later.

Green
Freshly cut or unseasoned; moisture varies greatly.
Air-dried
Dried by surrounding air; the achievable condition depends on the climate and storage.
Kiln-dried
Dried in a controlled chamber. This is a drying history, not a promise that the board will never move or that every kiln-dried product has the same MC.

Choose and acclimate material for its intended service environment. Use measurements to judge readiness; a fixed number of days cannot guarantee equilibrium for every thickness and starting condition.

Most movement is across the grain

For normal straight-grained wood, shrinkage is usually greatest tangentially (along growth rings), smaller radially (across rings), and much smaller longitudinally (along the cells). Swelling reverses the moisture-loss trend. Actual amounts depend on species, grain orientation, and the moisture interval.

Do not apply a green-to-oven-dry shrinkage percentage directly to a small seasonal humidity change. A movement estimate needs a species-specific coefficient, board orientation, starting MC, and expected service MC. Reaction wood and juvenile wood can also depart from ordinary longitudinal behavior.

Estimate movement below using starting dimension × coefficient × (final MC − starting MC), with both MC values within 6–14%; then use the result in “Allow solid wood to change width” below.

Wood movement estimate — both moisture contents must be 6–14%.

Change = starting dimension × coefficient × (final MC − starting MC), with MC entered as a percentage (8, not 0.08). Negative means shrinkage; positive means swelling. Use the tangential coefficient CT for the width of a flat-sawn board or when the ring orientation is unknown, and the radial coefficient CR for the width of a quarter-sawn board or the thickness of a flat-sawn one.

Worked example (the Handbook's own): a flat-sawn white fir board 9.15 in wide at 8% MC, CT = 0.00245, reaching 11% MC: 9.15 × 0.00245 × (11 − 8) = 0.067 in wider, about 9.22 in. In millimetres, 232 mm becomes about 233.7 mm.

Commercial lumber is rarely perfectly flat- or quarter-sawn, so this method tends to overestimate width change and underestimate thickness change, and the straight-line assumption underestimates movement by about 5%. Outside 6–14% MC a different method applies.

Name the distortion before deciding what to do

Defects described by shape
DefectWhat you see
CupCurvature across the width: the face resembles a shallow trough.
BowCurvature along the length, out of the broad face's plane.
CrookCurvature along the length within the face's plane; the edge wanders sideways.
TwistThe corners no longer lie in a common plane.
Check / splitA separation along the grain. A check may be confined to a surface or end; a split extends through the piece.

Unequal shrinkage, uneven moisture, and irregular grain can contribute to warp. Drying stresses can open checks and splits. A stain is a color change, not automatically proof of lost strength; decay is a different concern.

Allow solid wood to change width

A solid-wood tabletop needs room to expand and contract across its grain. Suitable slotted attachments let it move relative to a supporting frame. A panel held in grooves needs clearance rather than rigid restraint across its full width. Details depend on the joint and expected movement.

Do not assume that a finish locks moisture out. It can slow moisture exchange, but does not eliminate seasonal movement. Plywood often solves a panel-stability problem better than simply making a solid board thicker.

  • Identify the grain direction and width that can move.
  • Check moisture against the intended service environment.
  • Inspect for warp, splits, and grain interruptions before laying out parts.
  • Check that attachments allow the expected movement.
