Start with both plastics and what the joint must do

Fabricating joins pieces into a useful assembly. Name both materials, then state the load, service temperature, appearance and whether the assembly must come apart. A leak-tight seam, a removable cover and a clear display corner need different joints.

  • Identify the two resins and any coating or surface contamination.
  • State the load direction, environment and need for disassembly or sealing.
  • Check the joining product or process guidance for that material pair.
  • Make a representative trial joint and inspect it after full cure or cooling.

Solvent cement softens the surfaces; adhesive adds a bond layer

Solvent cementing uses a compatible liquid to soften or dissolve the surfaces so polymer chains can mix across the joint. As solvent leaves, the joint develops strength. It is chemical softening, not heat melting. Acrylic-to-acrylic is a common application; PVC and ABS also have specific cement systems. The cement must match the resin and fit of the joint.

Adhesives bond through a separate layer. Epoxies, cyanoacrylates and other formulations have different gap, flexibility and temperature limits. “Plastic glue” does not mean it bonds every plastic. Allow the specified cure time before loading; a joint that holds itself together may not have reached service strength.

Polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) resist many ordinary adhesives. Their low surface energy makes it difficult for an adhesive to spread into intimate contact, called wetting. Specialized adhesives or a controlled surface treatment can help; sanding alone is not a universal fix. See [[chemicals-and-sds]] for chemical handling.

Why a clean-looking bond can peel off

The bond is only as good as the material it touches. Mold-release residue, oil or an unsuitable coating can become the weak layer. Peel loads concentrate force at an edge; an overlapping joint can distribute load more effectively, although its dimensions still need validation. 3M explains low surface energy, and its bonding guidance shows why PE and PP are difficult, rather than impossible, to bond.

Welding needs compatible thermoplastics and a designed joint

Plastic welding heats the joint region so compatible thermoplastics can fuse. Cooling while the joint is held in position completes the connection. Cured thermosets do not remelt into a comparable fusion weld. Even two thermoplastics are not automatically weld-compatible.

Why heat alone does not guarantee a strong or sealed weld

Temperature, force, time and joint fit work together. Too little heating leaves an unfused interface; excessive heating can degrade the polymer. Ultrasonic joints often use a small molded ridge, an energy director, to concentrate heating. A continuous-looking bead does not prove a leak-tight seam. Leister's extrusion-welding guidance identifies matching filler and joint geometry; Dukane's Guide to Ultrasonic Plastics Assembly explains energy directors for ultrasonic welding.

Hot-melt adhesive is not a fusion weld

A hot-melt adhesive is applied molten and usually gains handling strength as it cools. The adhesive melts; the two parts do not have to melt together. Choose the formulation for both surfaces, the available assembly time and the service temperature. An ordinary hot-melt joint may soften or creep under sustained load in warmth. Fast setting is not the same as suitability for structural service.

Use mechanical joints when access and disassembly matter

A snap fit bends a feature during assembly so it catches behind another feature. It needs room to flex, rounded transitions and a resin that tolerates the required strain. Some snap fits release repeatedly; others are intentionally difficult to undo.

Thread-forming screws for plastics create their mating thread in a designed hole. Repeated removal can wear plastic threads. A threaded insert supplies a metal thread; a heat-set insert is installed by locally softening a thermoplastic around it. Hole size, surrounding wall thickness and installation conditions come from the insert supplier. A metal insert cannot rescue an undersized plastic wall.

Through-bolts and suitable washers can spread the clamping load. Excessive tightening can crack brittle plastic; sustained load can cause creep, a gradual change in shape, and reduce clamping force. Allow for movement and thermal expansion rather than trapping a sheet tightly at every hole.

Compare two cases: a clear permanent acrylic corner is a candidate for compatible solvent cement; a PP cover that must be removed regularly is a candidate for screws and a suitable thread arrangement. An adhesive that works on the first pair tells you little about the second.

Insert dimensions come from the chosen insert system

SPIROL's hole-design guidance explains how oversized holes reduce performance and undersized holes can introduce stress or cracks. Its insert design guide distinguishes heat-installed thermoplastic inserts from other installation methods.
