Condition the wood for the environment where it will be used

Conditioning changes material properties. Drying removes moisture so wood can be prepared for a particular use. It does not permanently freeze the moisture content: dried wood still exchanges moisture with surrounding air. Review the wood-as-a-material topic for the underlying grain and movement behavior.

Not every use requires dried wood, but closely fitted indoor furniture generally does. Choose a target moisture content appropriate to the expected indoor temperature and humidity, then check the actual stock before final machining and assembly. A mill's drying target and a suitable in-service range are not necessarily identical. “Kiln-dried” describes a process; it is not a measurement of what the board contains today.

For indoor furniture in most of the United States, the USDA Wood Handbook recommends 8% average moisture content, with individual pieces at 6–10%, at installation. Regional guidance differs: the dry Southwest uses a 6% average (4–9% for individual pieces), while damp, warm coastal areas use an 11% average (8–13%). Use these as starting points and match the actual service environment. See Table 13–2 and Figure 13–1, page 13–5.

Why the indoor moisture target depends on the building

The handbook's interior recommendations were developed from well-ventilated buildings without unusual moisture sources or air conditioning. Air conditioning and other building conditions can change the appropriate target. These are installation guidelines, not a universal kiln-drying specification; see Recommended Moisture Content, page 13–3.

Drying faster is not always drying better. The outside of a board may dry while the center remains wet. Suitable schedules manage this difference; elapsed time or a dry-feeling surface alone does not prove the board is ready.

Radio-frequency and microwave systems both use electromagnetic energy for dielectric heating, but the terms are not synonyms. Industrial systems control frequency, power and moisture removal; this is a process overview, not a household-microwave procedure.

Acclimate stock with airflow and repeated measurements

Acclimating means giving lumber time to approach the conditions in the intended working or service environment. There is no reliable fixed number of days for every board. Thickness, initial moisture, species and humidity all affect the time required.

  • Identify the intended service environment before choosing the moisture target.
  • Support boards flat with aligned stickers and space for airflow.
  • Check several boards and locations with a suitable moisture meter, using its species and temperature corrections where required.
  • Record repeat measurements and look for stability, not just one favorable reading.
  • Recheck after major material removal or a change of storage environment before final fitting.

Meter depth and measurement method matter: a surface reading can miss a wetter core. Keep stock in suitable conditions after drying. Joint design still needs to allow movement; see [[wood-joinery]].

Calculate moisture content using oven-dry mass as the denominator

On the oven-dry basis used for wood, MC (%) = (initial mass − oven-dry mass) / oven-dry mass × 100. Both masses must refer to the same sample and use the same units. “Initial” means the sample's mass before the moisture determination; it need not be freshly cut green wood.

For an illustrative sample with initial mass 230 g and oven-dry mass 200 g, the water removed is 230 − 200 = 30 g. Then MC = 30 / 200 × 100 = 15.0%.

Why dividing by the initial mass answers a different question

30 / 230 × 100 = 13.0% gives the water fraction of the initial sample, not wood moisture content on the oven-dry basis. Wood MC can exceed 100% when water mass exceeds dry wood mass. The calculation does not set a suitable target for furniture. An actual oven-dry determination requires an appropriate laboratory method and equipment; these exercises provide the dry mass.

Distinguish temporary softening from permanent treatment

Heat and moisture in steaming can soften wood enough for bending. The softening is conditioning; changing the shape is Forming. The part must be restrained as it dries and sets, and some springback can occur. Species, grain quality, thickness and bend radius govern success; there is no universal bending radius for all wood.

Other treatments have specific purposes. Resin impregnation introduces resin into wood; impreg is resin-treated wood, while compreg also uses compression during manufacture. Increased dimensional stability or hardness can come with changes in other properties. Decorative color is not what defines compreg.

Pressure treatment is a delivery method, not a promise of every kind of resistance. Preservative treatment and fire-retardant treatment use different systems for different purposes. Neither the word “treated” nor “pressure-treated” alone establishes water exclusion, chemical resistance or suitability for an application. Use the treatment identification and product specification.

Wood–plastic composites made by combining wood particles or fibers with a polymer are a materials topic. Irradiation is not a universal requirement for making them. Finishing preparation is covered in [[wood-finishes]]; chemical handling belongs in [[chemicals-and-sds]].
