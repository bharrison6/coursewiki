Types of Steel

Carbon, alloy, stainless and tool steels, plus the difference between a steel family and a complete material specification.

Steel is iron-based, with carbon and other elements controlling behavior

Steel is iron-based, with carbon and other elements controlling behavior

Steel contains iron and carbon, often with additional alloying elements. Both composition and processing matter. The same grade can arrive softened for machining or hardened for service.

Use four practical groups: carbon steel, alloy steel, stainless steel and tool steel. These are useful buying categories, not four mutually exclusive chemical definitions: stainless and tool steels also contain alloying elements.

Carbon steel spans easy forming through hardenable cutting edges

Carbon steel spans easy forming through hardenable cutting edges

Relative carbon levels; actual grade limits come from the specification
GroupTypical behaviorTypical uses
Low carbonGenerally readily formed and welded; limited response to ordinary through-hardening.Sheet parts, brackets and general fabrication.
Medium carbonMore potential for heat-treated strength; forming and welding require more consideration.Shafts, axles and machinery parts.
High carbonCan develop high hardness after suitable treatment; less forgiving of forming and impact.Springs and some cutting tools.

These are trends, not a strength ranking of every item on a shelf. Compare the delivery condition and required properties before substituting one grade for another.

Alloy, stainless and tool steels solve different service problems

Alloy, stainless and tool steels solve different service problems

Choose a family, then qualify a specific grade
FamilyComposition idea and purposeUses and limits
Alloy steelIntentional additions such as chromium, nickel or molybdenum adjust properties and heat-treatment response.Loaded shafts and gears. Alloy steel is not automatically corrosion resistant.
Stainless steelChromium supports a protective surface film; other elements adjust performance.Food equipment, fittings and exposed hardware. Grade, cleaning and environment still matter.
Tool steelCarbon and alloy additions support hardness, wear resistance or performance at elevated temperature.Dies, punches and cutting tools. Heat treatment must balance wear resistance against chipping.

Use Heat Treating Metals to distinguish hardening from tempering. Hardenability means the ability to harden to depth under given cooling conditions; it is not the same as a measured surface hardness.

Grade labels identify a material; they are not a heat-treatment recipe

Grade labels identify a material; they are not a heat-treatment recipe

Common labels include 1018 and 1045 for carbon steels, 4140 for alloy steel, 304 and 316 for stainless steels, and O1, A2 and D2 for tool steels. Different designation systems have different rules. Do not apply a four-digit carbon-steel decoding rule to stainless or tool-steel labels.

On an order, identify the governing material specification, grade, delivery condition, form and dimensions. A short grade name alone does not specify strength, hardness, surface finish or dimensional tolerance. Ask for the required certification when traceability matters.

Use the current governing specification or the producer's grade sheet for composition limits and treatment requirements.

Decode carbon and alloy steel numbers; read stainless and tool grades separately

Decode carbon and alloy steel numbers; read stainless and tool grades separately

In the common AISI/SAE four-digit carbon and alloy steel system, the first two digits identify the steel series: 10 is plain carbon steel and 41 is chromium-molybdenum steel. The last two digits give nominal carbon content in hundredths of a percent.

Reading the series and nominal carbon content
GradeSeriesNominal carbon
101810: plain carbon18 → 0.18%
104510: plain carbon45 → 0.45%
414041: chromium-molybdenum40 → 0.40%

304 and 316 stainless: their chemistries are similar, but 316 has an added alloying element, molybdenum. This improves resistance to chloride exposure and urban or industrial pollution compared with 304.

Tool-steel groups: O1 is an oil-hardening grade, A2 is an air-hardening grade, and D2 is a high-carbon, high-chromium grade. These labels describe different tool-steel groups; they are not carbon-percentage codes.

Sources and quoted designation meanings

The Metals Service Center Institute identifies the first digit as “family of steel,” the second as “additional elements,” and the third and fourth as “nominal carbon content”; its example is 1018 at 0.18% carbon. Total Materia's SAE-AISI introduction gives the final two digits as “the carbon content in hundredths of a percent” and lists 41XX as “Chromium-molybdenum steels.”

The International Molybdenum Association's 304/316 comparison states that “Type 316 contains a molybdenum addition” and explains the corrosion benefit. Hudson Tool Steel's A2 data sheet describes O1 as “oil-hardening,” A2 as “air-hardening,” and D2 as “high-carbon, high-chromium.” These are introductory meanings, not complete material specifications.

Cast iron is a separate iron-based material, and it can be machined

Cast iron is a separate iron-based material, and it can be machined

Cast irons generally contain more carbon than steels. Their properties depend strongly on how carbon appears in the structure. Gray iron contains graphite flakes and is useful for machine bases and housings where vibration damping matters; it is relatively brittle in tension. Ductile iron contains graphite nodules and can provide much greater ductility.

Casting creates the initial shape. Machining can then finish bores, faces and other accurate features. “Cast” and “machined” are not competing material identities. White cast iron contains substantial hard carbide and behaves differently from graphite-containing irons.
