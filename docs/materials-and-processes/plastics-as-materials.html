Plastics as Materials

Polymer chains, thermoplastics and thermosets, common plastics, and what resin identification codes actually tell you.

Plastics get their behavior from polymer chains

Plastics get their behavior from polymer chains

A polymer is a substance made of very large molecules built from repeating units. Small starting molecules called monomers react to build those molecules. Many polymers have long chains; some also have branches or chemical links between chains.

A plastic combines a polymer with ingredients that may change its color, flexibility, processing behavior or resistance to weather. The polymer name is a starting point, not a complete material specification. Two grades of the same plastic can behave differently.

Plastics is one of the four material families in the material-family foundations topic. A polymer reinforced with glass or carbon fibers is a composite; the course places composites in Wood & Composites.

Thermoplastics soften again; cured thermosets do not remelt

Thermoplastics soften again; cured thermosets do not remelt

The useful distinction is behavior on reheating
TypeWhat happensExamples and recycling
ThermoplasticHeating makes the material soft enough to reshape or flow. Cooling makes it firm again. Reheating can repeat this change, within the material's processing limits.PE, PP, PVC, PLA, ABS, PETG, acrylic, polycarbonate and nylon. Clean, compatible material can often be remelted for recycling; contamination, degradation and available collection systems limit actual recovery.
ThermosetCuring forms a network of chemical links between molecules. The cured material cannot be melted back into a moldable liquid. Excessive heating degrades it.Cured epoxy and unsaturated polyester resin. They cannot be recycled by ordinary melt-and-remold processing; specialized recovery methods may exist.

Curing is a chemical change; cooling is a temperature change. A two-part epoxy becoming solid is not simply a hot plastic cooling down.

Softening, melting and heat resistance are different questions

Some thermoplastics soften over a range rather than having one sharp melting temperature. A part can lose stiffness and fail under load well before it flows. Thermosets also have service-temperature limits: being unable to remelt does not make them heatproof. Use the grade's data sheet for the actual load and temperature.

The British Plastics Federation's thermoplastics reference explains reheating behavior and shows how varied the materials are.

Light weight and corrosion resistance come with limits

Light weight and corrosion resistance come with limits

  • Light weight: many unfilled plastics have much lower density than metals. Low weight does not automatically mean high stiffness.
  • Chemical resistance: plastics do not rust like steel, but a chemical may soften, swell or crack a particular plastic. Check the material, chemical, temperature and exposure time together.
  • Low softening or melting temperatures: many common thermoplastics are easy to form with heat but unsuitable for hot, loaded service.
  • Creep: slow deformation under a sustained load. A plastic shelf can keep sagging even when its load stays constant.
  • UV degradation: sunlight can discolor or embrittle susceptible grades. Outdoor service calls for a suitable resin and stabilization, not just a preferred color.
  • Electrical insulation: many unfilled plastics resist current flow. Conductive fillers and special formulations can change that behavior.
Why a short strength test does not settle a long service life

Temperature, time, moisture and chemicals can change both dimensions and load-carrying ability. A part that survives a brief pull may still creep over months. Curbell's dimensional-stability guide connects these effects to material selection.

Match common thermoplastics to their useful properties

Match common thermoplastics to their useful properties

These are typical uses and tradeoffs, not design ratings for every grade. All materials in this table are thermoplastics.

Common plastics: a first selection, followed by a grade data sheet
MaterialTypical uses and strengthsWatch for
PLA — polylactic acidModels, packaging and filament; stiff, often made from renewable feedstocks.Ordinary grades have limited heat resistance and can be brittle. Bio-based does not mean an item belongs in home compost or a recycling bin.
ABS — acrylonitrile butadiene styreneHousings and toys; a useful balance of toughness and rigidity.Ordinary grades weather poorly outdoors; some solvents attack it.

PC — polycarbonateImpact-resistant glazing and housings; transparent and tough.Scratching and chemical attack; select an appropriate UV-resistant grade for outdoor exposure.

Material references for checking a particular choice

Compare BPF's thermoplastic families with supplier guidance for acrylic, polycarbonate and PETG sheet. Their grade differences matter more than a single generic strength number.

Epoxy and polyester resin cure into thermosets

Epoxy and polyester resin cure into thermosets

Two common thermosetting resin systems
ResinTypical uses and strengthsLimitations
EpoxyAdhesives, cast parts and composite matrices; good adhesion and generally low cure shrinkage.Mixing and cure conditions affect properties. Often costs more than general-purpose polyester resin; unprotected epoxy can degrade in sunlight.
Unsaturated polyester resinFiberglass boat shells and panels; economical for many composite parts.Typically greater cure shrinkage and lower adhesive performance than epoxy. Cured resin cannot be remelted.

“Polyester” alone is ambiguous. PET packaging is a thermoplastic polyester. The unsaturated polyester resin used in a conventional fiberglass layup cures into a thermoset. See the hand-layup card in Plastic Forming Processes.

Where the resin comparison comes from

The American Composites Manufacturers Association guide, Appendix D, compares composite resin systems. The resin holds and transfers load between the reinforcing fibers; the fibers and their orientation strongly affect the resulting composite.

Resin codes identify plastic; they do not promise recycling

Resin codes identify plastic; they do not promise recycling

The number on a container is a resin identification code (RIC). It can help sorting, but it does not say whether the local recycling program accepts that product.

The seven code-to-resin matches, checked against current public guidance
CodePlastic
1 — PET / PETEPolyethylene terephthalate
2 — HDPEHigh-density polyethylene
3 — PVCPolyvinyl chloride (vinyl)
4 — LDPELow-density polyethylene
5 — PPPolypropylene
6 — PSPolystyrene
7 — OTHERResins outside the first six categories; also some combinations of resins. It is not one particular plastic.

For example, code 5 identifies PP; it does not prove that a PP lid is accepted with PP containers. Use the collector's instructions for that item. Do not assign PETG to code 1 just because its name contains PET.

Verified code sources and the standard's exact distinction

ASTM D7611/D7611M-26, section 4.2, states: Resin Identification Codes are not “recycle codes.” Read the current ASTM significance-and-use statement.

The number-to-material matches are published in the City of Sioux Falls RIC guide. The PET Resin Association consumer guide supplies the familiar label terminology. Checked October 27, 2026. This is an identification reference, not a specification for manufacturing compliant labels.
