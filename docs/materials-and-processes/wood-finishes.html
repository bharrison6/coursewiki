Preparing and Finishing Wood

Prepare a uniform surface, distinguish color from protection and choose a finish for appearance, wear and repair.

Remove each grit's scratches before moving to a finer abrasive; Practice

Remove each grit's scratches before moving to a finer abrasive

Start with the least coarse abrasive that removes the actual defects efficiently. Higher grit numbers generally mean finer particles within the same grading system. A possible bare-wood sequence is 80 → 120 → 150 → 180, but sound, smooth stock may start later. Stop at the grit recommended for the chosen stain or finish; 220 is not a universal destination.

Each step removes the previous step's scratches. Jumping straight from a coarse grit to a very fine grit leaves deep scratches that the fine abrasive removes slowly. Inspect under light across the surface, remove dust between steps and replace clogged abrasive. Finish hand sanding along the grain where practical; machine sanding also requires control of pressure and motion.

Do not use prolonged sanding to hide a poorly fitted joint. It can round edges, dish a surface or sand through a thin veneer. Remove glue residue before coloring; a smooth glue patch may still reject stain.

Practice grit order

Treat raised fibers, defects and open pores as different problems

Treat raised fibers, defects and open pores as different problems

Water can raise loose fibers and make a previously smooth surface feel rough. When the finishing system calls for it, lightly dampen bare wood, let it dry, then sand the raised fibers lightly with the final preparation grit. This grain raising step reduces roughness under later water-based coats. Heavy sanding can expose fresh fibers and restart the problem.

Preparation products do different jobs
Product or stepPurposeCheck before using it
Defect filler or puttyFill a small hole or surface defect.Some products harden and sand; others remain softer and belong after finishing. Color and stain uptake may differ from wood. Sawdust mixed with glue does not reliably stain like the surrounding wood.
Grain/pore fillerFill open pores for a level surface, especially under a glossy film.Choose compatible filler and test whether it goes before or after coloring. Filling pores is optional when visible texture is wanted.
Pre-stain conditionerReduce uneven stain absorption on blotch-prone wood.It can lighten or alter the result; it does not guarantee uniform color. Follow the compatible stain system and its timing.
Sanding sealerProvide a readily sanded initial coating in a compatible system.It is not required under every finish. The wrong sealer can reduce adhesion or durability.

Bleaching changes wood color chemically; it is not the same as staining. Product chemistry determines the sequence, drying and any required follow-up preparation. Do not use a universal “bleach immediately after final sanding” rule. Consult Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets and Personal Protective Equipment for chemical handling and protection.

Use stain or dye for color, then decide what protection is needed

Use stain or dye for color, then decide what protection is needed

Pigment stain contains fine solid particles that lodge in pores and surface texture. It can emphasize grain and partly obscure it. Dye dissolves in its carrier and can give transparent color; it can still absorb unevenly. Some commercial stains contain both pigment and dye. A gel stain has a thicker consistency and often gives more surface color control on vertical or blotch-prone work.

Coloring alone is not normally the protective finish. Follow the product's application and wipe-off interval: leaving ordinary wiping stain on indefinitely is not a dependable way to make it darker. Test the complete stain-and-topcoat sequence because the clear coat changes the appearance too.

FinishAppearance and useTradeoff
Drying oils, such as linseed or tung oilUsually applied thinly for a low-build, grain-enhancing surface.Easy to renew in many applications, but thin oil treatments usually give less wear and spill protection than a suitable film. Drying oil cures; mineral oil does not cure the same way.
ShellacA resin coating available in different colors; useful for furniture and compatible sealing applications.Repairable, but vulnerable to alcohol and heat. Check wax content and compatibility before applying another finish over it.
LacquerA family of film coatings often used for furniture and sprayed production work.Many dry quickly; conventional solvent lacquers can be repaired by re-dissolving the surface. Catalyzed products cure differently and are not equally repairable.
Varnish/polyurethaneFilm-forming resin systems often selected for wear and spill resistance.Repair and recoating need preparation. Modern varnishes commonly use synthetic resins. Exterior protection requires an exterior formulation; “varnish” alone does not promise UV resistance.
Water-based clear coatingsOften chosen to retain a lighter color; may use acrylic, polyurethane or blends.Water-based identifies the carrier, not a separate guarantee of durability. Grain raising and intercoat adhesion need attention. Color change depends on both coating and wood.
Wax or paintWax offers a low-build, easily refreshed surface. Paint supplies opaque color in a film.Wax alone offers limited protection. Paint hides grain and needs compatible preparation, primer where specified, and a suitable topcoat system.

Names such as “Danish oil” or “tung oil finish” may describe blends rather than pure oil. Read the product description. No ordinary finish makes wood completely moisture-proof or eliminates the movement allowance in Wood Joinery and Fasteners.

Match application method and recoat timing to the product

Match application method and recoat timing to the product

Brushing suits many small assemblies; use a compatible brush and maintain a wet edge without repeatedly working a coating that has begun to set. Wiping gives thin, controllable applications when the product is designed for it. Spraying can coat complex shapes evenly but needs suitable equipment, setup and the safety controls in Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets.

Roller coating suits suitable products on broad surfaces. Industrial curtain coating passes panels through a flowing sheet of finish; dip coating immerses parts. Electrostatic spraying requires a suitable coating and process setup to attract charged droplets to the work; ordinary dry wood does not automatically provide that setup.

Build the recommended coats and observe the recoat window. Sand between coats only as directed, with the specified abrasive and after sufficient drying. A fixed 320-grit rule does not fit every coating. Dry-to-touch is not full cure; allow the specified cure before service or final rubbing to adjust sheen.

Choose with a sample board and the expected wear in mind; Checklist; Practice

Choose with a sample board and the expected wear in mind

A frequently wiped surface needs a finish rated for that use; a lightly handled decorative object may favor appearance and easy renewal. Compare resistance to water, cleaning products, abrasion and heat, then consider color, sheen, repairability and available application equipment. A durable film is useful only if it adheres and cures properly.

  • Prepare a sample of the same wood with the intended sanding sequence.
  • Test any conditioner, filler and colorant together with the topcoat.
  • Record products, grits, application method and drying intervals.
  • Inspect color, texture and adhesion after the specified cure.
  • Choose the complete system before coating the finished assembly.
Further product-specific preparation guidance

General Finishes' wood preparation guide is a starting point for that manufacturer's systems. Use the exact product's current instructions to choose the final grit and recoat schedule.
