From Current, Voltage, and Resistance

1. Current in a control circuit: A resistor in a control circuit is 470 Ω. What current flows when 24 V is applied across it? Give the answer in milliamperes.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. Pick the form with the two quantities you have: I = V/R = 24 V ÷ 470 Ω = 0.051064 A.

Then move the prefix:

0.051064 A × 1,000 mA1 A = 51.06 mA

The current is 51.1 mA. Check the units rather than trusting the arithmetic: an ohm is a volt per ampere, so volts divided by ohms leaves amperes. And mind the case — mA is a milliampere, while MA would be a megaampere, a billion times larger.

From Current, Voltage, and Resistance

2. Voltage drop along a cord: A portable tool draws 12 A through an extension cord. Both of the cord’s conductors are in the current path — out to the tool and back — and together they add 0.32 Ω to the circuit. What is the voltage drop across the cord?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. V = IR = 12 A × 0.32 Ω = 3.84 V.

The drop is 3.84 V, and that is 3.84 V that never reaches the tool: the supply is divided between the cord and the load, and the cord takes its share first.

Read the resistance figure carefully before using it. A cord carries the current out on one conductor and back on the other, so what belongs in this calculation is the resistance of the whole path. A table that gives resistance per unit length gives it for a single conductor; use that figure alone for a cord and the answer comes out about half of what a meter across the ends would show.

From Current, Voltage, and Resistance

3. Resistance from a nameplate: A resistive heating element is rated 1,500 W at 120 V. What resistance does that rating imply?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. The two quantities given are power and voltage, so use R = V2/P.
  2. R = (120 V)2 ÷ 1,500 W = 14,400 ÷ 1,500 = 9.6 Ω.

The rating implies 9.6 Ω. Two steps get to the same place if you prefer: I = P/V = 12.5 A, then R = V/I = 120 ÷ 12.5 = 9.6 Ω.

This is the resistance at the rated operating point. A metal heating element is not the same resistance cold as it is hot, so a meter reading on an unpowered element will not match the nameplate figure exactly.

From Current, Voltage, and Resistance

4. Heat lost in a cable run: A cable run carries 25 A and has a total resistance of 0.18 Ω. How much power is lost as heat in it?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. P = I2R = (25 A)2 × 0.18 Ω = 625 × 0.18 = 112.5 W.

The run loses 112.5 W as heat, spread along its whole length.

Notice the squared current. Halve the load to 12.5 A and the loss does not halve, it quarters, to 28.1 W. That is why a drop that is harmless on a light load becomes a real problem on a heavy one, and why the length and size of a conductor matter more the more current the load draws.

From Angular Motion

1. Index table in radians: A rotary index table advances 40° between one station and the next. How many radians is one advance?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

Put degrees on the bottom so they cancel:

40° × 0.01745329 rad1 ° = 0.6981317… rad

or 0.6981 rad. The same thing exactly: 40° × π/180° = 2π/9 rad. A quick check on the size of the answer — one radian is about 57.3°, so 40° should come out a little under 1 rad, and it does.

From Angular Motion

3. Spindle slowing down: A spindle is running at 1,800 r/min. The drive brings it down to 600 r/min in 4.0 s. What is its average angular acceleration in rad/s2, taking the running direction as positive?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

Only the change in speed matters, so take it first:

Δω = 600 r/min − 1,800 r/min = −1,200 r/min

−1,200 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s1 r/min = −125.6637 rad/s

Then α = Δω / Δt = −125.6637 rad/s ÷ 4.0 s = −31.4159 rad/s2, or −31.42 rad/s². The sign is the answer as much as the number is: it says the angular acceleration points opposite the direction of rotation, which is what slowing down means. This is the average over the 4.0 s; a drive that ramps its braking has a changing instantaneous value inside the same interval.

From Angular Motion

4. Back to the nameplate: A calculation has produced an angular velocity of 52 rad/s, and the machine has to be set in r/min. What speed is that?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

Turn the factor over so that rad/s cancels:

52 rad/s × 9.549297 r/min1 rad/s = 496.5634… r/min

or 497 r/min. Carry the factor to at least five figures — the exact value is 60/2π = 9.5492966…, and rounding it to 9.54 puts the answer about 0.1% low, which is a real error on a speed that has to be dialled in.

From Rotation and Linear Speed

5. Belt speed from a drive roller: A conveyor drive roller is 160 mm in diameter and turns at 60 r/min. What is the belt speed in m/s?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. Halve the diameter: r = 80 mm = 0.080 m.
  2. Convert the speed: 60 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s per r/min = 6.283185 rad/s.
  3. v = ωr = 6.283185 rad/s × 0.080 m = 0.5026548 m/s.

The belt runs at 0.503 m/s. Worth a sanity check: 60 r/min is one turn per second, and one turn of a 160 mm roller lays out π × 0.160 m = 0.5027 m of belt — the same number, reached without the formula. The answer assumes the belt does not slip on the roller.

From Rotation and Linear Speed

6. Setting the roller speed: Material has to feed past a station at 1.25 m/s. The drive roller is 250 mm in diameter. What speed must the roller be set to, in r/min?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. r = 125 mm = 0.125 m.
  2. Rearrange: ω = v/r = 1.25 m/s ÷ 0.125 m = 10 rad/s.
  3. The machine is set in r/min, so convert back: 10 rad/s × 9.549297 r/min per rad/s = 95.49297 r/min.

Set the roller to 95.5 r/min. Two chances to go wrong here, and both are worth a deliberate check: using 0.250 m instead of the radius halves the answer, and stopping at step 2 leaves it in rad/s, which is not what a drive is dialled in.

From Rotation and Linear Speed

7. Wheel to road speed: A wheel 24 in in diameter turns at 6.0 rad/s. How fast is the vehicle travelling, in mi/h?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. r = 12 in, which is exactly 1 ft, or 12 in × 0.0254 m/in = 0.3048 m.
  2. v = ωr = 6.0 rad/s × 0.3048 m = 1.8288 m/s.

Then convert to mi/h:

1.8288 m/s × 1 mi/h0.44704 m/s = 4.0909… mi/h

or 4.09 mi/h. Both factors used here are exact by definition: 1 in = 0.0254 m and 1 mi/h = 0.44704 m/s.

From Rotation and Linear Speed

8. Wheel angular acceleration: A vehicle accelerates at 3.2 m/s2 on wheels 0.62 m in diameter. What is the angular acceleration of one wheel, in rad/s2?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. r = 0.31 m.
  2. Rearrange a = αr into α = a/r = 3.2 m/s2 ÷ 0.31 m = 10.3226 rad/s2.

The answer is 10.3 rad/s². Check the unit as well as the number: dividing m/s2 by m leaves 1/s2, and because the radian is a length over a length, rad/s2 is that same thing named for what it describes. The result assumes the wheel rolls without slipping.

From Torque

9. Torque at a pulley: A belt applies 240 N of tangential force to a pulley 300 mm in diameter. What torque does that put on the shaft, in N·m?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. The pulley is given by diameter, so halve it: r = 150 mm = 0.150 m.
  2. τ = rF = 0.150 m × 240 N = 36 N·m.

The answer is 36 N·m — lower-case m, because N·M would read as newton times mega. Using the 300 mm diameter instead of the radius gives 72 N·m, exactly twice too much.

From Torque

10. Reading a torque rating: A motor is rated 42 lbf·ft. What is that rating in N·m?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

42 lbf·ft × 1.355818 N·m1 lbf·ft = 56.944… N·m

The rating is 56.9 N·m. Read the given unit carefully before reaching for the factor: lbf·ft is a torque and converts to the newton metre, while ft·lbf — the same two words the other way round — is an energy and converts to the joule. The numerical factor happens to be the same 1.355818 either way, so the arithmetic will not warn you if you have read the wrong quantity.

From Torque

11. Force from a torque: A winch drum is 400 mm in diameter and its drive supplies 95 N·m. What tangential force does that produce at the drum surface?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. r = 200 mm = 0.200 m.
  2. Rearrange τ = rF into F = τ/r = 95 N·m ÷ 0.200 m = 475 N.

The drive produces 475 N at the drum surface. Notice what the radius is doing: wind more cable onto the drum, the effective radius grows, and the same torque pulls with less force. That single rearrangement is the whole story of a longer wrench, a bigger steering wheel, and a larger pulley.

From Work and Energy

1. Steady lift in SI: A hoist raises a 320 kg load 4.5 m at a steady speed. How much work is done against gravity? Use standard gravity, g0 = 9.80665 m/s2.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. The load is a mass, so turn it into the force needed to lift it: F = mg = 320 kg × 9.80665 m/s2 = 3,138.128 N.
  2. W = Fs = 3,138.128 N × 4.5 m = 14,121.576 J.

The work done is 14.1 kJ. Two assumptions are doing real work in that answer: the lift is steady, so no extra force goes into accelerating the load, and the force acts along the direction of travel for the whole 4.5 m.

From Work and Energy

2. A lift in foot pounds-force: How much work is required to lift a 750 lb load 22 ft? Give the answer in joules.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. The force needed to lift steadily is the load’s weight, 750 lbf, acting along the 22 ft of travel: W = 750 lbf × 22 ft = 16,500 ft·lbf.

Then convert:

16,500 ft·lbf × 1.355818 J1 ft·lbf = 22,370.997 J

or 22.4 kJ. Note the word order in the working unit: energy is ft·lbf, and it converts to the joule. The 750 lb figure is treated as a weight in pound-force, which is what the formula needs — not a mass in pound-mass.

From Work and Energy

3. A meter reading in megajoules: An electricity meter records 8.4 kW·h over one shift. How many megajoules of energy is that?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

A kilowatt hour is a power multiplied by a time and nothing more: one kilowatt sustained for one hour. So 1 kW·h = 1,000 W × 3,600 s = 3,600,000 J exactly, which is 3.6 MJ.

8.4 kW·h × 3.6 MJ1 kW·h = 30.24 MJ

The shift used 30.24 MJ. The kilowatt hour is not itself an SI unit — NIST lists it among the units to be converted into SI — but the factor is exact, so nothing is lost in the conversion.

From Work and Energy

4. Work from a torque: A fastener is run down with a steady 45 N·m through 2.5 revolutions. How much work is done, in joules?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. The angle has to be in radians before it can multiply a torque: 2.5 r × 6.283185 rad per r = 15.70796 rad.
  2. W = τθ = 45 N·m × 15.70796 rad = 706.858 J.

The work done is 707 J. Nothing was converted between N·m and J along the way, and nothing needed to be: the radian is a length over a length, value 1, so multiplying by an angle in radians leaves the units alone. Feed the same formula 2.5 revolutions or 900 degrees directly and the answer is wrong, because both of those carry a chosen count inside them.

The steady 45 N·m is an idealization — a real run-down torque climbs as the joint seats.

From Power and Efficiency

5. Horsepower for a lift: A 750 lb load is raised 22 ft in 9 s. How much power does that take, in hp?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. W = 750 lbf × 22 ft = 16,500 ft·lbf.
  2. P = W/t = 16,500 ft·lbf ÷ 9 s = 1,833.33 ft·lbf/s.
  3. One horsepower is defined as 550 ft·lbf/s, so dividing by 550 is a unit conversion, not a fudge: 1,833.33 ÷ 550 = 3.3333 hp.

The lift needs 3.33 hp delivered to the load. A motor for the job needs more than that, because no drive is perfectly efficient. Cross-check in SI if you like: 16,500 ft·lbf × 1.355818 = 22,371 J, and 22,371 J ÷ 9 s = 2,486 W, which is 2,486 ÷ 745.6999 = 3.33 hp.

From Power and Efficiency

6. Power from torque and speed: A motor delivers 12 N·m at 1,750 r/min. What is its mechanical power output, in W?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. P = τω needs the speed in rad/s: 1,750 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s per r/min = 183.2596 rad/s.
  2. P = 12 N·m × 183.2596 rad/s = 2,199.1 W.

The output is 2,199 W, or about 2.20 kW. Substituting 1,750 straight into the formula gives 21,000 — wrong by the factor of about 9.55 that separates r/min from rad/s. In coherent SI units the relationship needs no constant at all, which is exactly why the speed has to arrive in rad/s.

From Power and Efficiency

7. The torque-and-rpm shortcut: A drive delivers 250 lbf·in at 1,200 r/min. What is the power, in hp?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

The shortcut takes the torque in pound-force inches and the speed in r/min:

hp = (250 lbf·in × 1,200 r/min) ÷ 63,025 = 300,000 ÷ 63,025 = 4.760 hp

The answer is 4.76 hp. Check it the long way, since the constant is only a folded-up set of conversions: 250 lbf·in = 20.833 lbf·ft, 1,200 r/min = 125.664 rad/s, so P = 20.833 × 125.664 = 2,618.0 ft·lbf/s, and 2,618.0 ÷ 550 = 4.760 hp. The two routes agree to four figures.

Give the shortcut a torque in lbf·ft instead and the answer comes out twelve times too small — the constant already contains the inch-to-foot step.

From Power and Efficiency

8. The volts-and-amps shortcut: A direct-current motor draws 14 A at 24 V. What is that electrical input power, in hp?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. Electrical power first: P = VI = 24 V × 14 A = 336 W.
  2. Then convert: 336 W ÷ 746 W per hp = 0.4504 hp.

The input is 0.450 hp. The 746 is exact because NIST defines a separate unit, horsepower (electric), as 746 W.

The condition sits in step 1, and this problem satisfies it: volts times amps gives real power in watts on direct current or a purely resistive load. On alternating current it gives apparent power in volt-amperes, and the real power is that multiplied by the power factor — so the same shortcut used on an AC motor without its power factor reads high. This is also the motor’s input; its mechanical output is less.

From Power and Efficiency

9. Hoist efficiency: A hoist motor draws 2.2 kW and raises a 900 N load at a steady 2.0 m/s. What is the efficiency of the system, as a percentage?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution
  1. Useful output, using P = Fv: 900 N × 2.0 m/s = 1,800 W.
  2. η = Pout/Pin = 1,800 W ÷ 2,200 W = 0.8182.

The system is 81.8% efficient. The ratio is two like quantities, so the watts cancel and the answer is a plain number. The missing 400 W has not vanished — it is heat in the windings, the gearbox, and the bearings. An answer above 100% would mean an error in the numbers, not a remarkable machine.

This is a system efficiency, measured from the electrical input to the load. The motor alone would quote a higher figure, over a different boundary.
