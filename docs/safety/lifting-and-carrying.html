Lifting and Carrying

Team lifting above 40 lbs, and the technique that prevents the injury. At these loads, how you lift matters more than what it weighs.

Safe manual handling

Safe manual handling

Poor technique injures people at weights they thought were safe. At the loads handled in a lab like this one, how you lift matters more than what it weighs.

The problem is rarely that something is heavy. It is that it is big for its weight. Sheet goods, tube, extrusion and aluminum stock are all low density, so a 40 lb load is a large object — and a large object is one you cannot keep close to your body, cannot see past, and cannot get both hands near the middle of. Every rule below assumes you can hold the load close. Bulk is what takes that away.

Team lifting is required for

  • Anything over 40 lbs.
  • Items lighter than 40 lbs but awkward in shape, size or balance.
  • Any object that feels unstable or is difficult to grip.

The 40 lb line is as much about dropping it as about your back. A load you cannot fully control is a hazard to your feet, the floor, the workpiece and anyone walking past — well before it is a hazard to your spine.

Weight becomes the limiting factor when it defeats your technique, and where that point falls is different for every person and every shape. If you cannot keep the load close and your back straight, it is too heavy for you to lift alone — whatever the number says.

Proper lifting technique

Proper lifting technique

  • Plan the lift — clear your path and know where you are going.
  • Get close to the object before lifting.
  • Squat down using your leg muscles; keep your back straight.
  • Grip firmly with both hands; avoid awkward hand positions.
  • Lift smoothly using your legs, not your back.
  • Keep the load close to your body throughout the lift.

Setting it down

  • Use the same technique in reverse — squat down, do not bend over.
  • Place objects gently. Do not drop them.
