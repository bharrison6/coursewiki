Fire Safety

When to fight a fire and when to leave, and how to use an extinguisher.

Decide before you reach for anything

Decide before you reach for anything

An extinguisher is for a small fire you caught early.

Fight it only if all four are true

  • The fire is small and contained — a bin, a rag, a puddle. Not spreading.
  • You know what is burning, and you have the right extinguisher for it.
  • You have a clear exit behind you and the fire is not between you and it.
  • Someone else has already raised the alarm.

Otherwise: get out. Pull the alarm, leave, call 911 from outside, and tell the instructor and the fire crew what was burning.

If you try and it does not go out in the first few seconds, stop and leave. A hand extinguisher lasts roughly 10–20 seconds. That is your whole attempt.

Using an extinguisher — PASS

Using an extinguisher — PASS

Remember the acronym: P–A–S–S.

When it looks out, back away watching the fire, exit behind you. Do not assume it is over — a knocked-down fire can reignite.

Fire classes, and what puts them out

Fire classes, and what puts them out

Fires are classed by what is burning. An extinguisher is rated for the classes it can handle, and using the wrong one ranges from useless to dangerous.

    Most extinguishers on a wall are ABC dry chemical — one unit covering the first three classes. Check the label; the classes it is rated for are printed on it.

    Metal fires, and what it takes to start one

    Metal fires, and what it takes to start one

    Metal burns only when it is divided finely enough, and then only when several conditions line up at once. Ordinary aluminum chips and swarf will not do this, and neither will the quantities of fine material a lab space like this one generates. Solid stock does not burn at all. The cases below are the ones that are genuinely different.

    The form matters more than the metal

    • Magnesium is the real Class D case. Thin sections, chips and turnings ignite readily and burn with intense heat and a brilliant white light, and dull tools alone can produce enough heat during machining to start it.
    • Titanium turnings can ignite, and titanium fines and grinding dust are worse — fine enough that a static discharge is a credible ignition source.
    • Aluminum fines — the dust off sanding, grinding, buffing and polishing — are a genuine hazard, but only through the specific mechanisms below.
    • Aluminum chips and swarf are hard to ignite. Aluminum carries heat away from a hot spot fast, it wears a tough oxide skin that holds together while the metal underneath melts, and a chip has little surface area for its mass. Put a torch to a pile of chips and they melt.
    • Solid stock does not burn. A block of aluminum is not fuel.

    Magnesium and titanium are not common in student labs. A senior project might bring one in. If you are about to cut, grind or sand either one, ask first — every time.

    Size is the usual line — shape is the exception

    The line in the standard is about half a millimeter, measured across a particle’s smallest dimension — which different editions write as 500 microns or as 420 microns (fine enough to pass a 40-mesh sieve), not two separate limits. Below it, metal dust can carry an explosion. Above it, generally it cannot — and generally is the word the standard uses, not never.

    Flakes are the exception, and buffing and polishing make flakes. A flake is wide but thin, so it is too big for the sieve and still burns like dust. Treat anything off a buffing or polishing wheel as fines however big it looks. Machining chips are genuinely coarse — that is the difference between a chip tray and a dust collector.

    The ways fine metal actually ignites

    • A suspended dust cloud meeting an ignition source. Fines sitting in a tray are not the hazard. Fines thrown into the air, at the right concentration, reaching a flame, a torch, a spark or a static discharge, are — burning travels from one particle to the next with extreme speed. It is why you never blow fine dust around, and why you do not raise a cloud of it cleaning up.
    • Fines heavily mixed with rust or grinding scale. Aluminum plus iron oxide — rust, or the scale off steel grinding — is a thermite reaction: the aluminum strips the oxygen off the iron, so the mixture burns without needing air from outside. That is what makes it a metal fire rather than an ordinary one, and why water and an ABC extinguisher do nothing to it. It takes a real mixture to go — something near three parts iron oxide to one part aluminum by weight, and an ignition source far hotter than a spark. That is a rust-caked pile, not a trace. See Metal Chips and Swarf for how far apart that threshold and the recycling one actually sit.
    • Wet fines. Aluminum fines in contact with water slowly give off hydrogen. That is a reason not to seal damp fines into a closed container, and part of the reason water is the wrong thing to put on a metal fire.

    If fine metal does ignite

    Do not fight it. Most general-purpose shops have no Class D extinguisher — assume you do not have one unless somebody has shown you where it is. Pull the alarm, get out, call 911, and tell the fire crew it is a metal fire. That changes how they fight it, and they need to know before they arrive with a hose.

    PROHIBITED — the ABC extinguisher off the wall. “Dry powder” (Class D) and “dry chemical” (ABC) are not the same thing. An ABC unit throws an incompatible agent at pressure: it scatters burning fines and spreads the fire.

    NEVER put water on a metal fire. The stream throws burning material, can raise an explosive dust cloud, and reacts with the metal to release hydrogen.

    Alarm, evacuate, report.

    Why dry sand is on the list, and why it is probably not on yours

    Sand does work. The Aluminum Association’s Guidelines for Handling Aluminum Fines (F-1) lists fine dry sand, “preferably finer than 20 mesh,” next to approved dry powders as effective for isolating an aluminum fines fire. It also says what has to be true first.

    • Dry. F-1 says dry sand. Damp sand is water, and water “will react with the aluminum fines to form flammable hydrogen gas.”
    • Already in the room. “A plentiful supply should be easily available in the operating area and stored in covered bins or drums.” Sand fetched after the fire starts is not that.
    • With the right tool waiting beside it. “Long handled shovels should be available at each bin or drum, and should be constructed of non-sparking metal.”
    • Laid, not thrown. The shovels “lay the sand or powder around the edge or perimeter of the fire.” You are walling it in. Anything thrown at the pile raises the dust cloud that is the actual explosion.
    • Decided before it started, by someone senior. “Qualified supervisory personnel should decide in advance whether it is better to attempt to fight the fire or to withdraw to a safe location and allow it to burn itself out.”

    Those are preconditions, not tips. If you cannot point at the covered sand bin and the non-sparking shovel beside it right now, you do not have this option — and the call to fight it was never yours to make on the spot. Alarm, evacuate, report.

    What that means day to day

    • Do not let fine dust accumulate. Empty trays, vacuums and collectors rather than letting them fill — see Machine Hazards and Housekeeping for housekeeping.
    • Chips and fines are disposed of differently, and the difference matters — see Metal Chips and Swarf.
    • Let hot chips and debris cool before they go anywhere near a bin of paper or rag.
    • Ask before you machine an unfamiliar metal. If you do not know what it is, that is the answer: ask.
    Hot work and the things that start fires

    Hot work and the things that start fires

    • Grinding throws sparks a surprising distance. Clear rag, sawdust, solvent and packaging out of the area before you start, not after it smolders.
    • Oily rags — it depends entirely on which oil. Drying oils — linseed, tung, oil-based stains, varnishes and finishes — cure by reacting with oxygen, and that reaction gives off heat. Bunched in a pile the rags hold the heat in, and the pile can reach ignition on its own with nothing to light it. Those rags NEVER go in a pile: spread them flat to dry, drop them in water, or use the designated container.
    • Petroleum oils do not do that. Way oil, cutting fluid, hydraulic oil and ordinary shop oil do not cure by oxidation, so a rag wiped with them will not self-heat and will not spontaneously combust. It is still combustible — a spark or a hot chip will light it like any oily cloth — so keep it away from hot work and out of a bin of paper or sawdust. If you do not know which oil is on the rag, treat it as a drying oil.
    • Flammable liquids live in a flammables cabinet when they are not in your hand — see Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets.
    • A smoldering fire can restart long after you leave. If something got hot enough to smoke, tell the instructor before you go.
    Know these before you need them

    Know these before you need them

    None of the above helps if you are looking for equipment while a fire grows. On your first day in any lab space, locate:

    • The nearest fire extinguisher, and what classes it is rated for.
    • The nearest alarm pull station.
    • Both ways out of the room, and the route that does not pass the machines.
    • Where your class gathers outside, so you can be accounted for.
    • The first aid kit and the emergency stops — see Lab Conduct and Emergencies.
