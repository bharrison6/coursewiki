If you never say what a good answer looks like, who decides instead?

Before you start. Every answer has a shape — a length, an audience, a format. Somebody decides it, always. A success specification is the block where you decide it yourself. Leave it out and the decision still gets made, just not by you.

A real prompt, from building this collection

The instruction was “make it 100% accurate”, and every individual claim was accurate. So what went wrong?

This is the same machinery as the three levers, running in a direction that is harder to see.

Sourcedthis project's own build record, 2026-09-16 — a first-party account, not a general finding

These demos were built by AI agents working from written briefs. One brief carried a reasonable-sounding instruction: make it 100% accurate.

Nobody lied. Every claim that came back checked out — the citations resolved, the numbers matched their sources, the corrections were real corrections.

And the overall picture it painted was wrong. Asked to survey how fast AI was improving, the work came back reading as though not much had changed.

The tell, from the record: the research found the cost of one benchmark task had fallen roughly 16,000-fold — from about $4,560 to about $0.284 — while accuracy rose. That got one clause. Four small qualifications about wording got a full paragraph.

Those figures are quoted from this project's own research file, which is where the episode is recorded. They are cited here as what that lane reported, not as a number this demo independently verified.

Guidehow to use this demo

A student asks a question. You decide how much you tell it before it answers. That is the whole demo.

Where this sits. Demo 5 of 17. Demo 2 showed that a model reads its context; here you control that context, one lever at a time.

  1. Context. Pick a question, leave every lever off, press Ask. Then turn on 1 — CONTEXT and ask again.
  2. Success spec. With lever 2 off, press Ask and watch the card it deals itself. Turn on 2 — SUCCESS SPEC and the deck is struck out.
  3. Expert keywords. Turn on 3 — EXPERT KEYWORDS, ask, then press Show me which ones and count the words nobody typed.

Every answer here was written by hand before the file was saved. Nothing is generated live and nothing touches the internet.

Step 2 is built in front of you from the levers you have pulled. That text is real and it is all of it.

Scriptedall 64, written before the file was saved

Every answer is a hand-written fixture looked up from a table. There is no model in this file, no API key and no request of any kind — the build refuses to produce the page if it contains one. The typing animation is decoration at a fixed rate; it is not a model's speed.

How it works

answer = table[ question + which levers are on ]

Four questions × eight lever states, plus three alternate target-deck answers for each of the four states where the success spec is off: 64 authored answers in total.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • These answers were written to make the levers legible. Each single-lever answer was authored to improve in that lever's dimension and nothing else. A real model is messier: it may improve two things at once, or nothing, or get worse. This shows what a better prompt tends to buy you — it is not a measurement of what any model did.
  • The bare answers are bland, not broken — and we wrote them that way so the room cannot dismiss the demo as rigged. Bare prompts in the wild also produce confident errors and refusals.
  • A bare prompt is not information-free. The wording, register and topic of "help me get ready for my test" already carry a lot about the asker.
  • Four questions, one kind of asker. All four are school or first-job tasks for a US high-school or early-college room. Levers behave differently on tasks with a single correct answer.
  • Three levers are not the whole of prompting. Worked examples, step-by-step reasoning, output formats, tool access and simply iterating on a reply are all absent, and all matter.

Sources

  • Nothing on this stage is a published figure, so there is nothing to cite. The counts above were measured from this file's own content by its build gates — see stage 3.
Boundary: this demo does not claim to show what any particular AI system does. It shows what changes when a request carries more of what the asker knows — using answers a person wrote to make that change visible.

Live the card draw

With lever 2 off, the target card is chosen at random from three, in front of you, each time you ask.

Scripteda teaching device, not a mechanism

A real model does not deal from a deck of three. When you do not say what a finished answer should look like, it falls back on habits learned in training: more stable than a card draw, not confined to three options, and varying continuously rather than across three named archetypes. The deck makes a real and normally invisible thing visible. It is not what the machine does.

How it works

card = random of three  →  answer written to that card

Each question carries its own three cards, drawn from a printed deck of eight. In presentation mode a picker fixes the draw so a beat can be rehearsed; otherwise it is always random.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The deck is the simplification, and it is the biggest one in the demo. Three discrete named cards stand in for a continuous distribution of defaults. Keep the insight — an unstated target gets filled in by something else — and drop the dice.
  • The card is shown to you. A real system does not announce the target it assumed. That courtesy is the demo's, not the machine's.
  • Showing you the card is itself a teaching choice that makes the effect look tidier than it is.

Sources

  • No published figure is used on this stage. The claim about where real defaults come from is stated as the authors' reading, not as a citation — which is why it is not dressed as one.
Boundary: the deck is a prop for an argument, not a model of an algorithm. This demo does not claim that any system selects among a fixed menu of answer shapes.

What this page actually computes

Scriptedthe count is an author's tag list, not a detector

The big number under an answer counts the specialist terms the author tagged in that answer. Nothing on this page detects specialist vocabulary; it reads a list.

Live "words you never typed"

The second number is computed, now, against the prompt you assembled: each tagged term is looked for in your own text. It matches plain text, so a term that appears in your prompt only in another form — "mitosis" in the prompt against "mitotic" as a term — still counts as new.

Measuredthis file's own content, by its build gates

Across all 64 answers, keyword-off states carry 0 to 4 tagged terms and keyword-on states carry 15 to 20. Those are measured ranges, enforced by the content gates, not estimates.

How it works

novel = term not found in your assembled prompt

Terms are matched on word boundaries and only the first occurrence of each is marked, which is what makes the final count equal the authored list exactly.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • More expert words is not the same as a better answer. The counter measures vocabulary, and vocabulary is the easiest thing to mistake for understanding. The demo's own walkthrough asks you to check whether the answer got more useful or just more impressive; that question is the point of this stage.
  • The 1–10 curve measures this room, not AI. It plots the scores you give against how many levers were pulled. The answers were written to get better as levers are pulled, so a rising line means the room agrees with the author. It is a good way to make a room commit out loud; it is not evidence about models.
  • The lever colours include one non-brand hue. Lever 3's violet is not part of the Murray State palette. It is kept deliberately: the three lever colours are a code printed on the physical cut-out target cards a presenter hands round the room, so the screen and the paper have to agree. It is the only sanctioned exception in this demo.
  • Some bad answers are not a prompting problem at all. A lever can fix an answer that is vague, mis-aimed or the wrong shape. It cannot fix one that is confidently wrong, out of date, refusing, or past what the system can do. Those need checking, not a better prompt.

PROMPTING STRATEGIES The Stranger · presenter sheet · 12 minutes

Learning goals

The three stage Takeaways, verbatim from the app. Each is a plain claim the activity demonstrably shows, and each is what that stage's check-yourself item tests.

  1. CONTEXT With no context the answer is aimed at everybody, which is another way of saying nobody. Context changes who it is for — not its length, and not its vocabulary.
  2. SUCCESS SPEC An unspecified target does not stay unspecified — something else fills it in, and does not tell you. Saying what finished looks like takes that decision back.
  3. EXPERT KEYWORDS Asking in a field's vocabulary gets that vocabulary back — words nobody in the room supplied. That is real. It is also not the same thing as a better answer, and telling those apart is the skill.

Where this sits on the path

Demo 5 of 17 · Part three. Demo 2 showed that a model reads its context; here the room controls that context, one lever at a time. It hands forward to Demo 6: a perfect prompt in a browser tab still cannot touch your files, so the surface decides reach.

One question for the session

“Who decided what a good answer to your question looks like?”

The roleplay — three acts

You are the Stranger. A volunteer is the Asker. The app is on the projector behind you; run the same states on it as you say them out loud.

~3 minACT I — CONTEXT

“I am a stranger on a street corner. I am not stupid. I know a great deal about almost everything. I have never met you.”

1. The Asker says only: “Help me get ready for my test.” Answer out loud, as blandly and generally as you honestly can. Cover every case. Do not clown it — a real assistant is competent here, just aimed at nobody.

2. Ask the room: what would I have needed to know? Take three answers, put them on the board.

3. The Asker reads the context block. Answer again, aimed at that kid.

“I didn't get smarter. You got specific.”

~4 minACT II — THE TARGET DECK

Hold the eight cards from page two face down. “Somebody has to decide what a good answer looks like. If you don't decide, I do. And I never tell you what I picked.”

1. Same question, context on, no success spec. Draw a card and do not show it. Answer to the card — if it says exhaustive, talk for forty-five seconds without stopping.

2. Now show the card. Wait for the room.

3. Draw a different card. Same question, answer again. Same asker, same words, different answer — and the difference was never theirs.

4. The Asker reads the success spec. Put the deck down and slide it across the table, away from you. On the app, the deck lives inside lever 2: flip it on and the three card names get struck out where they sit. Same gesture, same panel.

“You just took the deck out of my hands.”

On the app

? is the Guide: what every control does. It opens itself on load. ⚙︎ beside it is Settings — Open Presenter Notes (this sheet, on screen; or press N), Presentation mode (the projector build: big type, the 1–10 score strip, compare, and the card picker inside lever 2) and Reset.

Reset is a fresh load without the reload: question 1, levers off, no answer on screen, scoreboard cleared. It deliberately leaves Presentation mode where you set it, so resetting between classes never drops you into student mode.

~4 minACT III — THE FOLDERS

Three labelled folders or sheets: BIOLOGY, BRIDGES, WRITING AT WORK. “I have a folder on nearly everything. The words you use decide which one I open.”

1. The Asker: “Why do bridges stay up?” Open nothing. Answer in plain words: the bottom gets stretched, the top gets squished. All true. All unusable.

2. The Asker adds the keyword block. Open BRIDGES and answer in the real vocabulary — load path, bending moment, neutral axis, section depth.

3. On the app, press H. Count aloud the expert words in the answer that the Asker never typed.

“You didn't know those words. You didn't have to. You only had to point at the folder.”

Say this before you move on — it matters. “There are no folders. I don't open anything. Your words change which words are likely to come next — that is Demo 2's machine, doing this.” The folders are a memory aid for the room. Left unsaid, the prop teaches a filing-cabinet model of a language model, which contradicts Demo 2 — this demo's own prerequisite — and a learner who does Demo 2 then Demo 5 is handed two incompatible machines.

House rules

  1. The Stranger is never stupid. In this demo the bare answers are bland, not broken — we wrote them that way, because the demo dies the moment the bad answer looks rigged. Say it as a rule about the demo, not about models: bare prompts in the wild also produce confident errors, wrong lengths and refusals.
  2. No trick answers. If the prompt supports a good answer, give a good answer.
  3. Every answer gets a number. 1–10, from the room, out loud, on the board before the next one.
  4. Nobody says “the AI is bad at this” without naming which lever is off.
  5. The Asker types nothing they wouldn't really type. No fair inventing a great prompt to prove a point.

2 Question: If you never say what a good answer looks like, who decides instead?

Expected observation: a target card is dealt, face up, and the answer is written to it. Turn lever 2 on and the deck is struck out inside the lever.
Answer key: something else fills it in. Correct option Success spec.

Misconceptions this demo can leave — name them, refute them

“I watched the model get better as the prompt got better.” No model ran. All 64 answers were written by hand before the file was saved, and the panel carries a permanent Scripted label saying so. What the room watched was an author's four answers, chosen to make one lever's effect legible. The claim the demo can honestly make is what a better prompt tends to buy you, never what this model did.
“The rising curve proves more levers make better answers.” It cannot. The answers were written to improve as levers are pulled, the room scores them, and the mean rises — so the curve measures the room agreeing with the author and could not come out any other way. Keep it: making a room commit out loud is good teaching. Do not offer it as evidence, and say which it is.
“If it is not in the prompt box, the AI does not know it.” False three ways. What it knows in general came from training and is not in the box; almost every real product prepends a system prompt the user never sees; and history, retrieved documents and tool output enter the context invisibly. The true and useful version: nothing about you and your situation reaches it unless you put it there.
“A bad answer means I left a lever unpulled.” Sometimes. A lever fixes vague, mis-aimed or wrong-shaped. It does not fix confidently wrong, out of date, refusing, or past what the system can do — and on those, “write a better prompt” is the wrong response. The closing line says both halves; do not shorten it to the first.
“A careful prompt cannot mislead.” The accuracy-brief case on stage 3 is a documented counter-example from building this very collection: a brief asked for 100% accuracy, every claim that came back was true, and the overall picture was still wrong — because the instruction only asked for one direction of finding. Symmetry is the repair, not more care.

Discussion questions

  1. The first answer was accurate. Why was it useless?
  2. Who decided what a good answer looked like, and when?
  3. The expert answer used words you never typed. Where did they come from, and how did asking in plain words keep them hidden?
  4. Which of the three levers is hardest to pull for a subject you know nothing about? What do you do then?
  5. Name a time you blamed a tool for an answer you had underspecified.
  6. Success spec means saying what “done” looks like. When is that hard to say — and what does that tell you about the task itself?
