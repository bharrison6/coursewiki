Pictorial Sketching

Block out height, width, and depth, then use light construction lines to develop a clear freehand pictorial.

Sketch the idea before committing to CAD

Sketch the idea before committing to CAD

A quick hand sketch lets you compare shapes, explain an idea, and find missing information before building a CAD model. Aim for a readable shape and sensible proportions. A sketch does not need mechanically perfect lines to be useful.

Use a pencil, eraser, and plain or grid paper. Isometric paper can help keep the three directions consistent. Start lightly so you can revise; darken the visible edges only after the overall form works. Add small features last.

Label important features and write any known dimensions. Do not expect someone to recover an exact size by measuring your freehand sketch. For line meanings, see The Alphabet of Lines. CAD sketch tools are covered separately in Sketch Entities and Planes.

Isometric sketches use three equally spaced directions

Isometric sketches use three equally spaced directions

Draw one axis vertically and the other two down to the left and right, each 30° from horizontal. The three outward rays are 120° apart. Edges parallel to these axes keep those directions throughout the sketch; they can extend either way along each axis line.

Use the same scale along all three axis directions. Lay out width, height, and depth along lines parallel to the axes; an isometric grid helps you keep equal intervals consistent.

Isometric drawing and isometric projection use different scales

An ordinary isometric drawing lays out axis-parallel lengths at the chosen drawing scale. A geometrically projected isometric view foreshortens all three axes equally. Do not mix an isometric projection scale with ordinary drawing lengths in one sketch. The angle of a drawn edge on the paper is not generally its angle on the part.

For CAD view types, see CAD Model Views and Checks.

Locate sloping edges by endpoints; sketch circles as ellipses

Locate sloping edges by endpoints; sketch circles as ellipses

A non-isometric line is not parallel to any of the three isometric axes. Locate both endpoints using width, depth, and height offsets, then join them. Do not lay the true sloping-edge length directly along its apparent direction on the paper.

For a circle on a face parallel to a principal plane, first sketch its enclosing square as an isometric rhombus. Mark the four side midpoints and sketch a smooth ellipse tangent there. Those are contact points, not sharp corners. Keep the curve inside the rhombus.

The circle-to-ellipse rule here applies to circles on the usual top, front, or side planes of the isometric sketch. Do not draw a round circle on one of these tilted faces.

Build a box, locate the cut, then remove material; Checklist

Build a box, locate the cut, then remove material

Start with the smallest simple box that encloses the part: height, width, depth. In this original example, a corner is removed uniformly through the depth. That makes the front profile a useful starting shape.

  • Compare the overall height, width, and depth before adding detail.
  • Mark where the cut begins and ends on the front profile.
  • Carry those points back along parallel depth lines.
  • Connect matching points to form the new face.
  • Check the remaining edges, then darken visible edges and lighten or erase construction lines.

“Extrude” means carry a profile through a distance. It is a useful mental model only when the cross-section stays constant over that distance. Check other views for features that stop partway through.

Choose isometric for three directions or oblique for a true front face; Practice

Choose isometric for three directions or oblique for a true front face

In an isometric sketch, the three principal directions share one scale, and none of the three principal faces appears in true shape. In an oblique sketch, draw the chosen front face in true shape at the drawing scale, then carry parallel edges back along one receding direction.

A 45° receding direction is common, but it is not required. Full-scale depth is called cavalier; half-scale depth is called cabinet. Keep the chosen depth scale consistent. A circle on the oblique front face stays circular; one on a receding face generally does not.

Both methods use parallel edges rather than perspective vanishing points. Pick the front direction deliberately; see Choosing Drawing Views.
