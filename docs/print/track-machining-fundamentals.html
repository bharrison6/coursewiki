Climb and Conventional Milling

Which way the work is fed past a turning cutter decides whether each chip starts thick or thin, which way the cut pulls on the work, and why backlash in a feed screw matters.

Feed direction decides whether each chip starts thick or thin

Feed direction decides whether each chip starts thick or thin

A milling cutter takes metal off in small slices, one slice per tooth. Whether each slice starts thick and ends thin, or starts thin and ends thick, comes down to one thing: which way the work is fed past the turning cutter.

Climb milling
Also called down milling. Where a tooth is cutting, it moves the same way the work is fed. Each chip starts thick and ends thin.
Conventional milling
Also called up milling. Where a tooth is cutting, it moves against the feed. Each chip starts thin and ends thick.
Feed per tooth
How far the work moves while the cutter turns from one tooth to the next. It sets how thick a chip can get.
Width of cut
How far the cutter reaches into the side of the work, measured across the cutter. Machinists also call it the radial depth of cut.

How to read the drawings on this page. Each drawing is a slice straight across the fluted part of an end mill, seen looking down its axis from the spindle; the small side view in the corner marks where the slice is taken, the way a section view does on a drawing. This sketch places the cutter’s axis horizontally, with the table below it; screen-down is toward that table. It is not a top view of a vertical spindle. From the spindle end a standard right-hand cutter turns clockwise. The cutter stays where it is and the work is fed past it, the way a mill table carries the work. Every Work feed arrow is the work’s motion. On a machine that moves the cutter instead, nothing changes: the cutter simply travels the opposite way to that arrow.

The cutter is the same in both modes and spins the same way. What switches climb to conventional is the direction of feed relative to the side of the cutter that is doing the cutting. The down/up force arrows belong to this horizontal arrangement. A top view of a vertical end mill would show forces across the table, not axial force into or away from it.

Watch one tooth take its chip

Watch one tooth take its chip

The dashed gold outline is the slice of metal the cutting tooth has taken so far, drawn where it was. The solid gold shape is that metal as a chip, curling off the cutting edge into the flute. The curled chip is scaled to fit the flute at this exaggerated feed; its drawn size is not a thickness measurement. Play it, or drag the tooth through the cut, and use the dashed slice to compare thickness.

Each chip forms on the edge, rides out in the flute and is thrown clear

Each chip forms on the edge, rides out in the flute and is thrown clear

A chip does not stay where the slice was. The cutting edge shears it off the work, it slides along the face in front of the edge and curls into the flute, and the flute carries it round until it is thrown clear. Where that happens depends on the direction of feed.

Swipe the four moments sideways on a narrow screen, or focus the drawing and use the arrow keys to scroll.

Reading the cutter

  • Cutting edge: the tip of each tooth, the only part that touches the finished surface. The tooth that is cutting is outlined in blue.
  • Rake face: the face just in front of the edge. The chip slides along it as it forms.
  • Flute: the curved valley in front of each tooth. The chip curls in it and is carried out of the cut in it, so a flute packed with chips cannot take the next one.
  • Core: the solid centre, hatched because the drawing cuts through it. In this illustrated family, more flutes leave a thicker core and less room for chips. Actual proportions depend on the tool design.

The chip is drawn shorter and thinner than the slice so it fits the flute at a feed drawn many times too large. A continuous metal chip usually becomes shorter and thicker through deformation; IIT Kharagpur’s chip-formation lesson explains the change. The path it takes is illustrative; coolant, tool geometry and the material affect actual chip motion. The cutter’s flutes and chip space follow Harvey Performance’s end mill anatomy.

Six things to try in the demo

Six things to try in the demo

  • Choose Compare both and step until each tooth has only just entered the work. Which chip is already thick, and which is barely there?
  • Drag Tooth position in the cut from 0% to 100%, once in each mode. Watch the uncut-thickness readout: in climb it runs down from the peak, in conventional it runs up to it.
  • Set the width of cut to 50%. The tooth now cuts through a quarter of a turn and the chip reaches the full feed per tooth. Drop it to 10%: the peak falls to 60% of the feed. At the same feed, a narrower cut makes a thinner chip.
  • Raise the feed per tooth and watch the dashed uncut slice grow thicker. Its thick-to-thin trend stays the same. The curled chip is scaled to fit the flute and may stop growing on screen; compare thickness using the dashed slice.
  • Follow one chip from start to finish. Pause just before a tooth leaves the work, then step forward. In which mode is the thick end of the chip at the curled tip, and in which is it still on the edge? Where does each chip go once it is free?
  • Watch the red-orange arrow, the pull of the cut on the work. In climb it pulls the work the way it is already going and presses it down. In conventional it pushes back against the feed and lifts. Keep that in mind for the card on backlash.
Starting thin means rubbing; starting thick means a bite

Starting thin means rubbing; starting thick means a bite

A real cutting edge has a rounded tip. When the uncut layer is extremely thin, the edge can push and rub the metal before a chip forms. The peripheral cuts shown here make that difference visible.

  • Conventional milling starts every chip at zero, so every tooth rubs before it bites. Rubbing makes heat and wears the edge. Look at where the tooth enters, too: on the finished surface, the one you are keeping.
  • Climb milling starts the chips shown here at their thickest, so the tooth bites at once, with a knock each time a tooth enters.

That is the main reason climb milling is often the preferred direction when the machine can hold the work steady against it. Whether it can is the next card.

Which way the cut pulls on the work

  • Climb: the teeth drag the work along the way it is already being fed, and tend to push it down onto the table in the peripheral arrangement shown.
  • Conventional: the teeth push back against the feed and tend to lift the work in this arrangement, so whatever holds the work has to resist that lift.
Backlash: why climb milling depends on the machine

Backlash: why climb milling depends on the machine

A feed screw moves the table by turning inside a nut. Unless the drive is built to take it out, there is some clearance between the screw’s thread and the nut. That clearance is called backlash, and it lets the table shift a little while the screw stands still.

  • Conventional: the cut pushes the table back, against the feed. That holds the nut against the flank of the thread the screw is driving with, so the gap stays on the far side and the table moves only as fast as the screw turns.
  • Climb: the cut pulls the table forward, the way it is already moving. If there is clearance, the table can slide across it ahead of the screw, and the cutter takes a bigger bite than the feed was set for, all at once. That sudden, oversized bite is hard on the cutter and on whatever is holding the work.

This is a question to ask about a machine, not a rule. How much clearance a feed drive has, and whether it is built to take it out, differs from machine to machine. This page does not tell you which direction to use on any machine you will meet. Ask the person responsible for that machine.

A slot is both at once

A slot is both at once

When the cutter is buried across its whole width, cutting a slot, each tooth enters at one wall and leaves at the other. Its chip starts thin, is thickest halfway across, and ends thin. The first half of the sweep moves against the feed, like conventional milling, and the second half moves with it, like climb milling, whichever way the work is fed. That is why the demo stops at half the cutter’s diameter: past that point the two can no longer be pulled apart.

Feeds and Speeds

How a cutting speed and a cutter diameter set the spindle speed, how the spindle speed, flute count and chip load set the table feed, and what changes when only the spindle speed does.

Two numbers come from cutting data; two go into the machine

Two numbers come from cutting data; two go into the machine

A milling setup is described by four numbers. Two describe what each cutting edge goes through, and they come from cutting data for the tool and the material. The other two are what you actually set on the machine, and you work them out from the first two.

Cutting speed
How fast the cutting edge moves through the material: the speed at the outside of the cutter. Written in surface feet per minute (SFM) or metres per minute (m/min). Also called surface speed. Comes from cutting data.
Feed per tooth
How far the work moves while the cutter turns from one tooth to the next, in inches or millimetres per tooth. Also called chip load, because it sets the size of the bite each tooth takes. Comes from cutting data.
Spindle speed
How many turns the cutter makes each minute, in revolutions per minute (rev/min, usually said RPM). Set on the machine.
Table feed
How fast the work moves past the cutter, in inches or millimetres per minute. Also called the feed rate. On a machine that moves the cutter instead, it is how fast the cutter travels. Set on the machine.

"Speeds" are the cutter turning; "feeds" are the work moving. That is all the phrase feeds and speeds means. The two are set separately on the machine, and the rest of this page is about how they depend on each other.

Spindle speed: the turns it takes to reach the cutting speed

Spindle speed: the turns it takes to reach the cutting speed

Cutting speed is a distance per minute, measured at the edge of the cutter. In one turn, a point on that edge travels once around the cutter: its circumference, π × D. So the spindle speed is the cutting speed divided by the distance one turn covers, once the two are in the same units.

Spindle speed (rev/min) = 12 × cutting speed (SFM) ÷ (π × cutter diameter in inches)

The 12 is there because cutting speed is in feet and the diameter is in inches: 12 inches to the foot. In metric units the same idea needs a 1000, millimetres to the metre:

Spindle speed (rev/min) = 1000 × cutting speed (m/min) ÷ (π × cutter diameter in mm)

Worked example, with illustrative numbers. A 0.5 in cutter, with cutting data that calls for 300 SFM: 12 × 300 ÷ (π × 0.5) = 2,292 rev/min. The 300 SFM is there to make the arithmetic concrete. It is not a recommendation for any tool or any material.

Look at where the diameter sits: underneath. Halve the diameter and the spindle speed doubles. A small cutter has to turn faster to give its edge the same cutting speed, which is why small cutters so often ask for more speed than a machine has. That case has its own card below.

Table feed: each tooth takes one chip load as it passes

Table feed: each tooth takes one chip load as it passes

Each time a tooth passes, the work should have moved one feed per tooth. The number of teeth passing each minute is the spindle speed times the number of flutes, so:

Table feed = spindle speed × flutes × feed per tooth

Worked example, continued. A 4-flute cutter at 2,292 rev/min, with a feed per tooth of 0.002 in: 2,292 × 4 × 0.002 = 18.3 in/min. The units work out on their own: turns per minute, times teeth per turn, times inches per tooth, leaves inches per minute.

Turned around, the same relation tells you what chip load a feed gives. That is the way to check a feed someone else has set:

Feed per tooth = table feed ÷ (spindle speed × flutes)

Flutes here means the teeth that actually take a chip in each turn. For an ordinary end mill that is simply the number of flutes.

How to read the drawings. You are looking down the cutter’s axis at a slot being cut. The cutter turns clockwise in place, and the work is fed to the left, so the slot trails behind it. The main drawing is to scale. The separate box is a schematic distance record: each tick records a tooth passing the front of the cut. Gold shows how far the table has moved since the last pass. Everything runs in slow motion, and the turning and the feed are slowed by the same amount, so what you see between them is the real ratio. The time scale is written under the drawing.

Six things to try in the calculator

Six things to try in the calculator

  • Load the half-inch cutter example and check each worked line against the formulas on this page.
  • Switch to metric and back. The lengths and speeds change units, but the cutter and the cut do not: 0.5 in becomes 12.7 mm, and the spindle speed stays exactly the same.
  • Hold the table feed and drag the spindle speed to 200%. The chip load halves. In the distance record the ticks crowd together and the gold travel bar gets narrower.
  • Now hold the chip load and do the same. The table feed doubles instead: the ticks keep their spacing but arrive twice as often.
  • Press Play and count how many times the cutter turns while the work, in the main drawing, barely seems to move. Then look at the enlarged distance record to see that it does.
  • Load the eighth-inch cutter on a 4,000 rev/min machine. Compare the speed the cutting data asks for with the speed the machine can reach.
When the machine cannot turn as fast as the formula asks

When the machine cannot turn as fast as the formula asks

Because small cutters ask for high spindle speeds, the formula can easily give more than a machine’s top speed. A 0.125 in cutter at 300 SFM asks for 9,167 rev/min. On a machine that stops at 4,000, it runs at 4,000.

  • Work the feed from the speed the spindle really turns. At 4,000 rev/min, with 2 flutes and 0.0008 in per tooth, that is 4,000 × 2 × 0.0008 = 6.4 in/min. Each tooth then takes the chip load you meant it to.
  • The cutting speed is what you give up. At 4,000 rev/min the edge of a 0.125 in cutter moves at π × 0.125 × 4,000 ÷ 12 = 131 SFM, not the 300 the data asked for.

Working the feed from the speed that was asked for, rather than the one the spindle is turning, gets the chip load wrong by the same ratio. A feed worked from 9,167 rev/min would give each tooth 9,167 ÷ 4,000 = 2.3 times the chip load intended.

The same applies to a machine that offers only certain speeds, through belts or gears: set the nearest speed it has, and work the feed from that one. The calculator’s machine top speed box does this for a top speed.

A calculator is not cutting data

A calculator is not cutting data

The arithmetic on this page is exact. Its inputs are the part that takes judgement. The cutting speed and feed per tooth come from cutting data, normally the toolmaker’s for that tool, that material and that kind of cut, or from the person responsible for the machine. This page does not supply them, and its example numbers exist only to make the arithmetic concrete.

Cutting data accounts for things these formulas cannot see: the material being cut, what the tool is made of, how much of the cutter is in the work, and how the setup is held. A calculator turns data into machine settings. It cannot tell you whether the data suit your cut.

Tolerances and Fits

The permitted size ranges on a hole and a shaft decide whether the two have clearance, interference, or could have either.

A size tolerance gives two limits

A size tolerance gives two limits

Manufacturing produces variation. For each diameter in this lab, the drawing gives a size between two limits. A measured diameter inside that interval passes this size check.

Nominal size
The size a feature is named by, such as Ø0.7500 in. Ø means diameter. Every limit is measured from this number.
Deviation
How far a limit sits above (+) or below (−) nominal. +0.0010 puts a limit 0.0010 in above it.
Limits
Nominal plus each deviation: the largest and smallest sizes that pass inspection. Ø0.7500 +0.0010 / 0.0000 means anything from 0.7500 in to 0.7510 in.
Tolerance
The width of that range: largest limit minus smallest limit. Here, 0.0010 in.

A size exactly on a limit passes. On this page the limits are part of the allowed range: a 0.7500 in hole and a 0.7510 in hole are both good. A 0.7511 in hole is not.

Clearance is the hole size minus the shaft size

Clearance is the hole size minus the shaft size

Put a shaft in a hole and the number that decides the fit is the difference between their two diameters.

clearance = hole size − shaft size

  • Positive: the hole is bigger. There is a gap, and the shaft slides in.
  • Negative: the shaft is bigger. That is interference: on paper the parts overlap, so the shaft has to be pressed in, or it does not go at all.
  • Zero: exactly the same size, called line-to-line. No gap and no overlap.

Clearance is measured across the whole diameter. With the shaft centred in the hole, the gap you would see on each side is half of it:

gap on each side = clearance ÷ 2

Three kinds of fit, decided by the two worst cases

Three kinds of fit, decided by the two worst cases

You do not have to check every pair of parts a drawing allows. Two pairs settle it: the tightest and the loosest.

Tightest legal pair
The smallest hole with the largest shaft. Its clearance is the least any good pair can have:
hole minimum − shaft maximum
Loosest legal pair
The largest hole with the smallest shaft. Its clearance is the most any good pair can have:
hole maximum − shaft minimum
The fit is named by where those two numbers land.

What this lab leaves out

What this lab leaves out

The lab compares two diameters and nothing else. A real assembly also depends on:

  • Shape. No hole is perfectly round or straight, and no shaft is either. A shaft can measure in tolerance everywhere a micrometer touches it and still be bent too far to go in.
  • Temperature. Parts grow when they warm up, so the same part can measure differently straight off the machine and an hour later.
  • Surface finish and measurement. A rough surface and an imperfect measurement both blur the last decimal place the drawing asks for.
  • Force. Whether an interference pair can be pressed together, how hard, and what that does to the parts are questions about the material, not about the two sizes.

The limits in the lab are made-up teaching values. Real drawings often take limits from published tables of standard fits; those tables are not reproduced here.

    Addendum 1. Why some machines climb mill without trouble

    A feed drive can be designed to reduce backlash, for example with a preloaded ball nut. The important question is whether the machine controls that movement, not simply whether it is called CNC. The chip is the same shape either way; what changes is whether the machine can hold the table against the pull of the cut.

  2. Machining/Milling/Feeds and Speeds

    Addendum 2. Feed per tooth is not always the thickness of the chip

    Feed per tooth is how far the work moves between teeth. The chip a tooth actually cuts is only that thick when the cutter is at least half buried across its diameter. In a narrower cut, each tooth meets the work at a slant and the chip comes out thinner. Climb and Conventional Milling shows this: at a width of cut of 10% of the diameter, the thickest part of the chip is 60% of the feed per tooth.

    Some cutting data raises the feed per tooth for narrow cuts to make up for this. Read what your data assumes before using its numbers, and ask if it does not say.

    The spindle-speed, table-feed and feed-per-tooth relations follow Sandvik Coromant’s milling formulas and definitions. The drawings use an idealized cutter section; see Harvey Performance’s end mill anatomy for the flutes, core and cutting edges of a real end mill.
