Metals and Their Properties

How bonding, grains and alloy condition affect the properties used to choose a metal.

Metal selection starts with the job the part must do

Metal selection starts with the job the part must do

A metal can carry a load, conduct current, transfer heat or resist wear. Those are different jobs. Start with the required behavior, then compare a particular alloy in a particular condition. An alloy combines a base metal with other elements to change its behavior.

Metals belong to the material-family foundations topic. Use Material Properties for the general property vocabulary; this page applies it to metals. Continue with Types of Steel, Nonferrous Metals, Metal Stock Forms and Purchasing and Heat Treating Metals.

Metallic bonding helps explain conduction and forming

Metallic bonding helps explain conduction and forming

In a metal, some electrons are shared throughout the structure rather than tied to one bond between two atoms. Their mobility helps metals conduct electricity and heat. Metallic bonding can remain intact while atoms rearrange during permanent deformation, which helps explain why many metals can be rolled or drawn.

Most engineering metals contain many tiny crystals called grains. Atoms have an ordered arrangement inside each grain; adjacent grains have different orientations. Grain size, alloy chemistry and processing history all influence properties.

Strength, hardness, ductility and toughness answer different questions

Strength, hardness, ductility and toughness answer different questions

Mechanical properties used when comparing metals
PropertyQuestion it answersSelection example
StrengthHow much stress can the material withstand? Yield strength concerns the start of permanent deformation; tensile strength concerns the maximum stress in a tensile test.A loaded bracket must resist permanent bending.
HardnessHow strongly does the surface resist local indentation?A wear surface may need hardness, but hardness alone does not predict every kind of wear.
DuctilityHow much permanent deformation can occur before fracture?A formed part needs enough ductility to survive bending.
ToughnessHow much energy can the material absorb before fracture?A part struck repeatedly needs resistance to fracture, not simply a hard surface.

Strong does not mean stiff. Stiffness describes elastic deflection. Hardening a steel can raise its strength substantially without a comparable change in its elastic stiffness. Geometry also affects how far a part bends.

Pull a tensile specimen: strength, stiffness, ductility and toughness on one curve; Interactive

Pull a tensile specimen: strength, stiffness, ductility and toughness on one curve

A tension test pulls a specimen of known size until it breaks and records force against stretch. Dividing by the original area and gauge length turns that record into an engineering stress–strain curve, and one curve answers four different questions: how stiff, how strong, how far it stretches and how much energy it absorbs.

The curves below are synthetic teaching curves, shaped to resemble common behaviour. Read the method from them, not the numbers for any real alloy.

Tensile test: one curve, four different properties. This fallback shows the same synthetic curves and calculations as the live lab.

A round specimen with a chosen gauge length L0 = 50 mm and diameter 12.5 mm (original area A0 = 122.7 mm²) is pulled by a moving crosshead. Engineering stress σ = F/A0 (N/mm² = MPa). Engineering strain ε = ΔL/L0 (mm/mm, shown here in %). Both use the original dimensions, so engineering stress falls after the neck forms even though the force is concentrated on a shrinking section.

  • Stiffness: the elastic slope, Young’s modulus E = σ/ε = 200 MPa ÷ 0.001 = 200 000 MPa = 200 GPa.
  • Yield strength (0.2% offset): draw a line with slope E starting at 0.2% strain; where it meets the curve, σy ≈ 250 MPa (at 0.325% strain). Specimen A then stretches along a level yield plateau (stress constant at 250 MPa up to 1.5% strain) before strain hardening makes the curve rise again.
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS): the highest engineering stress, 400 MPa at 20% strain. A neck begins to form after this peak.
  • Ductility: elongation after fracture ≈ εf − σf/E = 30% − 0.16% = 29.84%.
  • Toughness (tensile): the area under the curve to fracture, ∫σ dε ≈ 107 MJ/m³ (1 MPa × 1 = 1 MJ/m³ = 1 J/cm³) of work per original volume. For this gauge volume that is about 659 J. This is not fracture toughness KIC, which is measured on a cracked specimen.

Worked example: unloading leaves permanent set. Stop at ε = 10.0%, where σ = 371 MPa. Removing the load follows a straight line parallel to the elastic slope, so the elastic part σ/E = 371/200 000 = 0.185% springs back. Permanent set = 10.0% − 0.185% = 9.815%, or 4.91 mm on the 50 mm gauge length. Below the yield point the same rule gives zero set: at 0.1% strain and 200 MPa, 0.1% − 200/200 000 = 0.

Compare the four synthetic specimens. Each ranks differently on each property: the stiffest is not always the strongest, and the most ductile is not the toughest.

Model, assumptions and sources

The four curves are synthetic: piecewise cubic curves through chosen points, shaped to resemble common behaviour. They are not measurements, grades, certification values or design allowables. Real results depend on composition, processing, specimen geometry, test speed and temperature, none of which are modelled.

Unloading and reloading follow a straight line with the initial elastic slope E (an ideal elastic approximation, no hysteresis). The load never goes below zero: once the unload line reaches zero load the specimen is slack. Permanent set = ε − σ/E, never negative. Elongation after fracture uses the same rule at the fracture point.

The specimen length is drawn to scale. Its thinning before the peak keeps volume constant; the neck and the fracture are drawn only to show where deformation concentrates, not calculated from a necking or fracture law. True stress, strain rate, temperature and hardness are outside this lab.

The area under the curve is work per original volume in this tension test (MPa = MJ/m³). Impact and fracture-toughness tests measure related but different behaviour.

Sources: ASTM E8/E8M, Standard Test Methods for Tension Testing of Metallic Materials (the test method and its definitions; consult the current edition). W. D. Callister Jr. and D. G. Rethwisch, Materials Science and Engineering: An Introduction, chapter “Mechanical Properties of Metals” (engineering stress and strain, modulus, 0.2% offset yield, tensile strength and necking, ductility, toughness as area under the curve, elastic recovery on unloading).

Density, conductivity and corrosion can decide the choice

Density, conductivity and corrosion can decide the choice

Density is mass per volume. Compare equal volumes when comparing densities; a large aluminum part can still weigh more than a small steel part. Use Geometry, Mass, and Force and Dimensional Analysis for mass, weight and unit calculations.

Conductivity describes how readily heat or current travels through a material. Metals vary widely: choosing a good conductor is different from choosing a high-strength alloy.

Corrosion resistance depends on the metal and its surroundings. Stainless steel and aluminum can corrode. Water, salts, trapped moisture and contact with other metals can change performance. A protective surface film helps only while the environment allows it to remain effective.

Ferrous means iron-based; a magnet does not identify the grade; Practice

Ferrous means iron-based; a magnet does not identify the grade

Ferrous metals have iron as their base: steels and cast irons belong here. Nonferrous metals have another base, such as aluminum, copper or titanium. Nonferrous does not mean that no trace of iron can be present.

Magnetic response is a clue, not a material certificate. Some stainless steels are weakly magnetic or effectively nonmagnetic in certain conditions. Appearance and a magnet cannot establish an alloy, its temper or its strength.

Types of Steel

Carbon, alloy, stainless and tool steels, plus the difference between a steel family and a complete material specification.

Steel is iron-based, with carbon and other elements controlling behavior

Steel is iron-based, with carbon and other elements controlling behavior

Steel contains iron and carbon, often with additional alloying elements. Both composition and processing matter. The same grade can arrive softened for machining or hardened for service.

Use four practical groups: carbon steel, alloy steel, stainless steel and tool steel. These are useful buying categories, not four mutually exclusive chemical definitions: stainless and tool steels also contain alloying elements.

Grade labels identify a material; they are not a heat-treatment recipe

Grade labels identify a material; they are not a heat-treatment recipe

Common labels include 1018 and 1045 for carbon steels, 4140 for alloy steel, 304 and 316 for stainless steels, and O1, A2 and D2 for tool steels. Different designation systems have different rules. Do not apply a four-digit carbon-steel decoding rule to stainless or tool-steel labels.

On an order, identify the governing material specification, grade, delivery condition, form and dimensions. A short grade name alone does not specify strength, hardness, surface finish or dimensional tolerance. Ask for the required certification when traceability matters.

Use the current governing specification or the producer's grade sheet for composition limits and treatment requirements.

Decode carbon and alloy steel numbers; read stainless and tool grades separately

Decode carbon and alloy steel numbers; read stainless and tool grades separately

In the common AISI/SAE four-digit carbon and alloy steel system, the first two digits identify the steel series: 10 is plain carbon steel and 41 is chromium-molybdenum steel. The last two digits give nominal carbon content in hundredths of a percent.

304 and 316 stainless: their chemistries are similar, but 316 has an added alloying element, molybdenum. This improves resistance to chloride exposure and urban or industrial pollution compared with 304.

Tool-steel groups: O1 is an oil-hardening grade, A2 is an air-hardening grade, and D2 is a high-carbon, high-chromium grade. These labels describe different tool-steel groups; they are not carbon-percentage codes.

Cast iron is a separate iron-based material, and it can be machined

Cast iron is a separate iron-based material, and it can be machined

Cast irons generally contain more carbon than steels. Their properties depend strongly on how carbon appears in the structure. Gray iron contains graphite flakes and is useful for machine bases and housings where vibration damping matters; it is relatively brittle in tension. Ductile iron contains graphite nodules and can provide much greater ductility.

Casting creates the initial shape. Machining can then finish bores, faces and other accurate features. “Cast” and “machined” are not competing material identities. White cast iron contains substantial hard carbide and behaves differently from graphite-containing irons.

Nonferrous Metals

Aluminum, copper alloys, titanium, zinc and magnesium as choices for weight, conductivity, corrosion and manufacturing needs.

Choose the useful property, then check the tradeoff

Choose the useful property, then check the tradeoff

Nonferrous metals have a base other than iron. They are not all lightweight, corrosion-proof or easy to machine. A useful first comparison names both the benefit and the constraint.

Aluminum needs both an alloy label and a condition

Aluminum needs both an alloy label and a condition

Aluminum is sold in many alloys and conditions. Wrought products are shaped by processes such as rolling or extrusion. Their familiar four-digit labels are organized into series, including 1xxx through 7xxx. A series is a family, not a hardness scale: a higher first digit does not guarantee a harder or better part.

6061 and 7075 are common alloy labels to look for in supplier literature. Compare data for the actual product form and temper rather than treating either number as a complete specification. Cast aluminum uses a different designation system; do not transfer wrought-number rules to cast products.

A suffix such as -O or -T6 identifies a condition as part of an alloy-temper designation. It must stay attached to the alloy on an order. See Heat Treating Metals for why processing history matters.

The Aluminum Extruders Council defines the two tempers:

  • -O: “Annealed” — “Thermally treated to obtain the lowest strength temper.”
  • -T6: “Solution heat-treated and artificially aged.”

Use the governing product specification for an actual purchase.

Brass and bronze trade some of copper's conductivity for other properties; Practice

Brass and bronze trade some of copper's conductivity for other properties

Brass is principally a copper-zinc alloy. Depending on grade, it can offer useful forming or machining behavior for fittings, hardware and decorative parts. Do not assume every brass has the same machinability or suitability for contact with drinking water.

Bronze traditionally means copper-tin alloy, but commercial names also include aluminum bronze and silicon bronze. Bronzes serve in bearings, bushings, gears and corrosion-resistant hardware, depending on grade. The word “bronze” alone is not enough to buy a bearing material.

For a conductor, begin with conductivity requirements. For a bearing, begin with load, speed, lubrication and wear. The same copper-based family will not automatically win both comparisons.

Metal Stock Forms and Purchasing

Recognize stock shapes, distinguish size conventions, and specify enough information to receive the metal a design requires.

Stock is the material shape you buy before making the part

Stock is the material shape you buy before making the part

Stock is material supplied in a usable starting form. Choose a shape that leaves enough material for the finished part and any cutting or machining allowance. Buying the smallest possible shape can fail if saw cuts, surface cleanup or distortion consume the allowance.

Sheet, plate, bar and structural shapes use different size descriptions

Sheet, plate, bar and structural shapes use different size descriptions

What to specify for common forms
FormDescriptionSize information
Sheet and plateFlat products; plate is the thicker category under the applicable product convention.Thickness, width and length. There is no single sheet/plate boundary for every metal and specification.
BarSolid round, flat, square or hexagonal cross-section.Diameter, width and thickness, square side, or hex size across flats, plus length.
Structural shapesAngle, channel and I-shaped sections place material where the structure needs it.Section designation and dimensions, thicknesses and length under the relevant product system.

A gauge number is a sizing convention, not a universal thickness. Specify actual thickness and units when ambiguity would affect the part. Check the applicable material's gauge table if a drawing uses gauge.

Tube dimensions and nominal pipe sizes are not interchangeable

Tube dimensions and nominal pipe sizes are not interchangeable

Tube is commonly ordered by outside dimensions and wall thickness: outside diameter for round tube, or outside width and height for rectangular tube. State the length too.

Pipe commonly uses a nominal pipe size and a wall schedule. A nominal size is a label; it need not equal the measured outside or inside diameter. Use the applicable pipe dimensions table before calculating a fit or flow area. Structural tubing is not automatically suitable for pressure service.

Measure the right boundary. For round hollow stock, inside diameter equals outside diameter minus twice the wall thickness. The wall occurs on both sides of the opening.

Hot-rolled and cold-finished describe processing, not alloy identity

Hot-rolled and cold-finished describe processing, not alloy identity

Hot-rolled steel is shaped at high temperature and commonly arrives with mill scale and comparatively broad dimensional tolerances. Cold rolling or other cold-finishing operations can improve surface finish and dimensional control. Cold-finished bar may be drawn rather than rolled.

Cold work can increase strength and leave residual stresses. Removing material can release those stresses and move the part. Cold-finished does not automatically mean precision ground, and neither term guarantees a particular tolerance without a product specification.

A purchase description must preserve alloy, condition, form and size; Checklist

A purchase description must preserve alloy, condition, form and size

An illustrative request is: 6061-T6 aluminum round bar, 25 mm nominal diameter × 500 mm cut length, quantity 2. Before purchasing, add the governing product specification, diameter and cut-length tolerances, finish and certification requirements that the design needs. This example is not a complete engineering specification.

  • Identify the alloy and required delivery condition from the design.
  • State the stock form and every necessary dimension with units.
  • Allow for saw kerf (material lost in the cut), cleanup and final machining.
  • State tolerances, quantity and any required material certificate.
  • Compare availability, cut charges, shipping and minimum orders as well as material price.
  • Confirm that the delivered label and documents match the order before using the stock.

Drops or remnants are leftover pieces that may reduce cost. Their identification and usable dimensions still need checking.

Estimate mass from cross-sectional area, length and density; Practice

Estimate mass from cross-sectional area, length and density

For uniform stock, mass = cross-sectional area × length × density. For a solid round bar, area = πd²/4. For round tube, area = π(D² − d²)/4, where D is outside diameter and d is inside diameter. Subtract the opening before multiplying by length.

Use the density for the actual alloy from a material data sheet and keep units consistent. A result in kilograms is mass; weight as a force also requires gravitational acceleration. See Geometry, Mass, and Force and Dimensional Analysis.

Heat Treating Metals

Choose a conditioning process by its goal: soften, refine structure, harden, improve toughness or reduce residual stress.

Heat treatment is conditioning: change structure to change properties

Heat treatment is conditioning: change structure to change properties

Heat treatment uses controlled heating, holding and cooling to change a metal's internal structure. It belongs to Conditioning in Manufacturing Process Categories. It can change properties without intentionally changing the overall shape, although distortion can occur.

The result depends on alloy, starting condition, section thickness and the complete cycle. Heating more or cooling faster is not automatically better. A treatment that strengthens one alloy can soften another.

Supporting detail: Addendum 7 — Steel microstructures are not the four steel families.

Anneal to soften; normalize suitable steels to refine structure

Anneal to soften; normalize suitable steels to refine structure

Annealing is a family of treatments used to soften material, restore ductility or adjust its structure. Full annealing of suitable steels includes slow cooling; annealing cold-worked metal can form new grains and restore formability. Not every anneal uses the same temperature or cycle.

Normalizing heats suitable steel into its austenitic range and then cools it in air. It can refine and make the structure more uniform after earlier processing. Air cooling and slow furnace cooling produce different results.

Choose the process for the desired condition. Do not apply one universal temperature offset to all steels.

Quench hardening usually needs tempering afterward; Practice

Quench hardening usually needs tempering afterward

Quench hardening heats suitable steel to form austenite, holds as required, then cools fast enough to obtain the intended hardened structure. It commonly raises hardness and strength but can reduce ductility and toughness. Cracking and distortion are possible.

Tempering reheats hardened steel below the temperature at which austenite begins to form. It adjusts the balance of hardness and toughness and reduces some quenching stresses. It is not simply heating “just below” one critical temperature; the required cycle depends on grade and target properties.

These concepts explain specifications; they are not operating instructions. Use the approved process for the identified material, equipment and quench medium.

Practice the purpose of tempering

Surface hardening puts wear resistance near the outside

Surface hardening puts wear resistance near the outside

A part may need a hard wear surface and a tougher interior. Carburizing adds carbon near the surface of suitable steel before hardening. Nitriding introduces nitrogen to develop a hard surface layer. These change surface chemistry.

Induction hardening heats a surface region electrically and then quenches it. It uses the steel's existing composition rather than adding carbon. It therefore needs a suitable starting steel. Case depth, surface hardness and core properties must be specified together.

Cold work strengthens; stress relief addresses locked-in stresses; Practice

Cold work strengthens; stress relief addresses locked-in stresses

Work hardening, also called strain hardening, occurs as metal is permanently deformed during cold working. It commonly raises strength and hardness while reducing remaining ductility. “Cold” here means below the relevant recrystallization range, not necessarily cold to the touch.

Stress relief reduces residual stresses left by processing. These are stresses that remain without an external load. The goal is often dimensional stability during later machining or service. Stress relief is not automatically a full softening anneal and does not guarantee zero distortion.

Practice restoring formability

Aluminum strengthening does not use steel's martensite route

Aluminum strengthening does not use steel's martensite route

Some aluminum alloys can be strengthened by solution treatment, quenching and aging. Solution treatment puts selected elements into solid solution; subsequent aging develops small particles that obstruct deformation. Aging can occur naturally or through controlled heating, depending on the alloy and required condition.

Solid-solution strengthening and precipitation strengthening are different mechanisms. Dissolved atoms provide the first; particles of another phase provide the second. Other aluminum alloys obtain much of their strength through cold work. Annealing can soften cold-worked material.

An aluminum temper describes its processing condition. It is not synonymous with the steel process called tempering. Preserve suffixes such as -O and -T6 when reading or writing a material description; consult the relevant alloy-temper specification for their exact meanings.

    Addendum 1. Why two pieces of the same alloy can behave differently

    Permanent deformation involves movement of crystal defects called dislocations. Grain boundaries, dissolved alloying atoms and small particles can obstruct that movement. Cold working and heat treatment change these obstacles. Composition alone therefore cannot tell you the strength of a finished piece.

    Addendum 2. Why connecting two different metals can change corrosion

    When dissimilar metals are electrically connected in a conducting liquid, such as salty water, galvanic corrosion can accelerate attack on the more active metal. Material pairing, exposed areas and the environment matter. Isolation and drainage can be as important as the alloy choice.

  7. Materials and Manufacturing Processes/Metals/Heat Treating Metals

    Addendum 7. Steel microstructures are not the four steel families

    Ferrite and austenite are different iron-based crystal phases. Cementite is iron carbide, Fe₃C, a hard, brittle phase. Pearlite is a layered arrangement of ferrite and cementite. Martensite can form from austenite during sufficiently rapid cooling in suitable steel. These terms describe structure, not the buying categories carbon, alloy, stainless and tool steel.

    Carbon can diffuse during slower transformations. Quenching can suppress those transformations and produce martensite. The outcome is not determined by cooling speed alone: chemistry and section size affect what forms throughout the part.
