Belt, Chain and Gear Drives

Choose a drive by shaft spacing, timing, speed and environment, then check its load rating and maintenance needs.

Three ways to carry rotation from one shaft to another

Three ways to carry rotation from one shaft to another

A drive transfers motion and turning effort from a driver to a driven shaft. Belts and chains bridge a gap. Gears engage directly and fit into a compact housing. Start with the job the drive must do, then choose the parts.

Gears, chains and timing belts are positive drives: engagement maintains a tooth-count ratio without ordinary frictional slip. Flat and V-belts are friction drives: their grip depends on friction and tension, so slip can change the speed ratio.

Positive engagement does not mean perfect positioning. Tooth clearance, elastic deflection and chain speed ripple still matter. For ratios, compound trains and losses, use Gear Ratios. For turning effort and power, use Torque and Power and Efficiency.

Explore which drives fit the job; Interactive

Explore which drives fit the job

Choose a shaft arrangement, a timing requirement, a speed range and an environment. Change one requirement at a time and watch which reason changes. “Close” allows a compact gear mesh; “separated” needs a flexible span. These are qualitative screening rules, not a component sizing tool.

Try this on paper: two separated parallel shafts must stay synchronized in a clean, lubricant-sensitive area. A timing belt is a useful starting candidate: its teeth maintain timing and the belt mesh needs no oil. Flat and V-belts cannot guarantee timing. An ordinary chain needs lubrication; a single gear pair cannot bridge the chosen gap without making the gears much larger.

Now remove the timing requirement: flat or V-belts become candidates. Bring the shafts close together inside a lubricated housing: gears become candidates. Each change removes a different constraint.

Turn a shortlist into a rated drive; Checklist; Practice

Turn a shortlist into a rated drive

  • Record shaft directions, center distance, available space and adjustment travel.
  • Specify input speed, required output speed, power and torque, including starts and shock loads.
  • Decide whether the job needs synchronized motion, low noise or accurate reversal.
  • Check temperature, abrasive dust, water and chemicals against the material and lubricant ratings.
  • Use the manufacturer's selection procedure to check size, service factor, wrap or tooth engagement, shaft loads and bearings.
  • Plan inspection access, replacement parts, lubrication and the total cost over the drive's life.

A bigger pitch is not a complete strength rating. Width, material, speed, tooth count and duty also matter. A drive that fits the space can still fail the load check.

Belt Drives

Distinguish friction belts from timing belts, read belt and pulley terminology, and recognize common wear patterns.

Flat and V-belts grip; timing belts engage teeth

Flat and V-belts grip; timing belts engage teeth

Flat belt
A broad, flat band transmits force through friction against a pulley. Useful for smooth, quiet motion and long spans when the drive is designed for them. Tracking and tension matter.
V-belt
A trapezoidal section runs in a matching V-groove. Contact at the sides gives a wedging action that increases grip. The belt should not rely on rubbing the bottom of the groove.
Timing / synchronous belt
Teeth on the belt engage matching grooves in the pulleys. Load is carried by this engagement, so the drive keeps its tooth-count ratio without frictional slip in normal operation.

Timing does not come from extra tension. It comes from matching teeth. An undertensioned or overloaded timing belt can jump teeth or strip them; that is a fault, not its normal way of transmitting power. A notched V-belt remains a friction belt: relief notches for bending do not make it a timing belt.

Read length, tension and wrap before ordering

Read length, tension and wrap before ordering

Pulley / sheave
The wheel that carries a belt. “Sheave” is commonly used for a grooved belt pulley. A timing pulley has matching tooth grooves.
Pitch and pitch length
Timing-belt pitch is the spacing between adjacent tooth centers along the pitch line. Pitch length is the loop length along that reference line, near the tensile cords. It is not the outside circumference. Match pitch and tooth profile to the pulley.
Tension
The pulling force along the belt. The difference between the loaded and return spans transmits torque. Installation tension keeps the belt seated; too little encourages slip or tooth jumping, and too much loads belts and bearings.
Wrap angle
The angle through which the belt contacts a pulley. Less wrap gives a friction belt less contact and a timing belt fewer teeth in engagement. Check the smaller pulley in particular.

Catalogs may specify inside, outside, datum, effective or pitch lengths. Use the length convention and measuring method for that belt family; do not substitute a tape measurement of the outside for the catalog value.

One-stage reminder: nout = nin × Ddriver / Ddriven using matching effective/pitch diameters and assuming no slip. For timing belts, use tooth counts N in place of diameters. Write the reduction as i:1, where i = input speed / output speed. Follow Gear Ratios for the ratio method and Rotation and Linear Speed for belt linear speed.

Here N means teeth and n means rotational speed. Some class slides use n for teeth and ω for angular speed; check the quantity, not just the letter.

Choose a belt for the load and environment

Choose a belt for the load and environment

Belts can span separated shafts, soften vibration and avoid oil at the transmission contact. Flat and V-belts suit jobs where a small speed variation is acceptable. Choose a timing belt when the shafts must stay synchronized. Compare the alternatives in Belt, Chain and Gear Drives.

Check belt section or tooth profile, width, minimum pulley size, available length, wrap, power rating, temperature and chemical compatibility. There is no single maximum speed or temperature range for every belt. Reinforcement cords limit stretch, but the belt still needs correct alignment and installation tension.

Some specially designed flat-belt layouts connect nonparallel shafts. That does not make misalignment acceptable in an ordinary V-belt or timing-belt installation. Frictional slip may soften an overload, but it can also generate heat and destroy the belt; it is not a guaranteed overload-protection device.

Explore how pulley size and spacing change belt length and wrap; Interactive; Practice

Explore how pulley size and spacing change belt length and wrap

Use pitch diameters D (large) and d (small), and center distance C in one length unit. For an open belt, the approximate pitch length is L ≈ 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D − d)²/(4C). The last term is the correction for unequal pulleys. Increasing C increases length and brings small-pulley wrap closer to 180°; the no-slip speed ratio stays D/d.

Real belts come in standard lengths from the manufacturer's catalog. Pick the nearest stock length in the correct length convention, then use “Enter L → find C” to estimate the required shaft spacing. Chain Drives

Recognize chain components, decode ANSI pitch numbers, and distinguish ISO chain series before choosing sprockets.

Sprocket teeth engage a chain of moving joints

Sprocket teeth engage a chain of moving joints

A chain drive is a positive drive: sprocket teeth engage the chain instead of relying on frictional grip. It can bridge separated shafts and carry substantial load, but its joints and sprockets need attention.

Pitch, p
The center-to-center distance between adjacent chain pins. A chain's pitch must match its sprocket.
Roller
A sleeve that turns around a bush as it contacts a sprocket tooth. Some small chains are bush chains and have no separate roller.
Link
A connecting unit of the chain. Ordinary roller chain alternates inner links, carrying bushes and rollers, with outer links, carrying pins and side plates.
Sprocket
The toothed wheel that engages the chain. Tooth count sets the speed relationship; tooth shape and width must suit the chain series.
Master / connecting link
The link used to join the ends into a loop. Use the specified type and installation method; retention and strength depend on the product.
Read #25, #35 and #40 as pitch codes

Read #25, #35 and #40 as pitch codes

For the common ANSI short-pitch chain numbers here, remove the final digit of the base number and divide the remaining number by 8 to get pitch in inches. Thus #40 uses 4/8 in, not 40/8 in. #100 uses 10/8 in.

Positive engagement still permits wear and speed ripple; Practice

Positive engagement still permits wear and speed ripple

Chain is worth considering when separated shafts need a fixed average ratio and lubricant and noise are acceptable. Select it for the actual power, speed, starts, shock loads and environment. Tensile breaking strength is not an allowable continuous working load.

One-stage reminder: nout = nin × Ndriver / Ndriven; reduction i = Ndriven / Ndriver, written i:1. N counts teeth and n is rotational speed. For the reasoning and further stages, use Gear Ratios.

The chain wraps a sprocket as a series of straight links, forming a polygon rather than a perfectly smooth circle. This chordal action produces speed ripple, especially with small sprockets. Backlash and elastic stretch also limit precise positioning, even without ordinary slip.

  • Wear elongation: pin/bush wear increases joint clearance and measured chain length. The worn chain rides poorly on sprocket teeth and can jump. Take-up removes slack; it does not restore worn joints.
  • Stiff or seized joints: check lubrication reaching the joints, contamination and corrosion.
  • Fractured plates or pins: investigate fatigue, overload, misalignment and connecting-link installation.
  • Hooked or uneven sprocket teeth: inspect the chain and sprockets together and follow replacement limits for that drive.

Use the specified lubricant, alignment and slack. A chain is not installed taut like a belt. Set inspection intervals and wear limits from the maker's guide; isolate the drive before measuring or servicing it. See Equipment Operating Procedure.

Gear Drives and Tooth Geometry

Compare spur and helical gears, identify tooth dimensions, and match pitch and pressure angle before choosing a gear pair.

Spur teeth engage straight across; helical teeth engage progressively

Spur teeth engage straight across; helical teeth engage progressively

Gears transmit rotation by direct tooth contact. They make a compact positive drive, but accurate mounting, tooth compatibility and lubrication matter. This page compares external spur and helical gears on parallel shafts.

Spur gear
Teeth run parallel to the shaft. It is comparatively simple to make and produces no axial thrust from the ideal tooth mesh. Engagement can be noisy at higher speed.
Helical gear
Teeth run at a helix angle around the gear. Contact enters progressively across the face and overlapping teeth share load. This generally gives smoother, quieter operation, with added manufacturing cost.

The inclined helical tooth force also pushes along the shaft. That axial thrust needs a suitable bearing arrangement. A spur gear still loads its bearings radially; “no axial thrust” does not mean “no bearing force.”

The driver supplies power. The pinion is the smaller gear in a pair; it can be the driver or the driven member. An idler passes motion between gears and still needs a shaft or other bearing support. Follow Gear Ratios for direction, tooth-count ratios and compound trains.

Measure teeth from the pitch circle

Measure teeth from the pitch circle

Pitch diameter, d
The diameter of the reference pitch circle. In a standard unshifted pair at its standard center distance, the pitch circles roll together without slipping.
Module, m
Metric tooth size: m = d/N, with d in millimeters and N teeth. Larger module means larger teeth.
Diametral pitch, Pd
Inch-based tooth size: Pd = N/d, with d in inches. More teeth per inch of pitch diameter means smaller teeth. Do not confuse it with circular pitch, the distance along the pitch circle between successive teeth.
Pressure angle, α
The angle between the line of action (the contact-force direction, neglecting friction) and the common tangent to the pitch circles at the pitch point. It describes tooth geometry, not the helix angle.
Addendum / dedendum
Radial tooth height from pitch circle to tip / radial depth from pitch circle to root. Neither is a diameter difference by itself.
Backlash
Clearance between mating tooth flanks. It allows clearance in the mesh but creates lost motion when the load reverses.

Supporting detail: Addendum 7 — Check the size definitions with one gear.

Matching pitch is necessary, but not sufficient

Matching pitch is necessary, but not sufficient

Ordinary spur gears intended to mesh must share module or diametral pitch and pressure angle. Matching outside diameter or tooth count does not establish compatibility. Check the tooth system, center distance, clearance and manufacturer's mating information too.

For external helical gears on parallel shafts, match tooth size, pressure angle and helix angle. Use one right-hand and one left-hand gear (teeth slanting in opposite directions), and confirm the manufacturer's mating specification. Equal tooth counts are not required.

A larger tooth size can support more load when other conditions are comparable, but pitch alone cannot determine capacity, backlash or achievable reduction. Material, face width, heat treatment, alignment and speed remain part of the design.

Gear damage tells you where the load went

Choose gears for compact packaging, a positive ratio and a supported shaft arrangement. Choose helical teeth when smoothness and noise justify their cost and thrust bearings. Check tooth bending strength, surface durability, speed, lubricant, seals and bearing loads before approving a pair.

Gear Ratios

Follow tooth counts through pairs, idlers and compound trains; multiply stage efficiencies and choose gears to meet speed and torque limits.

Experiment: trade speed for turning effort; Interactive

Experiment: trade speed for turning effort

Predict first: a 20-tooth gear turns at 120 rpm clockwise and drives a 40-tooth gear. Will the larger gear turn faster or slower? What changes if you add an idler between them?

All three examples use the same ideal input: 120 rpm and 2 N·m. Input power is 25.13 W, and ideal output power is also 25.13 W. More output torque comes with less speed.

With each actual mesh at 96% efficiency, the pair delivers 3.84 N·m and 24.13 W; the idler train delivers 3.6864 N·m and 23.16 W; the compound train delivers 7.3728 N·m and 23.16 W. Their output speeds remain 60, 60 and 30 rpm. This comparison charges a loss at both idler meshes.

Count the teeth to find the speed

Count the teeth to find the speed

The driver supplies motion; the driven gear receives it. For one external gear mesh, each tooth passing on the driver advances the driven gear by one tooth space. A gear with twice as many teeth needs twice as many passing teeth to complete a turn.

Output speed = input speed × driver teeth ÷ driven teeth. A 20-tooth driver at 120 rpm driving 40 teeth gives 120 × 20 ÷ 40 = 60 rpm. External meshing reverses the direction.

This lesson uses reduction ratio i = input speed ÷ output speed, with speed magnitudes. A 2:1 reduction means two input turns for one output turn. Ratios above 1 reduce speed; ratios below 1 increase it. At zero speed, use tooth counts to find the ratio; do not divide 0 by 0.

Notation map: this page's original equations use N for teeth and n for speed. The course uses n for teeth, ω for angular velocity and τ for torque. Thus page N = course n; page speed n is written ω in the additions below. GR and i name the same reduction ratio.

In course notation, using magnitudes: GR = nout/nin = dout/din = ωin/ωout = τout/τin for an ideal single pair. Here d is pitch diameter; the torque equality assumes no losses. For a compound train, multiply the stage ratios; the first and last tooth counts alone do not give the overall ratio.

An idler and a compound shaft do different jobs

An idler and a compound shaft do different jobs

One mesh reverses

A → B turns the output opposite to the input, viewed from the same end of each shaft.

An idler cancels

A → I → B reverses twice. I affects its own speed and the spacing, but its tooth count cancels from the end-to-end ratio.

Compound stages multiply

A → B, then C → D, with B and C fixed together: i = (NB/NA) × (ND/NC).

Same shaft means the same angular speed. A large B and a small C fixed to one shaft make the same number of turns in the same direction. Their rim speeds differ because their radii differ. They do not mesh with each other.

In the compound example, 20 → 40 teeth halves the speed. A second 20 → 40 stage halves it again: 120 → 60 → 30 rpm. The two external meshes make the final output turn in the same direction as the input.

An idler is not another reduction stage. Drop its tooth count when finding the end-to-end ratio, but count every external mesh for direction: odd means opposite; even means the same, viewed from the same end of the parallel shafts. Do not drop a gear that shares a shaft with a different gear in a compound train.

More torque costs speed; it does not create power

More torque costs speed; it does not create power

Torque is turning effort. In this ideal, lossless train at steady load, output torque magnitude = input torque magnitude × reduction ratio. A 2:1 reduction changes 120 rpm and 2 N·m into 60 rpm and 4 N·m. The 4:1 compound example gives 30 rpm and 8 N·m.

Rotational power is torque × angular speed: P = T × 2πn/60 when T is in N·m and n is in rpm. Both sides of either example carry 25.13 W ideally. Real friction and other losses leave less output power. Tooth strength, bearings, shafts, lubrication and the motor all limit usable torque.

At zero speed, mechanical power is zero. A loaded, stationary gear train can still carry torque. The lab shows ideal static torque balance in that case; it does not model motor stall behavior or promise a safe load.

Stage efficiencies multiply; losses accumulate

Stage efficiencies multiply; losses accumulate

Each stage receives only the power that survived the stage before it. Use decimal efficiencies and multiply: ηtotal = η1 × η2 × η3. Never add or average them. See Power and Efficiency for the general meaning of efficiency.

At 98% per stage, three stages give 0.98³ = 0.941192: about 5.88% total loss, not 6%. Fewer, higher-quality stages often beat many cheap stages for efficiency, provided they can meet the ratio and load requirements.

Positive drives keep the speed ratio; losses reduce torque

Positive drives keep the speed ratio; losses reduce torque

A positive drive keeps input and output linked in a fixed ratio without slipping. Gears, chains and timing belts are positive drives. A slipping flat belt or V-belt is not. Follow Rotation and Linear Speed for the connection from rotation to belt travel.

At a stated steady input speed and load, use magnitudes:

ωout = ωin/i
τout = τin × i × ηtotal
Pout = ηtotal × Pin

The teeth still set the speed exactly in this model. Efficiency reduces output torque from its ideal value. It does not reduce output rpm as well. Use matching torque units on both sides: lbf·ft or N·m. The course conversion is 1 rpm ≈ 0.1047 rad/s when angular speed in rad/s is needed.

Count stages for ratio, meshes for direction and stated losses

Count stages for ratio, meshes for direction and stated losses

For a simple train A → I → B, the idler cancels from (NI/NA) × (NB/NI) = NB/NA. The two external meshes still reverse direction twice.

Follow the loss assumption in the problem. Real meshes lose some power, but a problem may explicitly ignore it. If every actual mesh is 96% efficient, A → I → B has ηtotal = 0.96². If a whole stage including its idler is assigned 96%, use that stage value once. If η = 1.00 is stated, ignore losses. Do not infer the loss count from the reduction-stage count when an idler is present.

Choose teeth, then check both output limits; Checklist; Practice

Choose teeth, then check both output limits

  • Record input speed and torque, the output speed ceiling and torque floor, available integer tooth counts, maximum ratio per stage, and the loss assumption.
  • Use the speed ceiling to find i ≥ ωin/ωceiling. With at most R:1 per stage, k reduction stages can provide at most Rk:1.
  • For each candidate stage count k, calculate ηtotal and check the torque bound i ≥ τfloor/(τin × ηtotal). Use the larger of the speed and torque bounds.
  • Choose whole-tooth pairs within the range and per-stage limit. Connect successive pairs by shared shafts. Multiply their actual ratios; do not assume a rounded target is achievable.
  • Calculate output speed and torque, keeping full precision for both limit checks. Check direction by counting external meshes. An added idler may require recalculating losses.

To choose the faster of feasible designs at the same input speed, compare their actual overall ratios: the smaller one is faster. A feasible design is not automatically the fastest possible design.

    Addendum 3. Derive the inverse and check speed and wrap

    Multiply the length approximation by 4C: 4CL = 8C² + 2π(D + d)C + (D − d)². Set B = 4L − 2π(D + d), giving 8C² − BC + (D − d)² = 0. The quadratic formula gives C = [B + √(B² − 32(D − d)²)]/16. Use this larger root and require C > (D + d)/2 so the pitch circles are separated. An impossible length produces no valid layout.

    Assuming no slip, n_small / n_large = D/d and v = πd n_small. With d in mm, divide v by 1000 to get m/min; with d in inches, multiply by 0.0254. Speed n is in r/min; N remains the symbol for teeth.

    The tangent-span angle is α = asin((D − d)/(2C)); small-pulley wrap is θ = 180° − 2α, with α expressed in degrees. Less wrap means less grip. The drawing uses circular arcs and tangent spans; the length equation approximates that geometry. Its error grows as (D − d)/(2C) grows, so very unequal, closely spaced pulleys need a more exact calculation for final selection.

    The normal plane cuts across a tooth at right angles to its slant; the transverse plane cuts across the gear at right angles to its shaft. Module (tooth size) and pressure angle can be specified in either plane, so compare values measured in the same plane. For this helical pair, match normal module, normal pressure angle and helix-angle magnitude, with opposite helix hands.

  10. Engineering Fundamentals/Motion and Rotation/Gear Ratios

    Addendum 10. Why tooth counts work, and what the dashed circles mean

    The gears here share one tooth size, called module, and a 20° pressure angle. Their pitch diameters are proportional to tooth count. The dashed pitch circles roll together at equal tangential speed, so nA × NA = −nB × NB when clockwise is positive on both shafts. Here n is signed rotational speed and N is tooth count.

    Tooth flanks are involute curves. Actual tooth surfaces can slide during contact even though the pitch circles roll. The drawing samples those curves, simplifies the tooth roots and adds a small visual clearance. It is a motion model, not a manufacturing drawing.

    Addendum 12. Why adding losses is only an approximation

  13. Engineering Fundamentals/Motion and Rotation/Gear Ratios

    Addendum 13. Follow both stages of one loaded train

    A 24-tooth driver at 1440 rpm and 4 lbf·ft drives 48 teeth. That shaft also carries a 15-tooth driver for a 45-tooth output. Both stages are 96% efficient, with no other losses. After stage 1: 720 rpm and 4 × 2 × 0.96 = 7.68 lbf·ft. The shared shaft carries that same 720 rpm into stage 2. Final output: 1440/(2 × 3) = 240 rpm and 7.68 × 3 × 0.96 = 22.1184 lbf·ft, versus 24 lbf·ft ideally. Overall efficiency is 0.9216.

  14. Engineering Fundamentals/Motion and Rotation/Gear Ratios

    Addendum 14. Worked design: a winch with a speed ceiling and torque floor

    A motor supplies 1680 rpm and 24 lbf·ft. The output must be at most 40 rpm and at least 950 lbf·ft. Available gears have 10–60 teeth, with unlimited copies; each reduction stage is at most 6:1 and 97% efficient. There are no idlers or other losses.

    1. Speed requires i ≥ 1680/40 = 42. Two stages give at most 6² = 36, so try three.
    2. Three stages give ηtotal = 0.97³ = 0.912673. Torque requires i ≥ 950/(24 × 0.912673) ≈ 43.3708. Torque controls.
    3. Choose 12 → 48, 15 → 45, and 16 → 60 teeth: i = 4 × 3 × 3.75 = 45. Every tooth count and stage ratio meets its limit.
    4. ωout = 1680/45 ≈ 37.33 rpm ≤ 40 rpm. τout = 24 × 45 × 0.912673 = 985.68684 lbf·ft ≥ 950 lbf·ft. Both pass. Three external meshes make the output opposite to the input.

    This is one feasible train, not a claim of an optimum or a complete winch specification. For load force at a drum, continue to Torque; for travel speed, use Rotation and Linear Speed.
