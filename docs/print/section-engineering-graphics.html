Auxiliary and Detail Views

Choose an auxiliary view for true shape, a detail for enlargement, and a broken view for a long part.

Look perpendicular to a face to see its true shape

Look perpendicular to a face to see its true shape

A face appears in true size and shape when it is parallel to the projection plane: you are looking straight at it. “True size” still allows the drawing’s stated scale; it does not mean the image must be life size.

An inclined face can be seen on edge in one principal view and foreshortened in the others. Foreshortened means that a length appears shorter because it slopes away from the viewing plane. None of those views shows the complete true shape.

A primary auxiliary view projects directly from a principal view onto another plane. Choose that plane parallel to the inclined face. Review Orthographic Projection and the Glass Box and Choosing Drawing Views for the principal views.

Project from the edge view and transfer the missing depth

Project from the edge view and transfer the missing depth

  1. Find the principal view in which the face is a line. In the example, the front view shows the sloping face edge P–Q.
  2. Draw a fold or reference line parallel to P–Q. It represents the hinge between the parent and auxiliary projection planes.
  3. Draw thin projectors perpendicular to the fold line from the face’s corners.
  4. Transfer each corner’s depth from the corresponding principal view, using the same reference and drawing scale. Here the top view supplies a constant depth of 80.
  5. Connect corresponding points. The face is a 200-by-80 rectangle in the auxiliary view, although the top view shows its sloping length shortened to 160.

These are invented drawing units for the construction. The faint projectors and fold line explain the method; they are not physical edges on the part. The auxiliary view shows the face’s true shape, not necessarily the true shape of every other face.

Use a partial auxiliary view when only one face matters

Use a partial auxiliary view when only one face matters

A partial auxiliary view shows the useful inclined face and enough nearby geometry to identify it. Omit unrelated geometry that would make the view crowded, using an appropriate break boundary where the view is interrupted.

Keep the viewing reference, label, and scale clear. Partial means limited coverage; it does not change the projection direction. The construction above isolates the inclined face for that reason.

A detail enlarges a feature; a break shortens the view

A detail enlarges a feature; a break shortens the view

A detail view enlarges a selected region, usually identified by a boundary and letter in the parent view. Match the detail label and read its scale. Enlargement alone does not change the viewing direction or reveal hidden features.

A broken view omits a middle length of a long, substantially uniform part so its ends and features fit on the sheet. Break lines identify the omission. The actual part remains continuous, and dimensions across the break state its full size.

Do not confuse a broken view with a broken-out section, which exposes interior geometry. See Section Views. Never recover a dimension by measuring an illustration across a break.

Create auxiliary, detail, and break views from the parent in CAD; Practice

Create auxiliary, detail, and break views from the parent in CAD

Auxiliary view
Select the parent and a reference edge representing the inclined face edge-on. Use Auxiliary View in SOLIDWORKS or Auxiliary view in Onshape, check the direction, and place the view. For a partial view, limit the displayed region with the application’s crop or partial-view controls while retaining identifying references.
Detail view
Use Detail View in SOLIDWORKS or Detail view in Onshape. Define the region, place the enlarged view, and set its scale and label. Verify that dimensions reference the intended model edges.
Broken view
Use Break View in SOLIDWORKS or Break view in Onshape. Choose break direction and style, then position the two break boundaries. Check that no feature needed to define the part was omitted.

Review the result after every model revision. A generated view can be geometrically valid yet omit the feature the reader needs.

Choosing Drawing Views

Choose a descriptive front view and add only the views needed to make the shape unambiguous.

Choose the front view that explains the shape

Choose the front view that explains the shape

The front of a drawing is a deliberate choice. It need not be the side that happened to face you when you picked up the part.

Start with a stable, understandable orientation. Prefer a view that shows characteristic shapes directly and keeps important features out of hidden lines. For a stepped part, a profile that shows the steps is usually more useful than a plain rectangle.

Once the front is chosen, the other principal views follow from it. Keep their orientation and alignment consistent with Orthographic Projection and the Glass Box.

One or two views can be enough when the missing information is stated

One or two views can be enough when the missing information is stated

One view
A flat plate with a constant thickness can be defined by its face view and a thickness note, provided the outline, holes, and other requirements are completely specified. A circle alone does not say whether the part is a disk, a sphere, or a cylinder.
Two views
A constant-depth stepped shape can use a profile and a top view to establish the step widths, heights, and depth. A third rectangle may add no information. A simple cylindrical part can often be described with a side view and an end view.
Three views
Front, top, and a side view are useful when features change across all three directions. Each view should resolve a question the others leave open.

Three is a common arrangement, not a quota. Many simple parts need three or fewer because dimensions, symmetry, and repeated geometry remove the need to draw every side.

Add a view when it removes a real ambiguity

Add a view when it removes a real ambiguity

Try to imagine two different parts that fit the views you already have. If both are possible, identify the missing information. Add the view or callout that separates them.

Hidden interior
A section view can expose a bore or recess more clearly than a tangle of dashed lines. Its cutting-plane arrows tell you which way to look.
Inclined surface
An auxiliary view looks perpendicular to a sloping face so that face can appear in true shape. A fourth principal view may still foreshorten it.
Small feature
An enlarged detail view makes a crowded feature readable. Read its stated scale; do not compare its apparent size directly with the main view.

Check the view set before moving on to dimensions

  • Confirm that the front view exposes the most useful shape.
  • Account for width, height, and depth without guessing.
  • Locate every hole, step, slot, or recess in enough views to explain its direction and extent.
  • Check hidden edges and the projection convention for consistency.
  • Remove a redundant view only if the remaining views and callouts still define the part.

Then continue to Drawing Dimensioning for placing the size and location information. Use Measurement Language for units and Tolerances and Fits for permitted variation.

Drawing Terminology Review

Recognize drawing terms used when turning a drawing into a CAD model, then follow links for the full methods.

Identify the view before interpreting the shape

Identify the view before interpreting the shape

This short vocabulary review supports CAD certification preparation. It is general drawing language, not a list of guaranteed exam terms.

Orthographic view
A view made with parallel lines of sight perpendicular to the viewing plane. Front shows width and height; top shows width and depth; a side view shows depth and height. See Orthographic Projection and the Glass Box.
Isometric view
A pictorial view in which the three principal axes are equally foreshortened, so their projections are drawn 120° apart. It helps you visualize the part; use stated dimensions to model it. Compare isometric with dimetric and trimetric views in CAD Model Views and Checks.
Visible, hidden, and center lines
Visible lines show edges you can see; hidden lines show concealed edges; center lines identify axes or centers. Review their patterns in The Alphabet of Lines.
Read dimensions and callouts as instructions about features; Practice

Read dimensions and callouts as instructions about features

Dimension and extension lines
A dimension gives a size or location. A dimension line is a thin line, usually ending in arrowheads, that shows the extent of the measurement; extension lines carry the edges of the measured feature out to it. Read the value and units instead of measuring a picture. See Dimension Anatomy and Placement.
Diameter and radius
Diameter spans a circle through its center; radius runs from its center to its edge. The diameter symbol is Ø; R denotes radius. Confusing them changes the modeled size. See how holes and arcs are called out in Hole and Feature Callouts.
Hole and feature callout
A note describing a feature, possibly including quantity, size, depth, or thread information. A through hole passes through the material; a blind hole stops within it. See Hole and Feature Callouts.
Tolerance
The permitted variation in a specified dimension. Read the stated limits or applicable drawing note; do not assume that every dimension has the same tolerance. See Dimension Anatomy and Placement.

Distinguish a section, a detail, and an auxiliary view

  • Section view: shows the part as if cut by an imaginary plane so internal features can be read. A cutting-plane indication locates the cut; section lining identifies material crossed by it. See Section Views.

Identify the projection symbol before interpreting where the top and side views sit on the sheet.

Check the projection convention before naming a side view

Check the projection convention before naming a side view

First-angle and third-angle projection can show the same part from the same directions. What changes is where those views are arranged on the sheet.

Look for the projection symbol or an explicit note, often near the title block. Use it with the view labels. If the symbol and the views disagree, resolve the inconsistency before interpreting the part.

The projection symbol is two views of a shortened cone

The projection symbol is two views of a shortened cone

The trapezoid is the side view of a cone with its tip removed, called a frustum. The two concentric circles are its end view. Their arrangement tells you which projection method is being used.

Expect third-angle on U.S. mechanical drawings, then verify it; Practice

Expect third-angle on U.S. mechanical drawings, then verify it

Third-angle is the usual U.S. mechanical drawing convention. A shared convention lets the designer and the person making or inspecting the part interpret the layout the same way. It is a communication choice; neither method changes the part's geometry.

The drawing's declared convention is the deciding information. Country of origin, inch units, or a familiar company name is not a substitute for checking. Drawings cross borders, and CAD templates can use different defaults.

Orthographic Projection and the Glass Box

Connect a part's width, height, and depth across aligned views, then unfold the six faces of an imaginary glass box.

Each view looks straight at the part from one direction

Each view looks straight at the part from one direction

A pictorial helps you recognize a part. A multiview drawing helps you describe its shape without the apparent shrinkage of perspective. In orthographic projection, the lines of sight are parallel and perpendicular to the viewing plane.

Choose a front direction and keep the part fixed. Look straight from the front, from above, and from the right. Do not turn the part independently to make each view look convenient.

Front and rear
Show width and height; depth points toward or away from you.
Top and bottom
Show width and depth; height points toward or away from you.
Right and left
Show depth and height; width points toward or away from you.

A face parallel to its viewing plane shows its true shape at the drawing's scale. A face tilted to that plane is foreshortened; a face viewed exactly edge-on becomes a line.

The glass box unfolds into six related views

The glass box unfolds into six related views

Imagine the part inside a transparent box. For third-angle projection, trace what you see onto the box face between you and the part. Each face receives one principal view: front, rear, top, bottom, left, and right.

The names identify the direction you look from. A right-side view means you stood at the part's right, even if a different projection convention puts that view elsewhere on the sheet. Compare First-Angle and Third-Angle Projection.

Predict a view, then compare it with the same block part; Interactive

Predict a view, then compare it with the same block part

Choose a part and a viewing direction in the explorer. Before revealing the view, predict its outline and any step edges. Switch the layout convention and notice what moves on the sheet. The part and the named views stay the same.

These are simple solids with constant depth. Construction guides are teaching aids. The explorer includes the step edges visible or hidden from each selected direction; it is not a dimensioned manufacturing drawing.

Use alignment to follow one feature through the drawing; Checklist

Use alignment to follow one feature through the drawing

  • Identify the front view and confirm the projection convention.
  • Track the same left-to-right position between front and top: they share width.
  • Track the same elevation between front and side: they share height.
  • Compare front-to-back positions between top and side: they share depth.
  • Explain each solid or dashed edge using the shape seen in another view.

At the same scale, a feature cannot be one width in the front view and a different width in the top view. A 45° miter construction can transfer depth between top and side while sketching; that construction line is not an edge of the part.

Section Views

Read imaginary cuts, choose a section type, and distinguish cut material from voids and conventional exceptions.

Use an imaginary cut to expose the interior

Use an imaginary cut to expose the interior

A section view shows what is inside a part without making you untangle a group of hidden lines. Imagine cutting the part, removing the material between you and the cutting plane, and looking at what remains.

The cut changes the drawing representation, not the manufactured part. Hatch the material crossed by the plane and show the useful visible edges beyond it. Hidden lines are usually omitted in sections unless needed to make the shape clear.

Start with The Alphabet of Lines and Orthographic Projection and the Glass Box if the line types or viewing directions are unfamiliar. For a sloping face or a small feature, see Auxiliary and Detail Views.

Follow the cutting-plane arrows and match the letters; Checklist

Follow the cutting-plane arrows and match the letters

The cutting-plane line locates the imaginary cut in a parent view. Its arrows point in the direction you look into the remaining part. Matching letters connect that line to a label such as SECTION A–A; the letters do not specify a dimension.

Use a prominent cutting-plane line that extends beyond the part, with thin section lining in the resulting view. Line weight, dash pattern, and arrow size follow the drawing standard and template. These teaching diagrams use blue for cutting planes; color is not required to interpret a drawing.

  • Find both cutting-plane ends and the matching section label.
  • Follow the arrows to decide which side is removed from sight.
  • Trace solid material and voids separately across the plane.
  • Compare the resulting section with the parent view.
Full sections cut all the way through the part

Full sections cut all the way through the part

A full section uses one plane through the entire part. For this hollow cylinder, a plane through the axis exposes both walls and the bore. The bore is empty space and receives no hatching.

Half sections combine the exterior and interior

Half sections combine the exterior and interior

A half section is useful for a symmetric part. Imagine removing one quarter with two perpendicular cutting planes. The resulting view shows one half in section and the other half as an exterior view, usually divided by a center line.

It is not a full section with half its hatch erased. The unsectioned half shows the outside, and symmetry supplies the connection between the two halves.

Offset sections reach features that are not in one straight line

Offset sections reach features that are not in one straight line

An offset section jogs the cutting plane to pass through several features. Project the selected parallel portions into one section. The jogs help explain the cut location; they do not create physical edges on the part.

Read the parent view for the holes’ actual locations. Their appearance together in the section does not mean they lie in one straight row.

Broken-out sections expose only a local area

Broken-out sections expose only a local area

A broken-out section removes a limited patch to a chosen depth in an existing view. An irregular break line marks the boundary. Use it to expose a small passage or wall without adding a complete section view.

A separate cutting-plane line is normally unnecessary: the local boundary identifies the area. Depth still matters; a cut that stops before a bore will not reveal it. This differs from a broken view, which merely shortens a long part on the sheet.

Revolved sections show a cross section in place

Revolved sections show a cross section in place

A revolved section turns a cross-sectional slice 90° into the plane of the drawing and places it on the parent view. It is useful for the shape of a bar, spoke, or arm. Show the slice itself, rather than the features behind the cut.

Removed sections place the slice clear of the parent view

Removed sections place the slice clear of the parent view

A removed section places the cross section away from the parent view to keep it readable. Match its identifying letters to the cutting plane. Several removed sections can describe changes along a long part; keep each location unambiguous.

Aligned sections rotate an angled portion into the section plane

Aligned sections rotate an angled portion into the section plane

An aligned section uses intersecting cutting planes through features arranged around an axis. An angled portion is rotated into alignment before it is shown in the section. This makes radial features easier to read.

Do not infer angular spacing from the aligned result. In this disk, one small hole is rotated into the section plane; its original angular position is established by the top view.

Hatch cut material, with conventional exceptions

Hatch cut material, with conventional exceptions

Use thin, evenly spaced diagonal lines, commonly near 45° to the main outline. Change the angle when the hatch would be confused with an edge. Keep a part’s hatch consistent; distinguish adjacent assembly parts by changing hatch angle or spacing.

  • Voids: holes and open spaces are never hatched as solid material.
  • Ribs, webs, and spokes: a cut along their length generally leaves them unhatched so they do not look like a thick solid wall. A transverse cut through their thickness is hatched.
  • Shafts and fasteners in assembly sections: when cut lengthwise through their axes, solid shafts, bolts, nuts, washers, pins, and similar standard parts are commonly shown unsectioned. A transverse shaft section can be hatched; “never hatch a shaft” is too broad.

An unhatched region may be empty space, an uncut surface, or a conventionally unsectioned component. Use its outline and the other views to decide which.

The Alphabet of Lines

Read line patterns, weights, and endpoints to distinguish part edges from centers, dimensions, cuts, and alternate positions.

Read the pattern before deciding what the line means

Read the pattern before deciding what the line means

Every line on an engineering drawing has a job. Some describe the part; others tell you how to measure it, where to look, or how a view was made. Treating every line as a physical edge gives you the wrong part.

Use three clues together: pattern (solid or dashed), weight (thick or thin), and context (arrows, letters, numbers, and nearby features). A thin solid line alone could be a dimension, an extension, a leader, or section hatching.

Visible and hidden lines describe edges; center lines locate axes

Visible and hidden lines describe edges; center lines locate axes

Visible / object
A relatively thick, continuous line shows an edge or outline you can see from this viewing direction.
Hidden
A thin series of short, evenly spaced dashes shows an edge concealed by material. A hole may appear as a visible circle in one view and two hidden lines in another.
Center
A thin long-short pattern locates a center, axis, or symmetry reference. The center line through a cylinder is not a seam in the cylinder.
Phantom
A thin long-short-short pattern can show an alternate position, repeated detail, or a nearby part used as a reference. An open position of a lever does not mean there are two levers.

Read all views together. A line's visibility depends on where you are looking from. The same edge can be solid in one view and dashed in another.

Arrows, notes, and hatching tell you what to do with a view

Arrows, notes, and hatching tell you what to do with a view

Dimension
A thin line with arrowheads identifies the extent of a measurement. Read the associated value; do not measure the picture on your screen.
Extension
A thin line carries a feature's location out to a dimension line. A small gap usually separates it from the part outline.
Leader
A thin line connects a note to the feature it describes, usually with an arrow at an edge or a dot on a surface. Follow it to its endpoint.
Cutting plane
A prominent line marks an imaginary cut used to expose the interior. Read its arrows for the viewing direction and its letters to find the matching section, such as A–A. The line pattern can vary; arrows and identification matter.
Section / hatch
Thin parallel strokes inside a section view identify material crossed by the imaginary cut. They are not grooves machined into the part.
Break
A short freehand break or a long thin line with zigzags marks an omitted portion of a view. The actual part is not necessarily broken; a long uniform member can be shortened on paper while its dimension gives the full length.

For units and decimals, use Measurement Language. For allowable variation, use Tolerances and Fits. Creating the dimension callouts comes next in Drawing Dimensioning.

When lines coincide, do not invent a second feature; Practice

When lines coincide, do not invent a second feature

Where a visible edge and a hidden edge project to exactly the same place, read the visible line. Where a hidden edge and a center line coincide, read the hidden line; the center line may still be identifiable where it extends beyond the edge.

A crossing is different from an overlap. Two lines crossing on the sheet do not prove that two physical edges meet in space. Check another view before deciding.

Choosing Dimensions

Break a part into simple forms, define each feature’s size and location, and remove redundant requirements.

Ask what information is necessary to make this part

Ask what information is necessary to make this part

Start with the geometry, not with the dimension tool. Break the part into familiar forms: blocks, cylinders, holes, steps, slots and arcs.

Size dimensions
Describe the feature itself: a block’s length, width and thickness; a hole’s diameter; a slot’s width and length.
Location dimensions
Place that feature relative to other features or a chosen origin. A hole diameter alone does not say where the hole goes.

Locate a round hole by its center, not by a tangent edge that moves when the diameter changes. Choose reference surfaces that make sense for how the part fits and is checked. A formal datum has a specific meaning in geometric tolerancing; an arbitrary sketch origin is not automatically a datum.

Dimension a feature in the view that shows its shape

Dimension a feature in the view that shows its shape

Contour dimensioning keeps a feature’s dimensions with the view where its outline is clearest. Put a hole size on a view showing its circle. Put a step’s height where the step is visible. Use a section or another suitable view for an internal feature.

For the beginner drawings here, do not dimension to hidden lines: choose a view that exposes the feature. Keep the dimensions of one feature together when practical. See the Reading Drawings group in Engineering Graphics for projection, section views and view selection.

Build the dimension scheme in a deliberate order; Checklist

Build the dimension scheme in a deliberate order

  • Identify the simple forms and list the features that need size, location or orientation information.
  • Choose the views that show those features clearly. Establish useful reference edges or surfaces.
  • Add the main body sizes. Leave space for smaller feature dimensions nearer the outline.
  • Add feature sizes and end conditions, such as a hole diameter and THRU.
  • Locate the feature centers, steps and other boundaries from the chosen references.
  • Check completeness, remove duplicate requirements, and arrange the lines for readability.

Do not add dimensions just because the CAD system offers them. Review each imported model dimension for purpose, view, placement and duplication.

Give each independent requirement once

Give each independent requirement once

Completeness means every feature is defined. It does not mean every possible distance is dimensioned. A feature may need several dimensions, but the same size or location requirement should not be repeated in another view or restated by a closed chain.

A closed chain gives all the segments and their total as controlling dimensions. Remove one link, or make a useful derived value a reference dimension. Which link to remove depends on what the part must do.

Supporting detail: Addendum 10 — Why nominal arithmetic is not enough.

Parentheses mark information, not an extra acceptance requirement; Practice

Parentheses mark information, not an extra acceptance requirement

A reference dimension is shown in parentheses, such as (1.50). It helps the reader without imposing another manufacturing or inspection requirement. Use one when the derived value is useful; leave it out when it only adds clutter.

In the two-hole example, .80 and 2.30 can control the centers while (1.50) reports their nominal spacing. Parentheses do not mean that the physical distance cannot vary.

A basic dimension, shown in a rectangular box, is different: it supplies a theoretically exact value for a geometric tolerance scheme. It does not demand a physically perfect part. See Tolerances and Fits before interpreting limits or fits.

Dimension Anatomy and Placement

Read the lines, arrows and text of a dimension, then arrange them so the part stays easy to understand.

A dimension connects a number to a specific feature

A dimension connects a number to a specific feature

A drawing shows the shape. Its dimensions tell the reader how big the features are and where they belong. Read the stated value; do not measure the picture.

Dimension line
Runs in the direction being measured. Its arrowheads mark the extent of the dimension.
Extension line
Projects an edge, center or other point out to the dimension. Leave a visible gap at the part and continue a little beyond the last related dimension line.
Arrowheads and text
The arrows identify the endpoints; the text gives the value and any needed symbol. Use a consistent arrow style and readable text size.
Leader
A thin line connecting a note or callout to its feature. A hole leader ends with an arrow on the circle, directed toward its center.

Keep the dimension line separate from the part outline. A visible edge or centerline is not a substitute for a dimension line. For line types and views, see the Engineering Graphics collection’s Reading Drawings group.

Put dimensions outside the view, with the shortest nearest the part

Put dimensions outside the view, with the shortest nearest the part

Keep the outline clear. Put short dimensions close to the view and longer dimensions farther out. This also reduces the number of extension lines that cross dimension lines.

A dimension inside the view can be appropriate when it is clearer there or avoids an excessively long leader. The aim is clarity, not an empty interior at any cost.

Leave room for the lines, arrows and text

Leave room for the lines, arrows and text

Use at least 10 mm from the part outline to the first dimension line and 6 mm between successive parallel dimension lines as layout guides. Those are distances on the drawing sheet, regardless of the part’s scale. The familiar inch drafting guides are about 3/8 in and 1/4 in; they are rounded guides, not exact conversions.

Keep the gap at the part visible and extend the extension line slightly beyond the dimension line. About 2 mm of overrun is a useful drafting target. Increase spacing when the annotation needs more room.

Keep text readable from the bottom of the sheet

Keep text readable from the bottom of the sheet

Use unidirectional text for the ordinary linear dimensions here: the numbers read from the bottom even when the dimension line is vertical. Leave clear space around the number. On a horizontal line, the text may sit above it or in a break in it.

Stagger values on several parallel dimension lines so they are easier to separate. Keep lettering free of part outlines, extension lines and leaders.

Rearrange crossing lines before accepting clutter; Practice

Rearrange crossing lines before accepting clutter

Move dimensions or notes to avoid crossing dimension lines, extension lines and leaders wherever practical. Keep a leader short, with a clear arrow target and a short horizontal landing next to the note.

If extension lines must cross, normally leave them continuous. Do not introduce gaps that make their endpoints ambiguous. A break near an arrowhead can preserve its readability.

Read radii, diameters, slots, repeated features and hole notes, including the units and decimal places on an inch drawing.

Read the units before reading the numbers

Read the units before reading the numbers

Find the drawing’s unit note or title block first. On an inch drawing, .625 means 0.625 inch, not 625 inches and not millimeters. The unit need not be repeated beside every value when the drawing states it.

Decimal-inch dimensions conventionally omit the leading zero below one inch. Keep trailing zeros: .50 and .500 have the same nominal size, but may select different rows in the drawing’s general tolerance block. There is no universal “three decimals means ±.005” rule; read the actual block and any local tolerance.

An angle still needs its angle notation: 90°. Mixed units must be identified. For units and precision, use Measurement Language; for tolerance values, limits and fits, use Tolerances and Fits.

Use a diameter for a full circle and a radius for an arc

Use a diameter for a full circle and a radius for an arc

⌀ before a value means diameter. R before a value means radius. Thus ⌀.60 describes a full circular diameter of .60, while R.30 describes an arc radius of .30. The values are related, but the callouts describe different kinds of feature.

n X means the requirement applies in n places. A repeated size still needs an unambiguous pattern: center coordinates, spacing, or a fully defined bolt circle. A sketch that merely looks evenly spaced is not enough.

A round-ended slot needs size, location and direction

A round-ended slot needs size, location and direction

For a straight slot with semicircular ends, give its width and either its overall length or the distance between the end centers. Make the chosen meaning clear. Then locate the slot and establish its direction relative to the part.

Do not control the width, end radius, center spacing and overall length all at once. That repeats the relationships already built into the semicircular-ended shape. If a slot is not semicircular-ended, this shortcut does not apply.

Zoom, pan, and rotate change your view of the model

Zoom changes how large the model appears. Pan shifts it across the window. Rotate orbits the view so you can inspect another side. These viewing operations do not resize the part or move an assembly component.

Use zoom to fit to recover the whole model, zoom to window to enlarge a boxed region, and zoom to selection when you need a selected detail. Normal to looks straight at a selected plane

Normal to looks straight at a selected plane

Front, rear, top, bottom, left, and right are standard model directions. Normal to a planar face or sketch plane means looking along a direction perpendicular to it. An inclined face can therefore have a normal view that is different from every standard view.

Isometric, dimetric, and trimetric are angled parallel-projection views. The three principal axes have equal foreshortening in isometric, two equal in dimetric, and three different in trimetric. Extrude carries a cross-section in a straight direction

Extrude carries a cross-section in a straight direction

For a plain solid extrusion, select a closed sketch region and give it depth. A rectangular region makes a block; a circular region makes a cylinder. Without draft, the cross-section remains constant along the extrusion.

A boss/base extrusion creates or adds solid material. An extruded cut removes material where the extrusion intersects the target solid. Check the arrow, preview, and selected bodies. A cut pointing away from the part cannot remove material from it.

In Onshape, choose the result operation New, Add, or Remove. In SOLIDWORKS, check the boss/cut command and merge or feature scope settings. Adding touching material and creating a separate body are different intentions.

Feature-based Modeling

Plan a solid part as a sequence of features, choose stable references, and test whether the model survives a change.

Build the main shape, then add and remove material

Build the main shape, then add and remove material

A parametric model stores instructions for building a shape. Dimensions and relationships are inputs; the solid is the result. Change an input and rebuild to see the result update.

The base feature creates the initial solid. Later features add material, remove material, or modify faces and edges. For a simple spacer, start with its main cross-section and extrude it. For a stepped round knob, a revolved section may describe the main shape more directly. There is often more than one valid feature sequence.

A body is a connected piece of solid geometry. A SOLIDWORKS part file can contain multiple bodies. An Onshape Part Studio can create multiple parts from a shared feature list. Neither arrangement, by itself, defines assembly motion or assembly relationships.

Start with sketch dimensions and relations in the Engineering Graphics collection. Then choose extrude or revolve for a straight or rotational shape, or sweep or loft for a path or a changing cross-section.

The feature tree records a build sequence and its dependencies

The feature tree records a build sequence and its dependencies

Read the tree from the base feature toward the end. A feature that supplies a sketch, face, edge, or other reference is a parent; a feature that uses that reference is a child. A child can depend on more than one parent. Being later in the list does not, by itself, make a feature a direct child of every earlier feature.

For example: a plate extrusion supplies a top face for a hole-position sketch. A hole feature uses that sketch. A pattern uses that hole as its seed. Delete the hole and the pattern loses its seed; delete the plate and the sketch loses its support as well. Inspect the deletion dialog before accepting dependent deletions.

Rename features for their purpose, such as Main plate, Locating hole, and Hole row. Renaming a feature in the tree is different from renaming a referenced part file. File references need their own reference-aware workflow.

Rollback lets you inspect an earlier stage without deleting later work; Checklist

Rollback lets you inspect an earlier stage without deleting later work

Move the rollback bar above later features to inspect or edit an earlier stage. Return it to the end when checking the completed part. Rollback is a temporary stopping point in regeneration. Suppression instead leaves a selected feature in the tree but excludes it from the active model; dependent features may also be affected. Hiding changes visibility, not the solid's definition.

  • Roll back to inspect the geometry and references available at that point.
  • Edit the sketch for its dimensions or relations; edit the feature for its operation, depth, direction, or selected references.
  • Roll forward to the end, rebuild, and inspect the downstream results.

Choose references that express what must stay aligned

Choose references that express what must stay aligned

A reference plane locates a sketch or defines symmetry. A reference axis supplies a direction or center of rotation. Neither adds material. Use the origin and default planes when they express the design; add offset, angled, or mid-planes when needed. Reference geometry can also have parents.

Design intent is what should stay true when dimensions change. A centered plate should grow equally on both sides of its center plane. A hole required to pass through the plate should remain through when the plate gets thicker. A row of identical holes should share one seed definition and a spacing rule.

Put major shape and functional references early. Add small edge treatments later when they are finishing details. This is a useful starting order, not a rule that every fillet must be last: a large blend that defines the main shape may belong before shelling or other features.

  • Change one important dimension, rebuild, and check the intended relationships.

A driving dimension controls size; a driven dimension reports it

A driving dimension controls size; a driven dimension reports it

A driving dimension is a constraint: changing its value asks the solver to change the geometry while keeping the other rules. Length, angle, radius, diameter, and location dimensions can all supply constraints.

A driven or reference dimension measures a result already controlled elsewhere. It does not remove another DOF. For example, a circle whose size is set by a 24 mm driving diameter already has a 12 mm radius; a second driving radius dimension duplicates that control.

Use a relation for an enduring geometric rule and a dimension for the independent value you intend to change. Two lengths that must remain equal need an equal relation and one driving length, rather than two unrelated numbers that happen to match today.

Onshape automatically makes a newly added dimension driven if it would over-define the sketch. Check whether a displayed value is driving before expecting it to control geometry.

Fully define a sketch in a repeatable order; Checklist

Fully define a sketch in a repeatable order

  • State what must stay aligned, centered, equal, or tangent when the size changes.
  • Choose the plane and an origin reference that suit that intent. Confirm the working units.
  • Draw the simplest useful entities. Check the automatic relations and remove unintended ones.
  • Connect the intended endpoints and apply the geometric relations that describe the shape.
  • Anchor the sketch's position to the origin or other stable references; control its orientation.
  • Add independent driving dimensions for the remaining sizes and locations.
  • Inspect the remaining free points and curves. Drag gently to diagnose motion, then undo the diagnostic move.
  • Resolve conflicts before adding more constraints. Inspect the whole sketch, including construction geometry.
  • Change one intended driving value, check that the sketch behaves correctly, then restore the value.

Fully defined is a useful check, not proof of good design intent. Fixing a misplaced point can remove freedom without giving it the correct location. A sketch can be completely constrained by dimensions that make future edits awkward.

Relations preserve geometry when dimensions change

Relations preserve geometry when dimensions change

A geometric relation is a rule the sketch must continue to satisfy: two lines stay parallel, two circles keep one center, or an endpoint stays on a curve. Onshape generally calls these rules constraints; SOLIDWORKS calls them relations.

Choose the entities carefully. Selecting a circle's center is different from selecting its circumference. A relation can remove some motion while leaving other motion available. The diagrams show only the named rule, not a complete dimensioning scheme.

Coincident connects a point; midpoint puts it halfway along

Coincident connects a point; midpoint puts it halfway along

Coincident

Two points share a location, or a point lies on a selected line or curve. Joining two endpoints closes their gap. A point on a line can still slide along it unless another rule locates it.

Midpoint

A point stays halfway between a segment's endpoints. It is more specific than simply putting the point somewhere on the line. Onshape also supports midpoint on an arc.

Collinear shares a line; parallel shares only its direction

Collinear shares a line; parallel shares only its direction

Collinear

Two straight segments lie on the same infinite line. They may have a gap between them; collinear does not join their endpoints.

Parallel

Two lines keep the same direction. Their spacing, lengths, and positions along that direction still need other rules.

Horizontal and vertical use the axes; perpendicular uses another line

Horizontal

A line's endpoints have the same vertical coordinate. For two selected points, horizontal aligns their heights without joining them.

Vertical

A line's endpoints have the same horizontal coordinate. Two selected points can likewise be aligned vertically.

Two line directions form a right angle. They may rotate together while preserving that angle. The relation does not require their finite segments to meet.

Concentric shares a center; equal shares size; coradial shares both

Concentric shares a center; equal shares size; coradial shares both

Concentric

Circles or circular arcs share a center. They can have different radii. For arcs, their endpoints and sweep angles are not determined by this relation.

Equal

Selected line segments keep equal lengths; selected circles or circular arcs keep equal radii. Equal does not move their centers together. Equal arc radii do not imply equal arc lengths when the sweep angles differ.

Coradial

Two circular arcs share both center and radius: they belong to the same underlying circle. They need not touch or have equal sweep angles. SOLIDWORKS names this relation Coradial. In Onshape, concentric plus equal radius expresses this condition.

Tangent controls direction at contact; Fix holds a reference in place

Tangent

A line and a circle touch without crossing: the line is perpendicular to the radius at contact. Curves meeting tangentially have a common tangent direction. Tangency alone does not necessarily join the endpoints you intended; add endpoint coincidence when a continuous profile needs it.

Sweeps and Lofts

Use a sweep to carry a section along a path and a loft to transition through ordered sections, with references that control the result.

A sweep needs a profile and a path

A sweep needs a profile and a path

A profile describes the cross-section; a path describes where it travels. A circular profile swept around a smooth bend makes a bent solid rod. An annular profile can make a tube. For a basic sweep, keep the section size constant and choose how it stays oriented along the path.

Place the profile on a plane normal (perpendicular) to the path at its start for an easy first construction. Constrain its position to the path. A shape that looks connected in one view can still be separated in three dimensions. Select the complete intended path, not a neighboring model edge.

A sweep can create material or remove it from an existing part. Advanced sweep options can control twist or scaling; a constant circular section is a simpler starting case. See Onshape: Sweep.

A loft passes through profiles selected in order

A loft passes through profiles selected in order

A loft transitions between two or more sections. Put sketches on suitable planes, then select the sections in the order the shape should pass through them. A broad oval transitioning to a smaller oval is a simple example; the section size changes along the length.

Use a guide curve when the profiles alone do not control the shape between them. For a straightforward boundary guide, constrain it to meet every profile at the intended boundary point. A Pierce relation can locate a sketch point where a curve crosses that sketch plane. Do not accept a connection solely because it looks right in an isometric view.

Match corresponding locations around successive profiles. If one section's corner connects to an unrelated corner on the next, the loft can twist. Inspect connection handles and a section view before adding more profiles.

Judge remaining motion against the design intent

Judge remaining motion against the design intent

A successful rebuild means the CAD system can solve the model. It does not mean the model describes the mechanism you intended. Try moving each component and name the motion that should remain.

Under-mated means freedom remains. That is a defect for a loose cover, but rotation at a lever pivot can be intentional. Over-mated or conflicting constraints ask for relationships that the solver cannot satisfy together. A redundant constraint repeats an existing condition; it adds no useful location and can complicate diagnosis.

For a shaft with concentric axes and seated shoulder faces, one rotation remains. Adding another mate that merely keeps those axes parallel is redundant. Adding an angle mate to lock that remaining rotation may be correct for a stationary shaft and wrong for a rotating lever. Review Assembly Files and Mates for the geometry.

Repair the cause of a mate error before adding more mates

3D Printing from CAD

Export a solid model, choose how its layers carry load, and inspect the sliced toolpaths before printing.

Build the part by adding layers

Build the part by adding layers

Additive manufacturing makes a shape by adding material. In fused deposition modeling (FDM), a printer feeds thermoplastic through a heated nozzle and deposits it along paths, much like a controlled hot-glue gun.

A slicer divides the model into horizontal cross sections and plans the paths for each layer. The nozzle and build surface move relative to one another to build the next layer. A CAD model describes the desired shape; the sliced file describes how a particular printer will make it.

This page focuses on filament printing. Resin printing uses a different process and requires its own preparation, handling, and finishing procedures.

Export the model, then check its size in the slicer; Checklist

Export the model, then check its size in the slicer

  • Finish and save the editable CAD model. Check that the intended part is a closed solid; see Feature-based Modeling.
  • Export the intended body as STL or 3MF if supported by both programs. Inspect curved surfaces for coarse facets.
  • Import into the slicer and compare a known dimension with the CAD model. Check units, scale, and the number of bodies.
  • Select the actual printer, nozzle, and approved material profile. Do not assume settings imported with a file match the available machine.
  • Orient the part, set walls, infill, layer height, and supports, then slice and inspect the layer preview.
  • Export the printer's required job format using the posted procedure. Save the slicer project so you can revise the setup.

Use walls for the shell, infill for the interior, and supports where needed

Use walls for the shell, infill for the interior, and supports where needed

Walls, also called perimeters, trace the outside and hole boundaries. Infill fills some of the interior. Solid top and bottom layers close the part. Increasing infill is not a substitute for choosing suitable walls and orientation.

Layer height sets the thickness of each deposited layer. Smaller layers can reduce the steps on sloped surfaces but usually add print time. They do not by themselves make a thin wall or tiny hole printable: nozzle size and extrusion width matter too.

Supports are temporary material beneath regions that cannot print reliably in the chosen orientation. They use material and time, may leave rough contact surfaces, and must be reachable for removal. Choose from the required shape and material first

Choose from the required shape and material first

Start with what the part must do. A process is useful only if it can produce the geometry in a suitable material with the required performance.

Filament printing
Builds three-dimensional shapes in layers. It suits changing cross sections, bosses, and internal features when those features can be printed and supports can be removed. Layer direction and service temperature affect the choice.
Laser-cut sheet
Produces planar profiles from a sheet of existing thickness. It suits flat panels, plates, and repeated outlines. Three-dimensional structures need joints, stacked layers, or another forming operation.

A flat nameplate with surface lettering is a natural laser candidate if the sheet is approved. Offset the path toward the waste by half the kerf; Practice

Offset the path toward the waste by half the kerf

Kerf is the width of material removed by a cut. In the simple model below, the laser follows the center of a constant-width kerf. Half the removed width lies on each side of the path.

For an outside part boundary, move the path outward by kerf / 2. For an opening, move the path inward toward the material being removed. Without compensation, a cutout part becomes smaller and a hole becomes larger.

For a desired outside width W, the path-to-path width is W + k, where k is kerf. For a desired opening width H, it is H − k. These are whole-width changes; the offset at each edge is only k / 2.

Measure a test cut in the same material and setup. Kerf can vary with thickness, focus, and settings, and cut edges may taper. If software already compensates the path, do not apply the same allowance again. See Tolerances and Fits for fit requirements.

Treat power, speed, and pulse settings as a matched setup

Treat power, speed, and pulse settings as a matched setup

Power controls laser output; speed controls how quickly the spot travels. With other conditions held fixed, higher power or slower travel generally puts more energy into a length of cut. That can also increase scorching or melting rather than improve the edge.

Frequency or pulse settings control how pulses are delivered. Controls may use pulses per second, pulses per distance, or a machine-specific scale; read the manual's definition. A percentage from one machine is not a transferable recipe for another.

    STL represents the surface with triangles and does not record a unit of length. A 3MF file can carry units and multiple objects; a slicer's 3MF project can also carry settings. Support for those extra settings varies between applications. Neither filename alone proves a job is ready for your printer. Prusa documents saving projects as 3MF and its supported file formats.

    Addendum 59. Why more infill does not solve every strength problem

    Addendum 60. Why there is no universal minimum wall or perfect press-fit allowance

    Printer paths depend on nozzle and profile; laser edges depend on the sheet and setup. Material batches and finishing change dimensions too. A small test of the critical feature supplies evidence that a generic dimension cannot. Use Tolerances and Fits to distinguish the intended size from acceptable variation.

    Addendum 61. Why a hairline is a workflow setting, not a cutting guarantee

    The teaching deck uses hairline strokes for vector cuts and strokes of at least 0.5 pt for raster content. Preserve those values only when the specified driver procedure calls for them. Stroke width alone does not establish cut depth, material suitability, or a safe setting.
