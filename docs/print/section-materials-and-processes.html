Design for Manufacturing

Make the required function easier to produce, assemble, inspect and maintain.

Keep the function and simplify how it is made

Keep the function and simplify how it is made

Design for manufacturing (DFM) considers production while a design can still change. Preserve required strength, fit and service performance; remove complexity that serves no requirement.

  • Reduce separate parts when this simplifies production and service.
  • Use standard components and available stock sizes.
  • Match geometry to the process and provide tool and inspection access.
  • Make assembly easy to orient, reach and verify.

Fewer parts can reduce joining work, but one complex part may need costly tooling or be hard to repair. Compare the whole route.

Specify only the precision the function needs

Specify only the precision the function needs

A tolerance is the allowed variation in a dimension or other feature. Tight tolerances may require careful setups, slower processing and more capable inspection. Keep tight control where fit or function needs it; do not apply it everywhere by habit.

No machine name guarantees a tolerance or price. Geometry, material, tooling, setup and measurement all matter. Confirm capability for the actual feature.

Reduce material loss without shortening useful life; Checklist

Reduce material loss without shortening useful life

Use Material Utilization and Cost to compare layouts. Also consider energy, durability, repair, disassembly and practical material recovery at end of life. A lighter part that fails early may consume more resources over its service life.

  • Identify each part's function and critical features.
  • Check standard components and stock sizes.
  • Sketch manufacturing, assembly and inspection access.
  • Check tolerances against function and process capability.
  • Compare material use, service life and repair or recycling options.

Make or Buy

Make means produce internally; buy means purchase from a supplier. Define quantity, specifications and delivery date before comparing options. Use Manufacturing Cost and Volume for an initial cost comparison.

Manufacturing Cost and Volume

Estimate unit cost and find where two production methods cost the same.

Count material, processing, quality and overhead costs once

Count material, processing, quality and overhead costs once

Material, labor, machine time, tooling and scrap all affect the cost of an acceptable part. Setup prepares an operation; cycle time is the time for a repeating operation.

  • Material: stock purchase, delivery and material lost during production.
  • Processing: labor, machine time, tools, consumables and energy.
  • Quality: prevention, inspection, rework, rejects and returns.
  • Overhead: shared support such as facilities and indirect labor.

Check what a machine rate already includes before adding labor, energy or depreciation. Scrap belongs in the estimate once, even if it appears under both material and quality headings.

Spread fixed cost over the quantity actually needed; Practice

Spread fixed cost over the quantity actually needed

For a simple batch model, C = F + vQ and u = C/Q = F/Q + v. C is total cost, F is fixed batch cost, v is variable cost per part, Q is the number of acceptable parts, and u is average unit cost. Use Q greater than zero.

Fixed cost stays constant within the stated production range; variable cost grows with quantity. Setup and dedicated tooling may be fixed for a batch. Material and processing per part often contribute to v. State the period and cost boundary before classifying a cost.

Illustrative process A costs $120 + $8Q. At 60 parts, total cost is $600 and unit cost is $10.00/part. Producing unwanted parts to spread fixed cost creates inventory; it does not create demand.

Practice unit cost

Set total costs equal to find process break-even; Practice

Set total costs equal to find process break-even

Compare routes that meet the same requirements at the same quantity. From F_A + v_A Q = F_B + v_B Q, obtain Q* = (F_B − F_A)/(v_A − v_B).

Let A cost $120 + $8Q and B cost $720 + $3Q. Then Q* = ($720 − $120)/($8 − $3) = 120 parts. Both cost $1,080 total or $9/part. A costs less below 120; B costs less above 120.

Manufacturing Process Categories

Classify an operation by its main purpose: separating, forming, fabricating, conditioning or finishing.

Five categories describe the change an operation makes

Five categories describe the change an operation makes

Name the operation's intended result. Are we removing material, creating shape, joining parts, changing internal properties or treating a surface?

This collection uses the course's five-category model. In this model, forming includes casting, molding and additive manufacturing such as 3D printing. Other technical references may separate those into their own process groups.

Separating removes material to make a size, shape or opening

Separating removes material to make a size, shape or opening

Separating cuts material apart or removes it from a workpiece, the piece being worked on. Look for a cut edge, hole, slot or reduced dimension as the intended result.

  • Sawing: cut a wooden board or plastic rod to length.
  • Drilling: remove material to make a hole in a metal bracket or wooden panel.
  • Milling: a rotating cutter removes material to make a flat face, pocket or slot.
  • Laser cutting: remove material along a narrow path to cut a suitable sheet material.

For metal-cutting examples, continue to Milling Operations and Lathe Operations. The tool motions differ, but both remove material.

Supporting detail: Addendum 4 — A laser cut does not have to vaporize all the removed material.

Forming creates shape without using material removal as the shaping method

Forming creates shape without using material removal as the shaping method

Forming gives material a shape by filling a mold, deforming it or building it up. The central action creates the shape rather than cutting unwanted material away.

  • Casting and molding: pour metal into a mold, mold a plastic cap, or cast a concrete planter.
  • Bending: bend sheet metal into a bracket or bend prepared wood around a form.
  • Forging: press or hammer metal into a new shape.
  • 3D printing: build a plastic part in layers.

Forming can still produce excess material. Trimming a molded edge or cutting away printed supports is a separate separating operation. “Forming” does not promise zero waste.

Fabricating joins parts into an assembly

Fabricating joins parts into an assembly

Fabricating brings separate parts together so they work as one assembly. Ask whether the intended result is a joint.

  • Welding: join metal frame members or suitable plastic parts.
  • Adhesive bonding: glue wooden strips together or bond a panel to a frame.
  • Fasteners: use screws, bolts or rivets to hold parts together.
  • Joinery: assemble matching wooden joint features, such as a mortise and tenon.

Cutting the joint features is separating; fitting and securing the parts is fabricating. In industry, “fabrication” can describe a whole production job. Here it has the narrower meaning joining.

Conditioning changes internal properties

Conditioning changes internal properties

Conditioning changes how the material behaves through its structure, moisture content or chemical state. A new outline or a decorative coating is not its main goal.

  • Heat treating: use a controlled heating and cooling cycle to change properties of a suitable metal.
  • Drying or seasoning wood: reduce moisture content before later processing or service.
  • Curing: allow a resin's chemical reaction to develop its intended properties.
  • Annealing: use a controlled thermal cycle to relieve stress or change structure, as appropriate to the metal, plastic or glass.

Conditioning can also change dimensions. Drying wood may shrink; heat treatment may distort a part. “Changes properties” does not mean “leaves every dimension unchanged.”

Finishing treats the surface for protection or appearance

Finishing treats the surface for protection or appearance

Finishing changes surface condition, appearance or protection. It may add a layer, remove a little material or alter the surface itself.

  • Painting: coat a wooden panel or metal housing.
  • Plating: deposit a metal layer on a part.
  • Staining: change wood's color; stain alone does not necessarily provide a protective film.
  • Polishing: improve the surface of metal, suitable plastic or glass.

A surface location alone does not decide the category. Surface hardening primarily changes material properties, so it belongs under conditioning in this model.

For overlapping processes, state the purpose and pick the primary category; Practice

  1. Identify one operation. Break a long manufacturing description into separate actions.
  2. State its acceptance goal. What result tells you this operation is complete?
  3. Choose the category that best matches that goal. Name any secondary effect in the explanation.
Sanding
Sanding a block down to a specified thickness is primarily separating. Sanding out visible scratches before a clear coat is primarily finishing, although both remove material.
Bent lamination
Bonding thin wood layers around a curved form combines fabricating and forming. If the stated goal is the curve, call forming primary; if the question focuses on the bond between layers, call fabricating primary.
Resin and adhesive curing
Curing a resin part to develop properties is conditioning. Bonding two parts with that resin is primarily fabricating; curing is the property-changing step that enables the joint.
Welding
A weld can change nearby material properties, but a step intended to join two frame members is primarily fabricating.

If the description only says “sanding” or “curing,” explain what purpose you are assuming. A defensible classification includes the reason.

Material Families

Compare exactly four families: Metals, Plastics (polymers), Wood & Composites, and Ceramics & Glass.

Use four material families as the starting point

Use four material families as the starting point

The course uses exactly four families: Metals; Plastics (polymers); Wood & Composites; Ceramics & Glass. A family helps you predict useful behavior. It does not specify a material well enough to manufacture a part.

Classify the material in the named part, not the entire product. A kettle can have a metal body, a plastic handle and a glass window.

Supporting detail: Addendum 5 — Why another book may show a different family list.

Metals combine useful strength, stiffness and conductivity

Metals combine useful strength, stiffness and conductivity

Metals include metallic elements and alloys, mixtures designed for useful properties. Steel, aluminum and copper are everyday examples.

Many metals conduct heat and electricity well and can deform permanently without breaking. Many are strong and stiff compared with unreinforced plastics. Density varies: aluminum is much less dense than steel, so “metal” does not always mean the heaviest choice.

Typical matches include copper wire for electrical conduction, aluminum cookware for heat transfer, and steel fasteners for carrying loads. Corrosion behavior depends on the alloy and environment. Rust specifically concerns iron-containing materials; other metals can corrode without rusting.

Metals may be cast, formed, cut, joined, heat treated and coated. Those process choices depend on the specific alloy and condition. Detailed metal processing belongs in the later metals topics; Milling Operations and Lathe Operations show separating operations.

Plastics are usually light, moldable and poor conductors

Plastics are usually light, moldable and poor conductors

Plastics (polymers) are materials based on long molecular chains, usually with additives to adjust their behavior. Polyethylene containers, acrylic display panels and nylon gears belong here.

Common unfilled plastics have relatively low density and low thermal and electrical conductivity. They can range from flexible to rigid, and from tough to brittle. Heat, sunlight and chemicals affect different grades differently.

Thermoplastics can soften on heating and be reshaped under suitable conditions. Thermosets develop a chemically linked structure during curing and cannot simply be remelted into a new shape.

“Light” does not mean “floats.” Solid acrylic is denser than water. A hollow product may float because its overall density includes the trapped air.

Supporting detail: Addendum 6 — A supplier value that checks the floating claim.

Wood & Composites depend on grain, layers and reinforcement

Wood & Composites depend on grain, layers and reinforcement

Wood has a cellular structure and grain. It is often light for its useful stiffness, relatively easy to cut and join, and a poor heat conductor. Strength and movement depend on direction and moisture content.

A composite combines materials so they work together. Examples include plywood, wood-fiber board, glass-fiber-reinforced plastic and carbon-fiber-reinforced plastic. The material surrounding and holding reinforcement is the matrix; the reinforcement helps carry load.

Do not assume every composite behaves like wood or like unfilled plastic. Fiber direction, layer arrangement, binder and moisture exposure affect the result. A composite can offer useful strength or stiffness for its mass while still being difficult to cut, join or repair.

Plywood shelves and glass-fiber-reinforced boat panels belong to the same course family, but need different processing choices. Later wood topics develop grain and moisture behavior.

Ceramics & Glass often resist heat and wear but can fracture abruptly

Ceramics & Glass often resist heat and wear but can fracture abruptly

Ceramics & Glass include fired clay products, porcelain, window glass and engineering ceramics such as alumina. Many are hard, resist chemical attack and carry compression well. Many also have little ability to deform permanently before fracture.

A tile can resist scratching yet break when struck. Hardness is not toughness. Many familiar ceramics and glasses are electrical insulators, but the family contains exceptions; thermal conductivity also varies widely.

Shaping may occur before firing, while hot, or through suitable cutting and grinding afterward. Glass can be formed while hot and annealed to reduce residual stress. Finished ceramic parts are often harder to machine than common metals or plastics.

Material Properties

Distinguish strength, stiffness, hardness and toughness, then compare physical and chemical behavior, cost and workability.

State the requirement before choosing the material

State the requirement before choosing the material

“Strong,” “light” and “durable” are starting points. A useful comparison says what load, temperature, environment and lifetime the part must handle.

Mechanical properties describe behavior under force. Physical properties describe features such as density and heat or electrical behavior. Chemical properties describe interaction with the environment. Cost and workability determine whether a suitable material can become a practical product.

Start with the four material families, then compare actual materials within them. Processing and condition matter: a material name alone does not tell you its final properties.

Strength resists failure; stiffness limits elastic deformation

Strength resists failure; stiffness limits elastic deformation

Strength describes the stress a material can withstand before a specified limit, such as permanent deformation or fracture. Stress relates force to the area carrying it. A thicker part may carry more load without being made of a stronger material.

Tensile strength
Resistance to a pulling load. Think of a tie rod being pulled along its length.
Compressive strength
Resistance to a pushing or crushing load. Think of a block squeezed between two surfaces. A material good in compression may be much less capable in tension.
Stiffness
Resistance to elastic deformation, the change that recovers when the load is removed. A shelf that sags too far may be strong enough to avoid failure but insufficiently stiff.

A part's stiffness depends on both its material and its geometry. Compare similarly shaped specimens when comparing materials; do not infer material stiffness by comparing a thin wire with a thick bar.

Supporting detail: Addendum 8 — Stress, strain and modulus give the comparison units.

Hardness, toughness, brittleness and ductility answer different questions

Hardness, toughness, brittleness and ductility answer different questions

Hardness
Resistance to local indentation or scratching, depending on the test. It can help resist wear, but hardness alone does not determine wear performance.
Toughness
Ability to absorb energy before fracturing. A part that must survive a blow needs more than a hard surface.
Brittleness
A tendency to fracture with little permanent deformation. Glass may resist scratching but crack instead of bending permanently.
Ductility
Ability to deform permanently in tension before fracture. A ductile metal can be drawn into wire. Elastic stretch that disappears when unloaded is not the same as ductility.

Do not treat these as synonyms. A hard ceramic tile can be brittle; a ductile metal can bend before it breaks. Toughness depends on both the stress sustained and the deformation before fracture, as well as test conditions.

Supporting detail: Addendum 9 — Why a single “toughness number” needs its test method.

Density and thermal behavior decide weight and temperature limits

Density and thermal behavior decide weight and temperature limits

Density
Mass per unit volume. At equal volume, lower density gives lower mass. A large hollow metal part can weigh less than a small solid plastic part, so compare the whole design too.
Melting point or melting range
The temperature or range where a material melts. It helps choose processing conditions, but it is not the allowable service temperature: a loaded part may lose useful stiffness or strength earlier.
Thermal conductivity
How readily heat travels through a material. A heat spreader benefits from high conductivity; an insulating handle usually benefits from low conductivity.
Electrical conductivity
How readily electric current passes through a material. A wire needs a conductor; its surrounding insulation needs low conductivity under the intended conditions.

Many common metals conduct heat and electricity well. Many unfilled plastics, dry woods and familiar ceramics are poor electrical conductors. Additives, moisture and temperature can change those tendencies.

Supporting detail: Addendum 10 — Not every material has one useful melting-point number.

Corrosion resistance is resistance to a particular environment

Corrosion resistance is resistance to a particular environment

Corrosion resistance is the ability to resist chemical or electrochemical deterioration in service. A metal that works well indoors may perform differently in salt water or against another metal.

Rust is one form of corrosion involving iron. Aluminum and stainless steel can resist many exposures, but neither is immune to every environment. Coatings can protect a surface, and their condition matters.

For plastics, wood and composites, also consider chemical attack, sunlight, moisture and decay as appropriate. “Does not rust” is not the same as “will last outdoors.” Name the exposure before selecting a family or grade.

Cost and workability belong in the same comparison

Cost and workability belong in the same comparison

Workability means how readily a material can be processed into an acceptable part. It is specific to the operation: a material that molds well may be difficult to bond, and one that cuts readily may be unsuitable for bending.

Cost includes more than price per mass. Consider how much material the design needs, available supply forms, tooling, processing time, waste, inspection and any protective finish.

A family profile narrows the search: common metals often offer stiffness and conductivity; plastics often offer low mass and moldability; wood and composites offer useful directional performance; ceramics and glass often offer hardness and chemical resistance. The particular material decides whether those benefits meet the job.

Use Manufacturing Process Categories to describe the required operations. Detailed manufacturing economics and design for manufacturing extend the cost comparison.

Material Utilization and Cost

Check a stock layout, account for cutting loss and roll up material costs.

Prove the parts fit before dividing areas; Practice

Prove the parts fit before dividing areas

Stock is material purchased for further work. Nesting arranges part outlines on stock to reduce waste. An area ratio cannot prove a layout fits. Check dimensions, orientation, grain direction where relevant, edge margins and spacing.

Kerf is the width removed by a cut. An offcut is leftover stock; a usable offcut may be saved. Include trim cuts and holding allowances before promising a part count.

Illustrative layout: a 1,000 mm bar supplies 98 mm blanks. Each blank requires one 2 mm separating cut, including the last blank, with no end trim. Ten blanks consume 10 × (98 + 2) = 1,000 mm. A different last-cut arrangement would need a different cut count.

Use a consistent physical basis for utilization

Utilization = material in acceptable parts / material purchased × 100%. Compare length for the same cross-section, area for the same thickness, or mass on a consistent basis.

The bar example gives 980/1,000 × 100 = 98.0%. The remaining 2% is kerf. Distinguish reusable offcuts from material discarded.

What Is Manufacturing?

Turn materials into useful products by choosing processes that fit the material, quantity, cost and required quality.

Manufacturing turns materials into useful products

Manufacturing turns materials into useful products

Manufacturing changes materials into parts and products people can use. A board becomes a shelf, plastic pellets become a bottle, and metal sheet becomes a bracket. Shape, properties, joints and surfaces all contribute to the result.

A process is an operation that makes one of those changes. Most products need several processes. A useful plan states what each operation must achieve and how the result will be checked.

Two organizing questions keep the subject manageable: What is the material? Use the four material families. What change are we making? Use the five process categories.

Primary processing makes usable material; secondary processing makes parts

Primary processing makes usable material; secondary processing makes parts

Primary processing converts raw resources into usable materials and common supply forms. Secondary processing turns those materials into parts or products. Stock means material supplied for further work, such as sheet, rod, boards or pellets.

Metal
Ore is refined into metal and made into stock such as sheet; sheet is then cut and bent into a bracket.
Plastic
Chemical feedstocks are made into polymer resin, often supplied as pellets; pellets are then molded into a bottle cap.
Wood
Logs are sawn into lumber; boards are then cut, joined and finished as furniture.
Glass
Mineral ingredients are melted and made into sheet glass; sheets are then cut and edge-finished for a window.

These terms describe a material's place in the supply chain. They do not replace the five process categories: sawing a log and sawing a furniture part are both separating.

Choose a process that fits material, quantity, cost and quality

Choose a process that fits material, quantity, cost and quality

Material
Can it tolerate the heat, force or chemical exposure? A process that bends metal sheet may crack a ceramic plate.
Quantity

Cycle time and cavity count set the ideal production rate; Practice

Cycle time and cavity count set the ideal production rate

A cavity makes one part per cycle. With cycle time in seconds, ideal parts per hour = 3600 / cycle time × cavities.

For a 30-second cycle and two identical cavities: 3600 / 30 × 2 = 240 parts/h. That assumes uninterrupted operation and every part acceptable. Use the complete repeating cycle time, including opening and ejection; do not add overlapping cooling time twice.

Actual useful output is lower when setup, stoppages or rejects occur. More cavities may increase tooling cost and require a larger machine, so a higher ideal rate does not by itself settle the process choice.

Conditioning and Finishing Plastics

Separate property changes from surface treatments, and match annealing, smoothing and coatings to the exact plastic.

Conditioning changes properties; finishing changes the surface

Conditioning changes properties; finishing changes the surface

Conditioning changes the material's state or properties: relieving internal stress, developing a thermoset cure, or controlling absorbed moisture. Finishing changes the surface for appearance, protection or function. A part can need both. Removing a support is Separating; sanding the resulting surface is Finishing.

Start with the resin, dimensions and service requirement. A glossy surface does not prove that a part is strong, properly cured or dimensionally correct.

Annealing relieves stress, but can change dimensions

Annealing relieves stress, but can change dimensions

Annealing uses a controlled heating, holding and cooling schedule. In acrylic it can reduce stress left by machining, bending or polishing. It is not a general instruction to heat near molding temperature. Use the resin supplier's schedule for the grade and thickness, including support of the part during treatment.

Printed PLA can also be annealed. Depending on its formulation and thermal history, heating can allow more crystalline order and improve heat resistance. Shrinkage, expansion in another direction or warping can spoil a fit. Measure before and after; do not assume every strength property improves.

Curing creates a network; drying removes moisture

Curing creates a network; drying removes moisture

A thermoset cure is a chemical reaction that builds links between polymer chains. Resin and hardener, heat or light may initiate the reaction, depending on the system. Correct proportions, time and temperature matter. A prescribed post-cure can develop properties further; an already cured thermoset will not simply remelt like a thermoplastic.

Drying moisture-sensitive filament addresses a different problem. Moisture can affect extrusion and part quality, but drying does not replace annealing or cure. Use a drying schedule intended for the material; excessive heat can deform filament or its spool.

Remove scratches progressively before adding gloss or paint

Remove scratches progressively before adding gloss or paint

Sanding replaces a coarse scratch pattern with progressively finer scratches. Start with the finest abrasive that will remove the defect efficiently. Within the same grit system, a larger number generally means a finer abrasive. A sequence such as P240 → P320 → P400 → P600 illustrates progression; it is not a required recipe for every plastic or coating.

Remove the previous scratch pattern before moving on and clean away abrasive residue between stages. Wet sanding with suitable waterproof abrasive can reduce loading and heat where the material and equipment permit it. Keep corners and fit surfaces from rounding over. Paint may need a controlled scuffed surface rather than a polished one; follow its preparation specification.

Tumbling can finish many parts together, but media and time affect edge rounding and small features. Buffing can improve gloss, but excessive heat or pressure can smear the surface. Check a representative sample rather than equating more polishing with a better part.

Flame and solvent smoothing are material-specific processes

Flame and solvent smoothing are material-specific processes

Flame polishing can smooth suitable acrylic edges by briefly softening the surface. It can also round edges and introduce stress. It is not a general treatment for clear plastics: polycarbonate is not interchangeable with acrylic, and a process must be validated for its exact grade. A clear edge is not necessarily an optically accurate or low-stress edge.

Solvent or vapor smoothing softens a compatible polymer's surface chemically. Acetone smoothing is associated with ABS and ASA; do not transfer that recipe to PLA or PETG. Smoothing can erase fine lettering, change dimensions and alter mechanical performance. It does not guarantee that weak layer bonds inside a print have been repaired.

Coatings need adhesion as well as the right appearance; Practice

Coatings need adhesion as well as the right appearance

For paint, remove contamination with a compatible cleaner, prepare the surface as specified and use a primer or adhesion promoter when the coating system calls for one. PE and PP often need special preparation or products because of low surface energy. A primer is not automatically compatible with every plastic. Powder coating plastics requires a suitable way to attract or deposit the powder and a cure that the part can withstand; an insulating plastic does not become conductive merely because a grounding wire touches it.

Electroplating needs a conductive surface. Plastics therefore require preparation and a conductive layer before electroplating. Decorative options also include printing, decals, in-mold labels and dyes for receptive polymers. Make chips instead of rubbing the plastic hot

Make chips instead of rubbing the plastic hot

Heat builds up near the cut because plastics generally conduct heat poorly. Softened chips can stick to the tool or weld back onto the edge. The symptoms are smeared surfaces, strings of softened material and chips packed into the cutter.

Slowing the feed alone can make melting worse. Feed is how quickly the tool advances through the work; spindle speed is how quickly it turns. Acrylic, PVC and polycarbonate behave differently under a laser

Acrylic, PVC and polycarbonate behave differently under a laser

Acrylic (PMMA) absorbs the infrared energy of a CO₂ laser. Concentrated heating removes material along the cut and can leave a smooth, glossy edge. That result depends on the sheet grade, thickness and process settings; it is not a promise for every clear plastic or every laser.

PVC is not a laser-cutting substitute for acrylic. Heating PVC can release hydrogen chloride; with moisture it forms hydrochloric acid, creating an exposure and corrosion concern. This is a chemical incompatibility, not simply a poor-looking edge.

Polycarbonate is often a poor choice for a clean CO₂-laser-cut sheet edge. It can yellow, char and leave a degraded edge instead of acrylic's clear finish. Specialized processes can cut some polycarbonate, so “polycarbonate cannot be laser processed” is too broad. Routing or sawing is often the better starting choice for a clear polycarbonate profile. Do not assign PVC's chlorine chemistry to polycarbonate.

Material approval and operating controls belong with Equipment Operating Procedure and Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets. Appearance or a trade name is not enough to identify an unknown sheet.

Trim the molding excess without cutting into the part

A burr is a raised edge left by cutting. These are different defects, even when the same trimming tool can remove them.

FDM 3D Printing

Read an FDM part as deposited roads and layers, then choose orientation, walls, infill and supports for its job.

A slicer turns a solid model into deposited layers

A slicer turns a solid model into deposited layers

Fused deposition modeling (FDM), also called fused filament fabrication (FFF), feeds thermoplastic filament into a heated nozzle. The machine lays down narrow roads of softened material that bond as the part builds. Relative motion between the nozzle and build plate advances the layers; the nozzle itself does not have to move upward on every printer.

A slicer converts the model into tool paths, including walls, internal fill and any temporary supports. Preview those paths before printing. An enclosed shape on a CAD screen does not tell you how its underside will be supported.

Supporting detail: Addendum 21 — Where additive manufacturing fits the five process categories.

Layer height, walls and infill do different jobs

Layer height, walls and infill do different jobs

Layer height is the vertical increment between layers. A smaller height makes steps on sloping surfaces finer and usually adds print time. It does not make all small horizontal features printable: nozzle size, road width and motion accuracy also matter.

Walls or perimeters trace the boundary. Top and bottom layers close the part. Infill is the pattern inside those boundaries. More infill generally adds material and time, but strength also depends on walls, orientation, bonding and load direction. A 20% infill setting does not mean the entire part uses 20% of its solid volume, because the walls and skins remain.

Simple layer-count example: for a 24 mm height with every layer 0.20 mm thick, 24 ÷ 0.20 = 120 layers. Real slicers may use a different first-layer height or variable layers. Halving layer height roughly doubles the layer count, not necessarily the total print time.

Orient for the load as well as for fewer supports; Checklist

Orient for the load as well as for fewer supports

FDM parts are anisotropic: properties depend on direction. A load that pulls layers apart can expose a weaker bond than a load carried along continuous deposited roads. Avoid placing the main tensile load across layer interfaces when an orientation can carry it along the layers. This is a design starting point, not a strength rating.

An overhang extends beyond the layer below. A short bridge spans between supported ends. A long unsupported underside may need temporary support material. Support contacts can leave marks, so keep them away from critical visible or mating surfaces where possible. There is no universal overhang angle that works for all materials and settings.

For a rectangular test bar pulled along its length, laying its length in the build plane usually gives a better starting orientation than standing it upright and pulling across stacked layers. A bracket may have several important load directions; choose a compromise or change the design, then test it.

  • Mark the main load direction and critical fit surfaces on the model.
  • Compare orientations for layer direction, build-plate contact and unsupported undersides.
  • Preview walls, infill and supports one layer at a time.
  • Check that support can actually be removed from each enclosed space.

Estimate mass from deposited volume, not infill alone; Practice

Mass = deposited volume × density. If a simplified model has 8 cm³ of solid walls and skins plus a 32 cm³ interior at 25% infill, its estimated deposited volume is 8 + (32 × 0.25) = 16 cm³. At an assumed density of 1.24 g/cm³, that is 19.84 g. Supports, brims and purge material add more.

Solvent cement softens the surfaces; adhesive adds a bond layer

Solvent cement softens the surfaces; adhesive adds a bond layer

Solvent cementing uses a compatible liquid to soften or dissolve the surfaces so polymer chains can mix across the joint. As solvent leaves, the joint develops strength. It is chemical softening, not heat melting. Acrylic-to-acrylic is a common application; PVC and ABS also have specific cement systems. The cement must match the resin and fit of the joint.

Adhesives bond through a separate layer. Epoxies, cyanoacrylates and other formulations have different gap, flexibility and temperature limits. “Plastic glue” does not mean it bonds every plastic. Allow the specified cure time before loading; a joint that holds itself together may not have reached service strength.

Polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) resist many ordinary adhesives. Their low surface energy makes it difficult for an adhesive to spread into intimate contact, called wetting. Specialized adhesives or a controlled surface treatment can help; sanding alone is not a universal fix. See Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets for chemical handling.

Hot-melt adhesive is not a fusion weld

Use mechanical joints when access and disassembly matter; Practice

Use mechanical joints when access and disassembly matter

A snap fit bends a feature during assembly so it catches behind another feature. It needs room to flex, rounded transitions and a resin that tolerates the required strain. Some snap fits release repeatedly; others are intentionally difficult to undo.

Thread-forming screws for plastics create their mating thread in a designed hole. Repeated removal can wear plastic threads. A threaded insert supplies a metal thread; a heat-set insert is installed by locally softening a thermoplastic around it. Hole size, surrounding wall thickness and installation conditions come from the insert supplier. A metal insert cannot rescue an undersized plastic wall.

Compare two cases: a clear permanent acrylic corner is a candidate for compatible solvent cement; a PP cover that must be removed regularly is a candidate for screws and a suitable thread arrangement. An adhesive that works on the first pair tells you little about the second.

Plastic Forming Processes

How eight plastic and composite processes shape material, what they can make, and where tooling and geometry limit the choice.

Start with the shape and how the material becomes solid

Start with the shape and how the material becomes solid

These processes make a shape by moving softened material, stretching sheet or curing resin in tooling. A mold shapes a part against its surfaces; a die gives flowing material a cross-section. Tooling is the equipment made specifically to produce the part.

Use the process-category foundations topic for the five-category framework. Here, follow what happens to the material: thermoplastics usually set by cooling; thermosets set by curing. Review Plastics as Materials if that distinction is unclear.

For equipment operation and hazards, use the Safety collection's Equipment Operating Procedure, Machine Hazards and Housekeeping and Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets topics.

Injection molding fills a closed cavity, then repeats the cycle

Injection molding fills a closed cavity, then repeats the cycle

In conventional thermoplastic injection molding, a heated barrel and screw prepare the melt. The screw advances to push a measured shot (the material delivered in one injection) through a nozzle into a clamped mold.

  1. Clamp: close the mold and hold it against the pressure of the incoming melt.
  2. Inject: fill the cavity.
  3. Hold/pack: maintain pressure to feed additional material as it shrinks, while the gate can still pass melt.
  4. Cool: allow enough heat to leave for the part to keep its shape during removal.
  5. Eject: open the mold and push the part free, commonly with ejector pins. Close for the next cycle.

Materials and products: thermoplastics such as ABS, PP and nylon become housings, caps, clips and gears. Specialized injection systems also mold thermosets; those require curing, so the cooling-based cycle above is not universal.

Tooling and quantity: detailed, pressure-resistant molds are a substantial initial investment. Repeated cycles and multiple cavities can make the investment worthwhile for many identical parts.

Sprue, runner and gate carry melt into the part

Sprue, runner and gate carry melt into the part

In a conventional sprue-and-runner layout, the sprue receives melt from the nozzle, a runner distributes it, and a gate is the restricted entrance to a part cavity. One mold may contain several cavities, and one cavity may have several gates.

Draft is a slight taper on a surface in the mold-opening direction. It helps the part release instead of scraping along the tool. Injection molding can produce ribs, bosses (raised features around holes) and fine detail, but every feature must have a way out of the mold.

An undercut traps material behind a tool feature. It may require a moving side core, another special mechanism or a redesigned part. A simple two-part mold cannot produce every enclosed hollow shape.

Uniform walls reduce sink marks and uneven shrinkage; Practice

Uniform walls reduce sink marks and uneven shrinkage

Aim for a reasonably uniform wall thickness, with gradual transitions where thickness must change. A thick mass cools differently from an adjacent thin wall. Its shrinkage can pull the surface inward, leaving a sink mark, or contribute to distortion.

A solid block under a thin housing wall is therefore a warning sign. Hollow out the bulky region where possible and use appropriately sized ribs for support. Packing and cooling settings also matter; changing the drawing is not the only remedy.

Blow molding inflates a hot tube or preform into a hollow part

Blow molding inflates a hot tube or preform into a hollow part

A mold closes around a hot parison (tube), or a previously molded preform. Air pressure expands the material against the mold, then it cools. Extrusion blow molding starts with a tube; injection-based versions start with a molded preform.

Materials and products: thermoplastics; HDPE detergent bottles and PET beverage bottles are familiar examples made by different blow-molding variants.

Tooling and quantity: dedicated molds and machinery favor repeated production, especially large packaging runs. Mold complexity and the chosen variant affect cost; lower forming pressure does not make the whole production line inexpensive.

Geometry: good for thin-walled hollow containers. Stretching affects wall thickness; external detail is controlled better than internal detail. It does not directly make a solid gear or complex internal ribs. Mold parting lines, and pinch-off seams in extrusion-blown parts, may remain visible.

Rotational molding coats a mold to make a large hollow shell

Rotational molding coats a mold to make a large hollow shell

A measured charge, usually thermoplastic powder, goes inside a mold. Heating and slow rotation about two axes distribute material over the inside surface as it melts. The mold keeps moving during cooling before the shell is removed.

Materials and products: predominantly polyethylene; tanks, playground shells and kayaks.

Extrusion makes a continuous, constant cross-section

Compression molding presses a charge between matched tools

Compression molding presses a charge between matched tools

Geometry: both faces can be shaped, including ribs and shaped panels. Material must flow before it cures; extremely thin features, deep narrow details and trapped undercuts are difficult.

Casting fills a mold with liquid resin for small quantities

Plastics get their behavior from polymer chains

Light weight and corrosion resistance come with limits

Light weight and corrosion resistance come with limits

  • Light weight: many unfilled plastics have much lower density than metals. Low weight does not automatically mean high stiffness.
  • Chemical resistance: plastics do not rust like steel, but a chemical may soften, swell or crack a particular plastic. Check the material, chemical, temperature and exposure time together.
  • Low softening or melting temperatures: many common thermoplastics are easy to form with heat but unsuitable for hot, loaded service.
  • Creep: slow deformation under a sustained load. A plastic shelf can keep sagging even when its load stays constant.
  • UV degradation: sunlight can discolor or embrittle susceptible grades. Outdoor service calls for a suitable resin and stabilization, not just a preferred color.
  • Electrical insulation: many unfilled plastics resist current flow. Conductive fillers and special formulations can change that behavior.

Grain and Moisture Movement

Connect grain orientation and moisture changes to board movement, defects, and design choices.

Follow the grain through the face, edge, and end

Follow the grain through the face, edge, and end

Grain describes the direction of wood cells. Figure is the visible pattern created by growth structure and the way the wood is cut. A board's broad surface is its face, its narrow long surface is its edge, and its cut end exposes end grain. Face and edge grain usually run generally along the board; they are not separate botanical structures.

Wood is generally stronger in tension along the grain than across it. A short projection with grain running across its narrow base can split off even if the rest of the part is thick. Knots divert nearby grain, and sloping or interlocked grain makes a board less predictable.

During machining, a cutting edge can lift fibers ahead of the cut and tear them out. Grain direction helps predict that result, but does not replace the machine's operating procedure. See Machine Hazards and Housekeeping and Equipment Operating Procedure for safety; wood separating and finishing are later topics.

Sawing orientation changes figure and movement

Sawing orientation changes figure and movement

Read the growth rings on the end of a board. Plain-sawn boards have rings more nearly parallel to the broad face and often show cathedral-shaped face figure. Quarter-sawn boards have rings more nearly perpendicular to the face; their width follows the radial direction more closely. Rift-sawn boards have an intermediate, oblique ring angle and commonly show straight face grain.

Quarter-sawn oak can expose conspicuous ray fleck. Rift-sawn oak is often selected for straight grain with less prominent fleck. Trade descriptions and angle boundaries vary, so inspect the end grain as well as the sales label. The diagram shows selected orientations, not a mill's complete cutting plan.

Moisture content uses oven-dry wood mass as its reference

Moisture content uses oven-dry wood mass as its reference

MC (%) = (wet mass − oven-dry mass) / oven-dry mass × 100. A sample weighing 150 g before drying and 100 g after oven drying has 50% MC. MC can exceed 100% because the reference is dry wood mass, not the original wet mass.

Water occupies cell cavities and is also held in cell walls. At the fiber saturation point, the walls are saturated while the cavities contain no free liquid water. Below this region, loss of water from the cell walls causes appreciable shrinkage. It is not one exact threshold for every species and condition.

Equilibrium moisture content (EMC) is the moisture content wood approaches in a particular surrounding humidity and temperature. A board can be dry today and absorb moisture later.

Most movement is across the grain

For normal straight-grained wood, shrinkage is usually greatest tangentially (along growth rings), smaller radially (across rings), and much smaller longitudinally (along the cells). Do not apply a green-to-oven-dry shrinkage percentage directly to a small seasonal humidity change. Reaction wood and juvenile wood can also depart from ordinary longitudinal behavior.

Wood movement estimate — both moisture contents must be 6–14%.

Change = starting dimension × coefficient × (final MC − starting MC), with MC entered as a percentage (8, not 0.08). Negative means shrinkage; positive means swelling. How Saws Cut Wood

Match teeth to grain direction, leave room for chips, and include the saw's kerf in every cutting layout.

Teeth remove chips; the kerf is the wood they remove

Teeth remove chips; the kerf is the wood they remove

Sawing is a Separating process: each tooth removes a small chip, and successive teeth make a slot. The slot's width is the kerf. The space ahead of a tooth, called the gullet, carries chips out of the cut.

Many steel saw teeth have set: teeth bend slightly to alternating sides so the cut is wider than the blade body. Carbide-tipped circular blades commonly get that clearance from tips wider than the steel plate instead. Set and kerf are related, but they are not the same measurement.

This page explains cutting concepts. For operation, use Machine Hazards and Housekeeping and Equipment Operating Procedure.

Rip along the grain; crosscut across the fibers

Rip along the grain; crosscut across the fibers

A rip cut runs along the grain, usually to reduce a board's width. A crosscut runs across the grain, usually to reduce its length. Describe the cut relative to the grain, not just as a long or short cut.

Ripping teeth commonly have a flat, chisel-like cutting edge. Crosscut teeth commonly have alternating bevels that score and sever fibers at the sides of the cut. These are useful patterns, not universal rules: combination and specialty blades use other arrangements.

Supporting detail: Addendum 50 — Why tooth geometry changes the surface.

Balance tooth count, chip space and hook angle

Balance tooth count, chip space and hook angle

For circular blades of the same diameter, more teeth generally share the work into smaller bites at the same feed and rotational speed. That can improve a crosscut surface, but leaves less space for chips. Thick ripping cuts often benefit from fewer teeth and larger gullets. “Thicker wood needs more teeth” is not a general rule.

Band and reciprocating blades commonly specify teeth per inch (TPI). Tooth spacing must suit the thickness engaged in the cut. A coarse blade has more chip space; a finer blade can suit thinner stock and finer work. Blade width also matters on a band saw: a narrower blade can follow a tighter curve.

Hook angle describes the tooth face's lean relative to a radial line on a circular blade. More positive hook generally gives a more aggressive bite; low or negative hook is less aggressive. Machine type and the blade maker's intended application matter along with the desired finish. A ripping blade is not automatically appropriate on a miter saw.

See Feeds and Speeds for the relationship between tooth engagement, feed and cutting speed; metal-cutting settings do not transfer directly to wood.

Count cuts before calculating strip yield; Practice

Count cuts before calculating strip yield

Start with the usable width after any edge preparation. Let W be usable board width, s the required strip width and k the kerf, all in the same units. These ideal layouts assume straight cuts, exact dimensions and no extra finishing allowance.

One cut per strip, leaving an offcut: used width = n(s + k). The maximum whole number is floor(W / (s + k)); “floor” means round down. For a 12 in board, 3 in strips and a 1/8 in kerf, floor(12 / 3.125) = 3 strips. Three cuts use 9.375 in, leaving 2.625 in. Four strips alone use 12 in, leaving no room for the cuts.

All final pieces use both original outside edges: W = ns + (n − 1)k for an exact partition. There are only n − 1 cuts between n pieces. If a layout leaves an extra offcut to separate, count that extra cut too. Draw the layout before choosing a formula.

Real layouts also allow for defects, edge trimming and later machining. Kerf is material removed, not useful strip width.

Milling Rough Lumber Square

Establish one flat face and one square edge before making the opposite surfaces parallel and cutting to length.

A jointer establishes flatness; a planer establishes thickness

A jointer establishes flatness; a planer establishes thickness

Stock is the material a part starts from. Rough lumber may have uneven surfaces or warp. A jointer removes material to establish a flat face. With that face as a reference against a square fence, it can establish a straight edge at 90° to the face.

A thickness planer cuts the upper face relative to the face supported by its bed. With a flat reference face down, it makes the opposite face parallel and brings the board to thickness. Jointing both faces separately does not ensure they are parallel.

Preparing and Finishing Wood

Prepare a uniform surface, distinguish color from protection and choose a finish for appearance, wear and repair.

Remove each grit's scratches before moving to a finer abrasive; Practice

Remove each grit's scratches before moving to a finer abrasive

Start with the least coarse abrasive that removes the actual defects efficiently. Higher grit numbers generally mean finer particles within the same grading system. A possible bare-wood sequence is 80 → 120 → 150 → 180, but sound, smooth stock may start later. Stop at the grit recommended for the chosen stain or finish; 220 is not a universal destination.

Each step removes the previous step's scratches. Jumping straight from a coarse grit to a very fine grit leaves deep scratches that the fine abrasive removes slowly. Inspect under light across the surface, remove dust between steps and replace clogged abrasive. Finish hand sanding along the grain where practical; machine sanding also requires control of pressure and motion.

Do not use prolonged sanding to hide a poorly fitted joint. It can round edges, dish a surface or sand through a thin veneer. Remove glue residue before coloring; a smooth glue patch may still reject stain.

Heat Treating Metals

Choose a conditioning process by its goal: soften, refine structure, harden, improve toughness or reduce residual stress.

Heat treatment is conditioning: change structure to change properties

Heat treatment is conditioning: change structure to change properties

Heat treatment uses controlled heating, holding and cooling to change a metal's internal structure. It belongs to Conditioning in Manufacturing Process Categories. It can change properties without intentionally changing the overall shape, although distortion can occur.

The result depends on alloy, starting condition, section thickness and the complete cycle. Heating more or cooling faster is not automatically better. A treatment that strengthens one alloy can soften another.

Supporting detail: Addendum 57 — Steel microstructures are not the four steel families.

Anneal to soften; normalize suitable steels to refine structure

Anneal to soften; normalize suitable steels to refine structure

Annealing is a family of treatments used to soften material, restore ductility or adjust its structure. Full annealing of suitable steels includes slow cooling; annealing cold-worked metal can form new grains and restore formability. Not every anneal uses the same temperature or cycle.

Normalizing heats suitable steel into its austenitic range and then cools it in air. It can refine and make the structure more uniform after earlier processing. Air cooling and slow furnace cooling produce different results.

Choose the process for the desired condition. Do not apply one universal temperature offset to all steels.

Quench hardening usually needs tempering afterward; Practice

Quench hardening usually needs tempering afterward

Quench hardening heats suitable steel to form austenite, holds as required, then cools fast enough to obtain the intended hardened structure. It commonly raises hardness and strength but can reduce ductility and toughness. Cracking and distortion are possible.

Tempering reheats hardened steel below the temperature at which austenite begins to form. It adjusts the balance of hardness and toughness and reduces some quenching stresses. It is not simply heating “just below” one critical temperature; the required cycle depends on grade and target properties.

These concepts explain specifications; they are not operating instructions. Use the approved process for the identified material, equipment and quench medium.

Practice the purpose of tempering

Surface hardening puts wear resistance near the outside

Surface hardening puts wear resistance near the outside

A part may need a hard wear surface and a tougher interior. Carburizing adds carbon near the surface of suitable steel before hardening. Nitriding introduces nitrogen to develop a hard surface layer. These change surface chemistry.

Induction hardening heats a surface region electrically and then quenches it. It uses the steel's existing composition rather than adding carbon. It therefore needs a suitable starting steel. Case depth, surface hardness and core properties must be specified together.

Cold work strengthens; stress relief addresses locked-in stresses; Practice

Cold work strengthens; stress relief addresses locked-in stresses

Work hardening, also called strain hardening, occurs as metal is permanently deformed during cold working. It commonly raises strength and hardness while reducing remaining ductility. “Cold” here means below the relevant recrystallization range, not necessarily cold to the touch.

Stress relief reduces residual stresses left by processing. These are stresses that remain without an external load. The goal is often dimensional stability during later machining or service. Stress relief is not automatically a full softening anneal and does not guarantee zero distortion.

Practice restoring formability

Aluminum strengthening does not use steel's martensite route

Aluminum strengthening does not use steel's martensite route

Some aluminum alloys can be strengthened by solution treatment, quenching and aging. Solution treatment puts selected elements into solid solution; subsequent aging develops small particles that obstruct deformation. Aging can occur naturally or through controlled heating, depending on the alloy and required condition.

Solid-solution strengthening and precipitation strengthening are different mechanisms. Dissolved atoms provide the first; particles of another phase provide the second. Other aluminum alloys obtain much of their strength through cold work. Annealing can soften cold-worked material.

An aluminum temper describes its processing condition. It is not synonymous with the steel process called tempering. Preserve suffixes such as -O and -T6 when reading or writing a material description; consult the relevant alloy-temper specification for their exact meanings.

Cold work can increase strength and leave residual stresses. Removing material can release those stresses and move the part. Cold-finished does not automatically mean precision ground, and neither term guarantees a particular tolerance without a product specification.

Metals and Their Properties

How bonding, grains and alloy condition affect the properties used to choose a metal.

Metal selection starts with the job the part must do

Metal selection starts with the job the part must do

A metal can carry a load, conduct current, transfer heat or resist wear. Those are different jobs. Start with the required behavior, then compare a particular alloy in a particular condition. An alloy combines a base metal with other elements to change its behavior.

Metals belong to the material-family foundations topic. Use Material Properties for the general property vocabulary; this page applies it to metals. Continue with Types of Steel, Nonferrous Metals, Metal Stock Forms and Purchasing and Heat Treating Metals.

Metallic bonding helps explain conduction and forming

Metallic bonding helps explain conduction and forming

In a metal, some electrons are shared throughout the structure rather than tied to one bond between two atoms. Their mobility helps metals conduct electricity and heat. Metallic bonding can remain intact while atoms rearrange during permanent deformation, which helps explain why many metals can be rolled or drawn.

Most engineering metals contain many tiny crystals called grains. Atoms have an ordered arrangement inside each grain; adjacent grains have different orientations. Grain size, alloy chemistry and processing history all influence properties.

Density, conductivity and corrosion can decide the choice

Density, conductivity and corrosion can decide the choice

Density is mass per volume. Compare equal volumes when comparing densities; a large aluminum part can still weigh more than a small steel part. Use Geometry, Mass, and Force and Dimensional Analysis for mass, weight and unit calculations.

Corrosion resistance depends on the metal and its surroundings. Stainless steel and aluminum can corrode. Water, salts, trapped moisture and contact with other metals can change performance. A protective surface film helps only while the environment allows it to remain effective.

Magnetic response is a clue, not a material certificate. Some stainless steels are weakly magnetic or effectively nonmagnetic in certain conditions. Appearance and a magnet cannot establish an alloy, its temper or its strength.

A ratio by weight is not automatically a ratio by volume; Practice

A ratio by weight is not automatically a ratio by volume

For a specified A:B = a:b by weight and total mixed mass M:

A = M × a / (a + b)
B = M × b / (a + b)

Arithmetic example only: suppose the stated ratio is 10:1 by weight and the required total is 220 g. There are 11 ratio parts, so A = 220 × 10/11 = 200 g, and B = 220 × 1/11 = 20 g. The total is 220 g. If instead you already have 220 g of A, B would be 22 g and the total 242 g. Read what the given mass represents.

Tare the container so its mass is excluded. Weigh with a scale suitable for the quantities. For a volume ratio, measure volumes as directed. Different component densities mean equal masses need not occupy equal volumes. Use only the ratio basis provided for that product.

  57. Materials and Manufacturing Processes/Metals/Heat Treating Metals

    Addendum 57. Steel microstructures are not the four steel families
