Safety/Equipment

Equipment Categories and Supervision

Every machine in every lab falls into one of three categories. The category tells you who has to be standing there before you switch it on.

Three categories, one question

Three categories, one question

Every piece of equipment in the lab is Category 1, 2 or 3. The category answers one question: who has to be present before you switch it on?

Permission from an instructor is required in every case. What changes between categories is when that permission can be given, and who has to be in the room while the machine is running.

Category 1
Permission may be given in advance. Work alone once you have it.
Category 2
Permission at the time of use. A peer must be present throughout.
Category 3
Permission at the time of use. A peer and an instructor must be present throughout.

The category never removes a requirement. General safety procedures and PPE apply to every category, to every person present — see Equipment Operating Procedure and Personal Protective Equipment.

Category 1

Category 1

  • Can be used with instructor permission — may be given in advance or at the time of use.
  • Can be operated independently, without peer or instructor supervision, once permission is granted.
  • You must still follow all general safety procedures and PPE requirements.
Category 2

Category 2

  • Can be used with instructor permission — permission must be given at the time of use.
  • Does not require instructor supervision during operation.
  • Requires peer supervision present at all times during use.
  • Both the operator and the peer supervisor must follow all safety procedures and PPE requirements.
Category 3

Category 3

  • Can be used with instructor permission — permission must be given at the time of use.
  • Requires both peer AND instructor supervision present during operation.
  • The highest-risk equipment, requiring maximum safety oversight.
  • All participants — operator, peer and instructor — must follow safety procedures and PPE requirements.

Which category is my machine?

Safety/Universal Rules

Equipment Operating Procedure

The procedure that applies to every piece of equipment in every lab, every time you use it.

Before operating any equipment

Before operating any equipment

  • Obtain permission to operate the equipment from the instructor.
  • Verify training — ensure ALL operators have received proper equipment training.
  • Check PPE — confirm you are wearing all required personal protective equipment. See Personal Protective Equipment.
  • Perform a pre-use inspection — check for any required maintenance or safety issues.
  • Confirm safety guards are in place and functioning properly.
  • Secure the workpiece — ensure all material is properly clamped or held.
  • Communicate your intentions — tell others in the area what you are about to do.
During equipment operation

During equipment operation

  • Stay alert — maintain focus, and never leave equipment unattended while it is running.
  • Follow proper procedures — use equipment only as trained and as intended.

Machine-specific hazards — pinch points, measurement, housekeeping — are covered in Machine Hazards and Housekeeping.

After operating any equipment

After operating any equipment

  • Power down safely — turn off and disconnect the equipment from its power source.
  • Report issues immediately — notify the instructor of any broken or malfunctioning equipment.
  • Return and organize — put all equipment and tools back in their designated locations.
  • Restore to shutdown state — return the equipment to its original, safe configuration.
  • Clean thoroughly — remove all dust, chips, liquids and debris from the equipment and the work area.

The habit behind the last two is 5S — the same discipline, named and made routine.

Eye protection

Eye protection

Safety glasses are MANDATORY in the lab spaces at all times.

If you wear prescription glasses

  • Side shields that attach to your existing frames.
  • Oversized safety glasses that fit over corrective glasses.
  • Prescription safety glasses are also acceptable.

Lens requirements

  • Safety glasses must be clear or yellow tinted only.
  • Dark or shaded safety glasses are not acceptable for indoor lab use.
  • Must meet ANSI Z87.1 safety standards.
Hearing protection

Hearing protection

Industry standard: shop tools nearly always require hearing protection in professional settings. In a teaching lab the exposure is typically shorter than in production work, but the protection still matters.

When to use it

  • Required for prolonged exposure to loud equipment — grinders, sanders, planers and similar.

The rule of thumb: if you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone at arm’s length, it is loud enough to need protection. You do not need a meter, and most shop tools fail this test the moment they start.

The numbers behind it

OSHA measures noise as an average across the working day, in A-weighted decibels (dBA). Two figures matter:

85 dBA
The action level, averaged over 8 hours — the point at which a workplace must run a hearing conservation programme.
90 dBA
The permissible exposure limit, averaged over 8 hours.

Above 90 dBA the allowed time drops fast — every extra 5 dBA halves it:

Impact noise must never exceed 140 dB peak — a single hammer blow or nail gun can reach it, and that damage is immediate rather than cumulative.

These are OSHA workplace limits (29 CFR 1910.95). NIOSH recommends a stricter 85 dBA limit that halves the allowed time every 3 dBA rather than every 5. Hearing loss from noise does not come back, so treat these as ceilings, not targets.

Two types available

Earplugs
Small foam plugs inserted into the ear canal. Disposable or reusable. Available on request from the instructor.
Ear muffs
Over-ear cups covering the whole ear. Reusable and adjustable. Often preferred for intermittent use.

Both types significantly reduce harmful noise levels.

Foot protection

Foot protection

  • Minimum requirement: closed-toe shoes are MANDATORY — no sandals, no flip-flops, no open-toe footwear.

Recommended upgrades

  • Slip-resistant soles, for traction on surfaces that may be slippery.
  • Puncture-resistant soles, against sharp metal debris.
  • Steel-toe or composite-toe protection — recommended, not required.

Avoid

  • Canvas shoes or thin-soled footwear.
  • High heels or unstable footwear.
  • Anything that does not fully enclose your feet.

You will be standing for most of the lab period — choose something supportive and comfortable.

Respiratory protection

Respiratory protection

MANDATORY when using any grinder or sander, and when working with materials that produce hazardous dust.

Grinding galvanized material is PROHIBITED. Zinc fumes from galvanized steel are toxic when heated.

Health hazards

  • Metal dust and particles cause respiratory irritation and long-term lung damage.
  • Wood dust can cause allergic reactions.

Dust and fumes are not the same problem, and they do not take the same protection. The next section is the one that decides which you need.

Respiratory protection — what to wear

Respiratory protection — what to wear

A dust mask does nothing against fumes or vapours. An N95 is a particle filter. It does not stop gases, welding or brazing fume, solvent vapour, or anything else you can smell. Wearing one while you can smell the air is not protection.

For dust — grinding, sanding, sawing

N95 mask
Filters 95% of airborne particles. The minimum for dusty work.
Half- or full-face respirator
Fitted with a particulate filter, for heavy or extended dust.

For fumes and vapours

  • You need an approved respirator with the right chemical cartridge, not a dust mask. Ask the instructor which cartridge before you start.
  • A respirator only works if it seals. A loose mask, or a beard along the seal, defeats it.
  • If there is no correct cartridge available, the job does not happen until there is.

If you can smell fumes or taste particles, your protection is inadequate. Stop work immediately and upgrade it — or stop the job.

Chemicals and liquids carry their own handling and first-aid information — see Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets.

Clothing and jewelry

Clothing and jewelry

Loose clothing and accessories catch in rotating machinery. That is how this causes serious injury or death — it is not a dress code.

Prohibited

  • Loose-fitting clothing, or garments with dangling features — drawstrings, ties and similar.
  • Long sleeves, hoodies or jackets near rotating equipment.
  • ALL jewelry, except silicone wedding rings.
  • Neckties, scarves and lanyards.
Hair, and what to wear instead

Hair, and what to wear instead

Hair

  • Long hair must be secured back and kept away from equipment.
  • Use hair ties, clips or caps to prevent entanglement.

Recommended

  • Cotton or fire-retardant materials — preferred, not required.
  • Close-fitting, short-sleeved shirts.
  • Pants without excessive loose material.
Safety/Universal Rules

Lifting and Carrying

Team lifting above 40 lbs, and the technique that prevents the injury. At these loads, how you lift matters more than what it weighs.

Safe manual handling

Safe manual handling

Poor technique injures people at weights they thought were safe. At the loads handled in a lab like this one, how you lift matters more than what it weighs.

The problem is rarely that something is heavy. It is that it is big for its weight. Sheet goods, tube, extrusion and aluminum stock are all low density, so a 40 lb load is a large object — and a large object is one you cannot keep close to your body, cannot see past, and cannot get both hands near the middle of. Every rule below assumes you can hold the load close. Bulk is what takes that away.

Team lifting is required for

  • Anything over 40 lbs.
  • Items lighter than 40 lbs but awkward in shape, size or balance.
  • Any object that feels unstable or is difficult to grip.

The 40 lb line is as much about dropping it as about your back. A load you cannot fully control is a hazard to your feet, the floor, the workpiece and anyone walking past — well before it is a hazard to your spine.

Weight becomes the limiting factor when it defeats your technique, and where that point falls is different for every person and every shape. If you cannot keep the load close and your back straight, it is too heavy for you to lift alone — whatever the number says.

Proper lifting technique

Proper lifting technique

  • Plan the lift — clear your path and know where you are going.
  • Get close to the object before lifting.
  • Squat down using your leg muscles; keep your back straight.
  • Grip firmly with both hands; avoid awkward hand positions.
  • Lift smoothly using your legs, not your back.
  • Keep the load close to your body throughout the lift.

Setting it down

  • Use the same technique in reverse — squat down, do not bend over.
  • Place objects gently. Do not drop them.

Electrical Safety

Where the shock hazard actually starts, why 5 V can still burn you, the inspection required before every use, cords on the floor, and what to do when someone is shocked.

Voltage in this lab

Voltage in this lab

One warning does not cover this range. What can hurt you at 5 V is not what can hurt you at 240 V — they are not the same hazard, and treating them as one thing does not make you safer. It makes the warning easy to ignore.

5 V / 12 V DC
Low-voltage DC — bench supplies, cells and battery packs. Below the shock threshold. Not below the burn threshold.
120 V
Standard receptacles. Dangerous — can cause serious injury or death.
240 V / 480 V
Industrial equipment. Extremely dangerous — often fatal.

Voltage tells you whether a circuit can push current through you. It does not tell you what that circuit can do to a ring, a dropped tool or a shorted cell.

Those are two different hazards with two different thresholds. The next two sections are about where each one starts.

Where the shock hazard starts

Where the shock hazard starts

There is a real number and it is not a secret. In US workplace rules the line is 50 volts.

  • OSHA requires live parts to be de-energized before you work on or near them — and then says that “live parts that operate at less than 50 volts to ground need not be deenergized if there will be no increased exposure to electrical burns or to explosion due to electric arcs” (29 CFR 1910.333(a)(1)). Read that second half again; the rest of this page hangs on it.
  • At or above the line, live parts must be guarded against accidental contact. The rule is written for “electric equipment operating at 50 volts or more” (29 CFR 1910.303(g)(2)(i)).
  • That number applies to DC as well as AC. The standard does not distinguish between them, and OSHA has stated it “considers all voltages of 50 volts or above to be hazardous” (interpretation letter, 4 September 2015).
  • NFPA 70E draws the same line. Below 50 V you may work live only where the capacity of the source and any overcurrent protection have been considered and found not to add burn or arc exposure (2024 edition, 110.2(B) Exception No. 5).

The international standards are not one number

On datasheets you will meet a different set of figures, and there the AC and DC limits are not the same:

50 V AC / 120 V DC
Extra-Low Voltage under IEC 61140 and IEC 60364-4-41 — 50 V AC rms, or 120 V ripple-free DC. “Ripple-free” is a real condition, not a figure of speech: past roughly 10 % rms ripple the supply is treated as AC and the lower number applies.
30 V AC / 60 V DC
Class ES1 in IEC 62368-1, the standard behind most benchtop and consumer equipment. ES1 is the class that needs no safeguard at all; ES2 runs up to 50 V AC / 120 V DC and requires one.

In this lab, use 50 V — AC or DC. It is the number OSHA enforces, it is the most conservative of the figures above, and it assumes nothing about your skin being dry or your hands being clean. If a circuit is at or above 50 V, it is not something you work on live.

About the 48 volts you may have heard. 48 V DC is a system voltage, not a safety threshold. It is the telephone-exchange standard that Bell settled on, carried into military and commercial communications gear ever since, and more recently into 48 V vehicle electrical systems (ISO 21780). It was picked to sit deliberately just under the 50 V line so that technicians could keep working on it live. So 48 is the right neighborhood from the wrong side: the threshold is 50, and 48 is what engineers built in order to stay below it.

Low voltage is not low energy

Low voltage is not low energy

Below 50 V, shock stops being the hazard and short-circuit current becomes it. This is the half of the page people get wrong, because the voltage that cannot shock you is the one nobody is careful with.

Look at what the exemption above is actually conditioned on. Not shock — burns and arcs. NFPA 70E names the same condition as the capacity of the source. Both standards are telling you that once you are under the shock line, the question changes from how many volts to how many amps can this thing deliver into a dead short.

Where the current comes from

  • A bench supply set to 5 V will deliver its full rated current into a short without complaint. That is what the current limit is for — and if you have wound the limit up to get a motor turning, you have wound the protection off.
  • A lithium cell or pack has almost no internal resistance. Shorted, a single cell can dump tens of amps within milliseconds. A pack does considerably worse.
  • A lead-acid battery is rated in hundreds of cold-cranking amps. That rating is its short-circuit capability, described politely.

None of these can push meaningful current through your body at 12 V. All of them can push hundreds of amps through a piece of metal — and metal carrying current it was never sized for gets hot, fast.

Metal jewelry is the classic way this happens

ALL jewelry is already PROHIBITED here — see clothing and jewelry. That rule was written for rotating machinery. It earns its keep a second time on the bench, and OSHA writes it a second time for exactly this reason: conductive jewelry “may not be worn if they might contact exposed energized parts” (29 CFR 1910.333(c)(8)) — a rule with no voltage floor at all.

A ring or a metal watch band bridging a low-voltage source is a near-zero-resistance path across something that can supply hundreds of amps. The ring becomes the heating element. This is documented injury, not a story: a mechanic’s gold ring touched the positive terminal of a 12 V car battery while the wrench in his hand bridged the ring and the negative terminal, and the result was a deep circumferential burn around the base of the finger that needed a skin graft. A review of nine ring burns at one burn center found the same pattern: the small size of the burn hides how bad it is, because a burn that wraps a finger is far more likely to need grafting and to leave lasting loss of function than a burn of the same area anywhere else.

You can drop a wrench. You cannot drop a ring.

The silicone wedding ring that the PPE rule allows is also the right answer electrically: silicone does not conduct, so it cannot become the short.

A shorted lithium cell does not just get warm

An external short is one of the recognized triggers for thermal runaway — the cell generates heat faster than it can shed it, and the outcome is “extremely high temperatures, violent cell venting, smoke and fire” (UL Research Institutes). Once it has started, disconnecting it does not stop it. The cell is now its own energy source.

NEVER leave loose cells or packs where metal can bridge the terminals — a drawer with tools in it, a pocket with keys in it, a bin with offcuts in it. Federal transport rules require lithium cells and batteries to be packed so their terminals cannot short (49 CFR 173.185). The reason that rule exists is the reason to do the same thing on your own bench.

A cell that has been shorted, dropped, crushed, punctured or has swollen is out of service. Tell the instructor. Do not put it back on charge to find out. See Fire Safety.

The sentence to take away: low voltage does not mean low energy. Voltage decides whether a circuit can shock you. Current decides whether it can burn you — and current is set by what the source can deliver and what is in the path, not by the number printed on the front of the supply.

Inspection — mandatory before each use

Inspection — mandatory before each use

Every cord, every time, before it goes in the receptacle. It takes about five seconds, and it is the one check that catches most of this page before it happens rather than after.

  • Run the cord through your hand end to end. Look for frayed, cracked, cut or exposed insulation, and for flat spots where something has been driven over it.
  • Check the plug: blades all present, straight, tight in the body, not discolored or pitted. Check the ground pin is still there — a missing ground pin means the cord is out of service, not that you have found a workaround.
  • Check where the cord enters the plug and enters the tool. That is where flexing breaks conductors, and it is where the damage hides.
  • Inspect extension cords for cuts, crushing and wear, and check the receptacle end for scorching or melted plastic.

If you find damage, stop, unplug it, and notify the instructor. Not “just for this one cut”. Tag it or hand it over, so the next person does not find it the hard way.

Tape is not a repair. OSHA allows electrical tape over superficial scuffs in the outer jacket, and has stated just as plainly that “tape repairs of the jacket may not be used to bring a worn or frayed flexible cord into compliance” (interpretation letter, 16 December 1998). If conductors or their insulation are showing, the cord is done.

Plugs, cords and connections

Plugs, cords and connections

  • Always disconnect power before performing any maintenance or adjustment.
  • NEVER yank or pull a cord — grasp the plug, not the cord. Pulling by the cord wrecks the strain relief where you cannot see it, and it is how a plug ends up half out of the receptacle with the blades exposed.
  • Push a plug fully home, and look at it. A plug that will not seat is a fault to report, not a fit problem to force.
  • Keep electrical connections away from water and wet surfaces. Wet skin has on the order of 1,000 ohms of resistance where dry skin has 100,000 ohms or more, so water moves you roughly a hundredfold closer to the current every rule on this page is trying to keep out of you.
  • Be aware of overhead wiring when you are handling long stock, moving a ladder or lifting.

A partly unplugged appliance is a short waiting for something to land on it

Exposed blades sit at full line voltage with nothing between them but air. Anything conductive that falls across them completes the circuit straight across the supply — and in a shop, the thing that falls is a metal chip. What follows is not a shock. It is an arc, at whatever fault current a 120 V branch circuit can deliver, throwing molten metal. The breaker will clear it; a breaker protects the wiring, not your face, and it does not act until after the arc.

Plugs are all the way in or all the way out. Nothing in between, and nothing left in between.

Chips travel further than you expect and they conduct — see Metal Chips and Swarf. That is the direct line between the housekeeping rules and this one.

Lockout/Tagout for machinery with rotating parts is covered in Machine Hazards and Housekeeping.

Cords on the floor — the one most likely to get you

Cords on the floor — the one most likely to get you

Nothing else on this page is as likely to actually injure someone as a cord lying across a walkway. It is not a shock hazard at all. It is a trip hazard — and a trip in a shop is not a trip onto carpet. It is a fall into a machine, onto hot metal, or with a running tool still in your hand.

The regulation treats this as a floor problem rather than an electrical one: “walking-working surfaces are maintained free of hazards such as sharp or protruding objects, loose boards, corrosion, leaks, spills, snow, and ice” (29 CFR 1910.22(a)(3)), and passageways and work areas are kept “in a clean, orderly, and sanitary condition” (29 CFR 1910.22(a)(1)). A cord across an aisle is an obstruction in an aisle.

What to do, in order of preference

  • Do not cross the walkway. Move the work to the outlet, or use a different outlet. This is almost always available, and it is almost never the first thing anyone tries.
  • Route along the wall or the bench line rather than across open floor. Take the long way around the edge of the room.
  • Go overhead where a drop or a reel is provided. That is what they are for.
  • If it genuinely must cross, cover or anchor it. OSHA’s own guidance is to “ensure that temporary electrical cords that cross aisles are taped or anchored to the floor”. A cord ramp or cord cover does the same job better and protects the cord as well as the person.
  • Use the shortest cord that reaches. Slack on the floor is the hazard, and a coiled surplus in a walkway is the same hazard in a tighter package.
  • Unplug it and put it away when you are done, in the same movement as shutting the tool down. The cord left out after the job is finished is the one nobody is expecting.

PROHIBITED — running a flexible cord through a doorway, a window, or a hole in a wall, ceiling or floor, and attaching it to building surfaces. Those are named prohibitions in the electrical standard (29 CFR 1910.305(g)(1)(iv)), along with using flexible cord as a substitute for the fixed wiring of a building. Flexible cord is temporary by definition. A cord treated as permanent gets pinched, crushed and forgotten.

NEVER walk past a cord lying across a walkway and leave it because it is not yours. If you can see it, you can move it or say something. The person who trips over it will not have seen it.

The same standard requires flexible cords to be protected from accidental damage at sharp corners, projections, doorways and other pinch points (29 CFR 1910.305(a)(2)(x)) — the same rule read from the cord’s side. A cord that gets walked on is a cord that fails the inspection above. This section and that one are the same problem twice.

Keeping walkways clear is the Set in Order and Shine part of 5S. The safety case and the lean case are the same case.

If someone is shocked

If someone is shocked

Do NOT touch them directly. If current is still flowing they are part of the circuit, and grabbing them puts you in it as well.

  • Kill the power first — the machine switch, the disconnect, the breaker, or pull the plug.
  • If you cannot kill it, use a dry, non-conductive object to separate them from the source, and stand on something dry while you do it.
  • Then call for help and start first aid.

Call 911 and notify the instructor — follow emergency procedure.

A small mark is not evidence of a small injury. Electrical and electrothermal burns run deep with very little to see on the surface, and a burn that wraps a finger can cost the use of it. Any shock, and any electrical burn, gets reported and gets looked at — however fine the person says they are.

Fire Safety

When to fight a fire and when to leave, and how to use an extinguisher.

Decide before you reach for anything

Decide before you reach for anything

An extinguisher is for a small fire you caught early.

Fight it only if all four are true

  • The fire is small and contained — a bin, a rag, a puddle. Not spreading.
  • You know what is burning, and you have the right extinguisher for it.
  • You have a clear exit behind you and the fire is not between you and it.
  • Someone else has already raised the alarm.

Otherwise: get out. Pull the alarm, leave, call 911 from outside, and tell the instructor and the fire crew what was burning.

If you try and it does not go out in the first few seconds, stop and leave. A hand extinguisher lasts roughly 10–20 seconds. That is your whole attempt.

Using an extinguisher — PASS

Using an extinguisher — PASS

Remember the acronym: P–A–S–S.

When it looks out, back away watching the fire, exit behind you. Do not assume it is over — a knocked-down fire can reignite.

Fire classes, and what puts them out

Fire classes, and what puts them out

Fires are classed by what is burning. An extinguisher is rated for the classes it can handle, and using the wrong one ranges from useless to dangerous.

    Most extinguishers on a wall are ABC dry chemical — one unit covering the first three classes. Check the label; the classes it is rated for are printed on it.

    Metal fires, and what it takes to start one

    Metal fires, and what it takes to start one

    Metal burns only when it is divided finely enough, and then only when several conditions line up at once. Ordinary aluminum chips and swarf will not do this, and neither will the quantities of fine material a lab space like this one generates. Solid stock does not burn at all. The cases below are the ones that are genuinely different.

    The form matters more than the metal

    • Magnesium is the real Class D case. Thin sections, chips and turnings ignite readily and burn with intense heat and a brilliant white light, and dull tools alone can produce enough heat during machining to start it.
    • Titanium turnings can ignite, and titanium fines and grinding dust are worse — fine enough that a static discharge is a credible ignition source.
    • Aluminum fines — the dust off sanding, grinding, buffing and polishing — are a genuine hazard, but only through the specific mechanisms below.
    • Aluminum chips and swarf are hard to ignite. Aluminum carries heat away from a hot spot fast, it wears a tough oxide skin that holds together while the metal underneath melts, and a chip has little surface area for its mass. Put a torch to a pile of chips and they melt.
    • Solid stock does not burn. A block of aluminum is not fuel.

    Magnesium and titanium are not routine materials here. A senior project might bring one in. If you are about to cut, grind or sand either one, ask first — every time.

    Particle size is the line

    Aluminum coarser than about half a millimeter will not sustain an explosion and finer aluminum can — one line, which different editions of the standard write as 500 microns or as 420 microns (fine enough to pass a 40-mesh sieve), not two separate limits. Machining chips are far coarser than that line. Sanding and grinding dust is well under it. That is the whole difference between a chip tray and a dust collector.

    The ways fine metal actually ignites

    • A suspended dust cloud meeting an ignition source. Fines sitting in a tray are not the hazard. Fines thrown into the air, at the right concentration, reaching a flame, a torch, a spark or a static discharge, are — burning travels from one particle to the next with extreme speed. It is why you never blow fine dust around, and why you do not raise a cloud of it cleaning up.
    • Fines heavily mixed with rust or grinding scale. Aluminum plus iron oxide — rust, or the scale off steel grinding — is a thermite reaction: the aluminum strips the oxygen off the iron, so the mixture burns without needing air from outside. That is what makes it a metal fire rather than an ordinary one, and why water and an ABC extinguisher do nothing to it. It takes a real mixture to go — something near three parts iron oxide to one part aluminum by weight, and an ignition source far hotter than a spark. That is a rust-caked pile, not a trace. See Metal Chips and Swarf for how far apart that threshold and the recycling one actually sit.
    • Wet fines. Aluminum fines in contact with water slowly give off hydrogen. That is a reason not to seal damp fines into a closed container, and part of the reason water is the wrong thing to put on a metal fire.

    If fine metal does ignite

    Do not fight it. Most general-purpose shops have no Class D extinguisher — assume you do not have one unless somebody has shown you where it is. Pull the alarm, get out, call 911, and tell the fire crew it is a metal fire. That changes how they fight it, and they need to know before they arrive with a hose.

    PROHIBITED — the ABC extinguisher off the wall. “Dry powder” (Class D) and “dry chemical” (ABC) are not the same thing. An ABC unit throws an incompatible agent at pressure: it scatters burning fines and spreads the fire.

    NEVER put water on a metal fire. The stream throws burning material, can raise an explosive dust cloud, and reacts with the metal to release hydrogen.

    Do not improvise. Alarm, evacuate, report.

    What that means day to day

    • Do not let fine dust accumulate. Empty trays, vacuums and collectors rather than letting them fill — see Machine Hazards and Housekeeping for housekeeping.
    • Chips and fines are disposed of differently, and the difference matters — see Metal Chips and Swarf.
    • Let hot chips and debris cool before they go anywhere near a bin of paper or rag.
    • Ask before you machine an unfamiliar metal. If you do not know what it is, that is the answer: ask.
    Hot work and the things that start fires

    Hot work and the things that start fires

    • Grinding throws sparks a surprising distance. Clear rag, sawdust, solvent and packaging out of the area before you start, not after it smolders.
    • Oily rags — it depends entirely on which oil. Drying oils — linseed, tung, oil-based stains, varnishes and finishes — cure by reacting with oxygen, and that reaction gives off heat. Bunched in a pile the rags hold the heat in, and the pile can reach ignition on its own with nothing to light it. Those rags NEVER go in a pile: spread them flat to dry, drop them in water, or use the designated container.
    • Petroleum oils do not do that. Way oil, cutting fluid, hydraulic oil and ordinary shop oil do not cure by oxidation, so a rag wiped with them will not self-heat and will not spontaneously combust. It is still combustible — a spark or a hot chip will light it like any oily cloth — so keep it away from hot work and out of a bin of paper or sawdust. If you do not know which oil is on the rag, treat it as a drying oil.
    • Flammable liquids live in the fireproof cabinet when they are not in your hand — see Chemicals and Safety Data Sheets.
    • A smoldering fire can restart long after you leave. If something got hot enough to smoke, tell the instructor before you go.
    Know these before you need them

    Know these before you need them

    None of the above helps if you are looking for equipment while a fire grows. On your first day in any lab space, locate:

    • The nearest fire extinguisher, and what classes it is rated for.
    • The nearest alarm pull station.
    • Both ways out of the room, and the route that does not pass the machines.
    • Where your class gathers outside, so you can be accounted for.
    • The first aid kit and the emergency stops — see Lab Conduct and Emergencies.
    Safety/Equipment

    Metal Chips and Swarf

    Chips come off hot, razor sharp and fast. How to handle them, how to clean them up, and the rules on compressed air.

    Five reasons a chip is dangerous

    Five reasons a chip is dangerous

    A chip is not scrap until it has cooled and stopped moving. Straight off the tool it is every one of these at once.

    Hot
    Chips can be upwards of 1000 °F the moment they are cut. They do not look it.
    Sharp
    The leading edge of a chip is always razor sharp. It is a freshly cut edge and it will open a hand.
    Fast
    Chips leaving a machine can travel at over 250 mph. That is why eye protection is not negotiable — see Personal Protective Equipment.
    Strong
    A long continuous chip is a steel ribbon. If it wraps a sleeve or a limb it can pull you into the machine.
    Slippery
    Chips on the floor roll underfoot. This is one of the most common ways people get hurt without the machine touching them.
    Never clear chips with your hands

    Never clear chips with your hands

    Do not reach into a machine to clear chips while it is running, and do not pick up a long stringy chip by hand at any time. Stop the spindle, then deal with it.

    Gloves

    • Gloves may only be used for cleanup after the machine is shut down.
    • Never wear gloves at a running machine. A glove catches where bare skin would pull free, and takes your hand with it — see Machine Hazards and Housekeeping.

    Brush

    • A brush may be used while the machine is in operation, but with extreme care.
    • On a CNC machine, take particular care: the tool can move without warning, and the enclosure invites you to reach further in than you should.
    Compressed air — there are limits on this

    Compressed air — there are limits on this

    Compressed air is convenient and it is the option people reach for first. It is also regulated, because a chip in a moving air stream is a projectile and air forced against skin can be driven into the body.

    The rule

    OSHA permits compressed air for cleaning only where it is reduced to less than 30 psi, and only then with effective chip guarding and personal protective equipment (29 CFR 1910.242(b)). All three conditions, together — not the pressure alone.

    In practice

    • Check the regulator is set for cleaning, not shop pressure, before you pull the trigger.
    • Eye protection, every time. Everyone nearby too — you do not get to decide the range.
    • Never at skin, yours or anyone else’s.
    • Never toward another person, and check who is behind the machine first.
    • Never into an enclosed space.
    • If air would just relocate the mess into the air or across the shop, use a brush or the vacuum instead.
    Cleaning up

    Cleaning up

    • Use cleaning equipment or gloves to handle chips for disposal — the sharp edge does not dull because the machine stopped.
    • Clear the machine so only the residue in the bottom tray remains.
    • Sweep the floor, and mop if the floor is wet with coolant.
    • If cleaning equipment such as a vacuum gets wet, open it up and let it dry before it is put away.

    Chips and fines do not go in the same place

    • Chips and swarf go in the recycling container. That container is for chips only.
    • Fines go in the trash. Dust from sanding, grinding, buffing and polishing, and what comes out of a vacuum or dust collector — never the recycling container.

    Why — two reasons, and the second one is the safety reason

    • The recycler pays for clean aluminum. The scrap specification for aluminum turnings requires the load to be free of free iron, and once iron is in an aluminum melt ordinary refining does not take it back out — so the buyer prices what is actually in the bin, not what you meant to put there. Fines pick iron up: off a shared belt or wheel, off steel work at the next bench, out of the vacuum. Fine material is a deduction in its own right as well. Iron in the fines is the reason fines do not go in.
    • Compare how often each container actually leaves the building. A chip recycling container fills slowly and can stand most of a semester between pickups; the trash goes out daily. Fines belong in the stream that leaves, not the one that stands in the corner for months quietly collecting more of them. Anything whose problem is accumulation does not go in the container designed to accumulate.

    Two thresholds, same observation. “There is iron in the fines” is a real problem for the recycling price and not a fire problem — and the reason both are true at once is that the two thresholds are orders of magnitude apart. The scrap spec is written against traces: the load has to be free of free iron, and a few steel chips are enough to drop it to a contaminated price. A thermite reaction needs iron oxide — rust or grinding scale, not bright steel — at something near three parts oxide to one part aluminum by weight, plus an ignition source far hotter than a spark. That is most of the pile, not a trace. Most rules in a shop have a number behind them; knowing which number you are being measured against, and how far you actually are from it, is the difference between following a rule and understanding it.

    Fine dust burns; chips do not. Aluminum chips and swarf are hard to ignite. The fire hazard is fines — and then only when a cloud of them is thrown into the air and meets an ignition source, or when a great deal of rust or steel grinding scale is mixed into them. Do not let fine dust accumulate, and do not raise a cloud of it cleaning up.

    If fine metal debris does ignite, do not fight it. The ABC extinguisher on the wall is the wrong tool and will scatter it. Alarm, evacuate, and tell the fire crew it is a metal fire. See Fire Safety.

    Lean and Quality/Lean and Organization

    5S Workplace Organization

    The five-step workplace organization method. It is a lean manufacturing practice, and it is also why a tidy shop is a safer one.

    Why 5S sits in a safety module

    Why 5S sits in a safety module

    5S is a lean manufacturing method for organizing a workplace. It earns its place here because most of what it asks for is also what keeps people from getting hurt.

    A cluttered bench hides a hazard. An unlabeled container is an unknown chemical. A floor with chips on it is a slip. Every one of those is a 5S failure before it is an incident — which is why the shutdown steps in After operating any equipment are 5S in everything but name.

    This page is the basics. 5S will get its own expanded treatment later.

    Sort — Seiri

    Sort — Seiri

    • Separate necessary items from unnecessary ones.
    • Remove or relocate items that do not belong in the work area.
    • Keep only what you need for the task in front of you.
    Set in Order — Seiton

    Set in Order — Seiton

    • Organize and label the remaining items clearly, for easy identification.
    • A place for everything, and everything in its place.
    • Create logical, efficient storage systems.
    Shine — Seiso

    Shine — Seiso

    • Clean work areas to maintain high hygiene standards.
    • Reduce accident risk through cleanliness and organization.
    • Inspect while cleaning — that is how problems get found early.

    This is the step that overlaps most directly with safety — see the environment around the machine.

    Standardize — Seiketsu

    Standardize — Seiketsu

    • Establish consistent procedures for maintaining the first three S’s.
    • Create habits through regular practice and routine.
    • Ensure everyone follows the same organizational systems.
    Sustain — Shitsuke

    Sustain — Shitsuke

    • Continuously review and maintain all 5S practices.
    • Build the discipline to follow established procedures long-term.
    • Regular audits, and improvements to the system itself.
