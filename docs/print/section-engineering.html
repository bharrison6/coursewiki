Dimensional Analysis

Convert quantities by multiplying by named equalities, cancelling units, and treating squared and cubed units as powers.

Convert by multiplying by one

Convert by multiplying by one

A conversion factor is a ratio of two exactly equal physical quantities written as a fraction. For example, 1 ft and 0.3048 m name exactly the same length. Their ratio therefore has the numerical value 1, so multiplying by it changes the unit expression without changing the physical quantity.

Either orientation is valid: 1 ft / 0.3048 m and 0.3048 m / 1 ft both equal 1. Choose the orientation that cancels the unwanted unit. To convert 12 m to feet, put meters on the bottom:

12 m × 1 ft0.3048 m = 39.370078740… ft ≈ 39.3701 ft.

For the reverse conversion, turn the same exact equality over so feet cancel:

39.370078740… ft × 0.3048 m1 ft = 12 m.

Do not memorize an arrow. Write the desired unit, then arrange the factor so the unwanted unit cancels. The units tell you whether the fraction is facing the correct way before you calculate.

Exact, defined
1 in = 0.0254 m, so 1 ft = 0.3048 m and 1 yd = 0.9144 m exactly.
Keep guard digits
Use exact factors or sufficient digits while calculating; round the displayed result only at the end and only as the task needs.
Match dimensions
Length converts to length, area to area, volume to volume, and force to force. A calculator cannot make incompatible dimensions meaningful.
Powers apply to the whole conversion

Powers apply to the whole conversion

Area and volume are not lengths with decorative superscripts. The conversion factor itself must be squared or cubed.

Why a single linear factor is not enough.

For liquid capacity, 1 m3 = 1,000 L and 1 U.S. gal = 3.785411784 L. The converter labels this as a U.S. liquid gallon so it cannot be mistaken for an Imperial gallon.

CNC production calculation

CNC production calculation

A CNC program estimates 4 min 15 s per part. The operator uses 30 min of an 8 h shift for setup. How many complete parts fit in the shift?

  1. Convert the cycle: 4 min 15 s = 4.25 min.
  2. Find machine-running time: 8 h × (60 min / 1 h) − 30 min = 450 min.
  3. Divide running time by cycle time: 450 min / 4.25 min per part = 105.882....

105 complete parts. The fractional result is time into the 106th cycle, not a finished part. This assumes one machine, no unplanned stops, and that the stated cycle time already includes every repeatable operation.

Engineering Fundamentals/Units and Measurement

Measurement Language

Use quantities, units, symbols, and SI prefixes precisely before a drawing, program, or calculation has a chance to drift.

A measurement is a number and a unit

A measurement is a number and a unit

A dimension on a drawing, a speed in a robot program, and a torque setting are all quantities: a number paired with a unit. The number tells how many; the unit tells what kind of thing and how large each part is.

25 mm
A length: twenty-five millimeters. Writing only 25 leaves the measurement incomplete.
4.25 min
A CNC cycle time. It is not interchangeable with 4.25 seconds or 4.25 hours.
3.0 m/s
A speed or velocity magnitude. The slash means meters per second.

Carry units through every line of a calculation. They are an error check, not decoration. If the last line needs a length and the units say seconds, stop before the wrong number reaches a drawing or controller.

Use the unit symbol exactly as specified: m for meter, s for second, kg for kilogram. Symbols are not pluralized and do not take a period: 12 mm, not 12 mms.

Two systems you will encounter

Two systems you will encounter

The International System of Units (SI) is the standard system for science and much technical work. U.S. drawings, hardware, materials, and equipment also use U.S. customary units. Neither system lets us skip the unit; the job is to state which one is being used and convert deliberately when they meet.

SI base units used here
meter (m) for length, second (s) for time, and kilogram (kg) for mass.
U.S. customary units used here
inch (in), foot (ft), mile (mi), second (s), pound mass (lbm), and U.S. liquid gallon (U.S. gal).
Coherent BG engineering system
The source slides call the coherent engineering form BG (British Gravitational): foot, slug, and second. In that system, slug · ft/s2 = lbf. A pound force and a pound mass are not the same unit.

A gallon must be named. This unit set uses the U.S. liquid gallon (U.S. gal), not an Imperial gallon.

Engineering Fundamentals/Motion and Rotation

Acceleration and Squared Time

Calculate change in velocity over time, distinguish average from instantaneous acceleration, and convert squared time units correctly.

Acceleration is change in velocity over time

Acceleration is change in velocity over time

Acceleration is not simply “speeding up.” It is a change in velocity, so it can describe speeding up, slowing down, or turning.

aavg = Δv / Δt = (vf − vi) / (tf − ti)

Δv
Change in velocity. With one chosen positive direction, Δv = vf − vi.
m/s2
Meters per second per second. Each second, velocity changes by the stated number of meters per second.
ft/s2
Feet per second per second. The coherent foot–slug–second system uses this acceleration unit with slugs for mass.

Write the sign. A final velocity lower than the initial velocity produces negative acceleration in the selected direction; it is not an error just because the answer is negative.

Average is an interval; instantaneous is a moment

Average is an interval; instantaneous is a moment

Average acceleration is calculated over stated start and end conditions. An accelerometer or control system can report instantaneous acceleration at one moment. They match only when the acceleration stays constant through the interval.

Machine arm example

A machine arm starts from rest and reaches 3 m/s in 4 s:

aavg = (3 m/s − 0 m/s) / 4 s = 0.75 m/s2.

This is the average acceleration for the four-second move. If the drive ramps gently at the start and end, its instantaneous acceleration changes during the move even though the average remains 0.75 m/s2.

Robot gripper example

A SolidWorks motion study programs a gripper from 0 to 5 ft/s in 0.5 s to pick an electronic component. Its average acceleration is 5 ft/s / 0.5 s = 10 ft/s2. In SI, 10 ft/s2 × (0.3048 m / 1 ft) = 3.048 m/s2.

Squared time needs a squared factor

Squared time needs a squared factor

Acceleration has time in the denominator twice. When converting an acceleration unit, convert the entire squared time relationship, not just one s.

1 min = 60 s means 1 min2 = (60 s)2 = 3,600 s2.

Use the inverse relationship in the correct direction. 1 ft/s2 = 0.3048 m/s2, not 0.30482 m/s2. The foot is the only unit being changed in that expression; seconds are already seconds in both units.

By contrast, converting m/min2 to m/s2 requires the full squared time factor. The unit cancellation shows why:

1 m/min2 × (1 min / 60 s)2 = 1/3,600 m/s2.

Angular Motion

Measure rotation in degrees, radians, and revolutions, then use angular velocity and angular acceleration to describe a turning shaft.

Rotation is measured three ways

Rotation is measured three ways

Angular displacement is how far something has turned. Its symbol is θ (theta), and there are three units in everyday use for it. They measure the same rotation; they differ only in how much turning counts as one unit.

Revolution (r)
One complete turn. This is what a machine nameplate counts.
Degree (°)
One revolution cut into 360 parts. This is what a drawing and a protractor use.
Radian (rad)
The angle whose arc is exactly as long as the radius. This is the SI unit, and it is the one every rotation formula expects.
The three units, describing one and the same rotation.

The two factors worth keeping to hand: 1° = 0.01745329 rad and 1 rad = 180°/π = 57.29578°.

The degree sign sits tight against the number — 90°, not 90 °. Every other unit symbol takes a space: 1.57 rad, 45 r/min. The degree, minute, and second of plane angle are the only exceptions to that rule.

Angular velocity is how fast it turns

Angular velocity is how fast it turns

Angular velocity uses the symbol ω (omega), and it is angle divided by time:

ω = θ / t

rad/s
Radian per second. The SI unit, and the form every formula on Rotation and Linear Speed needs.
r/min, or rpm
Revolutions per minute. What a motor nameplate, a spindle display, and a speed chart are marked in.

Nameplates speak rpm; formulas speak rad/s. Convert before you calculate, and convert back before you set a machine.

The exact pair, either way round:

1 r/min = 0.1047198 rad/s  ·  1 rad/s = 9.549297 r/min

Worked example

Convert 12.5 rpm to rad/s. Put revolutions per minute on the bottom so they cancel:

12.5 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s1 r/min = 1.309 rad/s.

Supporting detail: Addendum 8 — Where 0.1047198 comes from.

Angular acceleration is how fast the turning changes

Angular acceleration is how fast the turning changes

Angular acceleration uses the symbol α (alpha), and it is change in angular velocity divided by the time it took:

α = Δω / Δt

Its SI unit is rad/s2. Everything true of straight-line acceleration on Acceleration and Squared Time is true here as well: it is a change, so a shaft slowing down has a negative angular acceleration in whichever direction you called positive, and the squared second means a squared factor whenever the time unit changes.

Worked example: a motor speeding up

A motor shaft is turning at 17 rpm. Power to the motor is increased, and 1.1 s later it is turning 8 rpm faster. What is the angular acceleration in rad/s2?

  1. Only the change matters, so convert the 8 rpm increase: 8 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s per r/min = 0.837758 rad/s.
  2. Divide by the time: 0.837758 rad/s ÷ 1.1 s = 0.7616 rad/s2.

0.76 rad/s2, as an average over those 1.1 s. The starting 17 rpm never enters the calculation — acceleration is built out of the change, not out of the speed.

Worked example: a spindle running up

A machining-centre spindle goes from rest to 8,000 rpm in 2.5 s.

  1. 8,000 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s per r/min = 837.758 rad/s.
  2. (837.758 rad/s − 0 rad/s) ÷ 2.5 s = 335.10 rad/s2.

That is the average over the run-up. A drive that ramps gently at each end has a changing instantaneous angular acceleration inside the same interval, exactly as a linear drive does.

Engineering Fundamentals/Motion and Rotation

Motion Quantities

Distinguish path length from displacement, and speed from velocity, before using time to describe a machine or vehicle in motion.

Path length and displacement answer different questions

Path length and displacement answer different questions

Distance is how much path was traveled. Displacement is the directed change from the starting position to the ending position. A return trip can add distance while leaving displacement at zero.

Distance
A nonnegative scalar path length. A conveyor that moves a tote 12 m out and 12 m back travels 24 m of distance.
Displacement
A change in position with direction. If the tote returns to its start, its displacement is 0 m even though it moved.
Coordinate choice
Choose a positive direction before calculating. Along a straight robot rail, right might be positive and left negative.

Distance tells what the machine did along its path; displacement tells where it ended up relative to where it began. Do not substitute one for the other just because both use meters or feet.

Speed and velocity are not synonyms

Speed and velocity are not synonyms

Average speed is total distance divided by elapsed time. Average velocity is displacement divided by elapsed time. Velocity includes direction; speed does not.

average speed = total distance / elapsed time
average velocity = displacement / elapsed time

Notation bridge: this site uses x for position and Δx for displacement. Some course materials use s for position instead. For a stated interval, write average velocity as vavg = Δx / Δt; the symbols differ, but the physical idea is the same.

For the 12 m out-and-back path completed in 16 s, average speed is 24 m / 16 s = 1.5 m/s. Average velocity is 0 m / 16 s = 0 m/s. Both statements can be true at the same time.

A production-line example

A mechanism must carry a part 12 m in 8 s. Its required average speed is 12 m / 8 s = 1.5 m/s. Converting with exact factors gives 1.5 m/s × (39.37007874 in / 1 m) = 59.055... in/s, or 59.1 in/s. If the programmed direction is stated, its average velocity is +59.1 in/s in that direction.

Average and instantaneous readings

Average and instantaneous readings

An average uses a time interval. An instantaneous reading describes one moment: a car speedometer reports instantaneous speed; a robot controller’s live velocity value is an instantaneous velocity in its coordinate frame.

  • Use average speed for throughput, travel-time, and trip planning.
  • Use average velocity for a net movement over a stated interval.
  • Use an instantaneous reading when clearance, collision avoidance, or a machine limit depends on what is happening now.

Engineering Fundamentals/Motion and Rotation

Rotation and Linear Speed

Turn a shaft speed into the speed of what it drives, and back again, using the radius - belts, rollers, wheels, and grinding wheels.

The radius is what links turning to travelling

The radius is what links turning to travelling

A roller turns; the belt on it travels. A wheel turns; the vehicle moves. The two speeds are not the same number, and the thing that converts one into the other is the radius.

v = ωr  ·  a = αr

v
The straight-line speed of a point on the rim — the speed of the belt, the sheet, the vehicle.
ω
Angular velocity, in radians per second. See Angular Motion for the conversion from rpm.
r
The radius, in the same length unit you want the answer in. Rollers and wheels are almost always specified by diameter, so halve it first.

Both of these formulas are only true when the angle is in radians. Feed them rpm and the answer is wrong by a factor of about 9.55; feed them degrees per second and it is wrong by a factor of about 57.3. Convert to rad/s first, every time.

Two mistakes account for nearly every wrong answer here: using the diameter where the radius belongs, and leaving the speed in rpm. Both are worth a deliberate check before the arithmetic starts.

Supporting detail: Addendum 12 — Why the radian is the only angle unit that works.

Worked example: a conveyor drive roller

Worked example: a conveyor drive roller

A conveyor belt runs over a drive roller 200 mm in diameter, turning at 45 rpm. What is the belt speed, and how long does a package take to travel 15 m?

  1. Halve the diameter: r = 100 mm = 0.100 m.
  2. Convert the speed: 45 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s per r/min = 4.712389 rad/s.
  3. Multiply: v = 4.712389 rad/s × 0.100 m = 0.4712 m/s.
  4. Divide the distance by the speed: 15 m ÷ 0.4712389 m/s = 31.83 s.

0.47 m/s, and about 32 s for 15 m. That assumes the belt does not slip on the roller, which is the assumption a real drive is designed to make true.

Worked example: a roll-forming feed rate

Worked example: a roll-forming feed rate

Forming rolls 150 mm in diameter rotate at 25 rpm. What is the feed rate of the sheet in m/min, and how long does 20 m of sheet take?

  1. r = 75 mm = 0.075 m.
  2. 25 r/min × 0.1047198 rad/s per r/min = 2.617994 rad/s.
  3. v = 2.617994 rad/s × 0.075 m = 0.19635 m/s.
  4. The question asks for metres per minute, so convert the time unit: 0.19635 m/s × 60 s/min = 11.78 m/min.
  5. 20 m ÷ 11.78 m/min = 1.70 min, which is about 1 min 42 s.

Note what step 4 did and did not do. Changing seconds to minutes in a speed takes one factor of 60. If the quantity had been an acceleration, the same change would take 602 — see Acceleration and Squared Time.

Worked example: running the conversion backwards

Worked example: running the conversion backwards

Sometimes the linear quantity is what you know. The formulas simply rearrange: ω = v/r and α = a/r.

A wheel, from angular to linear

A tyre 28 in in diameter turns at 3.5 rad/s. How fast is the vehicle moving, in miles per hour?

  1. r = 14 in × 0.0254 m/in = 0.3556 m.
  2. v = 3.5 rad/s × 0.3556 m = 1.2446 m/s.
  3. 1.2446 m/s ÷ 0.44704 m/s per mph = 2.78 mph.

A wheel, from linear to angular

A vehicle accelerates at 5 m/s2 on tyres 78 cm in diameter. What is the angular acceleration of a wheel?

  1. r = 39 cm = 0.39 m.
  2. α = 5 m/s2 ÷ 0.39 m = 12.8 rad/s2.

Check the unit on the answer, not just the number. Dividing m/s2 by m leaves 1/s2, and because the radian is a ratio of two lengths, rad/s2 is the same thing named for what it describes.

Rim speed is why wheel size changes the safe rpm

Rim speed is why wheel size changes the safe rpm

A grinding wheel is limited by how fast its rim moves, not by how fast its centre turns. Put a bigger wheel on the same spindle at the same rpm and the rim goes faster, because every point on it travels a longer circle in the same time.

Take a wheel whose rim speed must stay at or below 35 m/s:

Same 35 m/s rim-speed limit, two wheel sizes.
Wheel diameterRadiusω = v/rSame speed in rpm
200 mm0.100 m350 rad/s3,342 rpm
250 mm0.125 m280 rad/s2,674 rpm

The larger wheel has to run slower, by about 670 rpm, to keep the same rim speed. Nothing about the machine changed; only the radius did.

Do not calculate your way to a grinding-wheel speed. The number that governs is the maximum operating speed marked on the wheel, and OSHA requires that the spindle speed of the machine be checked before a wheel is mounted, to be certain it does not exceed that marking (29 CFR 1910.215(d)(1)). The arithmetic above explains why a bigger wheel is marked slower. It does not authorise a wheel, and it is not a substitute for reading the wheel. See Machine Hazards and Housekeeping.

Geometry, Mass, and Force

Scale CAD geometry correctly, connect volume and density to mass, and keep force and weight as separate quantities.

Scale changes area and volume by powers

Scale changes area and volume by powers

If every linear dimension of a model is multiplied by a scale factor s, area changes by s2 and volume changes by s3. A 20% larger model is not 20% larger in area or volume.

Length
Lnew = sLold
Area
Anew = s2Aold
Volume
Vnew = s3Vold

For s = 1.2, area factor = 1.22 = 1.44 and volume factor = 1.23 = 1.728. This is why a modest scale change can matter to material use, shipping mass, tank capacity, and actuator selection.

Never scale an area with a linear factor or a volume with an area factor. The exponent belongs to the geometry, not to the number of decimal places.

Volume is capacity only after you name the unit

Volume is capacity only after you name the unit

CAD mass properties often report volume in a model’s document units. Before treating that number as a tank capacity or a material quantity, state the volume unit and convert it to the form the drawing or specification requires.

Volume relationships used in this unit.

Static solved reservoir example

A SolidWorks model gives a hydraulic reservoir internal volume of 2.5 ft3. Its capacity is 2.5 ft3 × 7.48051948 U.S. gal/ft3 = 18.7013 U.S. gal, or 18.7 U.S. gal. This assumes the reported CAD volume is the usable internal volume and no design headspace has yet been subtracted.

Density connects volume to mass

Density connects volume to mass

Density is mass per volume, and its symbol is ρ (rho): ρ = m/V. Turned around, when material density and CAD volume use compatible units, m = ρV produces mass.

(kg/m3) × (m3) = kg

For example, a volume of 0.13824 m3 made of material with a density of 2,700 kg/m3 has mass m = 2,700 kg/m3 × 0.13824 m3 = 373.248 kg.

Static solved steel example

A steel I-beam has a volume of 0.2 m3 and a density of 7,850 kg/m3. Its mass is m = 7,850 kg/m3 × 0.2 m3 = 1,570 kg. That is the same mass the weight example below starts from — a CAD volume and a material card are usually where a weight calculation actually begins.

Mass does not change merely because an object is moved from one planet to another. Weight can change because gravitational acceleration changes. CAD models, material databases, and shipping specifications commonly need mass; lifting and support calculations commonly need force or weight.

Force and weight are separate quantities

Force and weight are separate quantities

Force is a push or pull. For constant mass, Newton’s second law is Fnet = ma. Weight is one particular force: the force due to gravity, written W = mg.

Fnet
Net force, in newtons (N) or pound force (lbf). It is the combined force that produces acceleration.
W
Weight, a gravitational force. Near Earth’s standard gravity, g0 = 9.80665 m/s2; local gravity can differ slightly.
m
Mass, in kilograms, pound mass, or slugs. It is not measured in newtons or pound force.

In coherent SI, 1 N = 1 kg · m/s2. In the coherent foot–slug–second system, 1 lbf = 1 slug · ft/s2. Do not silently replace lbm with lbf.

Static solved weight example

For a 1,570 kg mass at standard gravity, W = mg = 1,570 kg × 9.80665 m/s2 = 15,396.44 N, or 15.4 kN. This is a weight calculation at standard gravity, not a claim that the mass is 15.4 kN.

Power and Efficiency

Power is work per unit time - three equivalent ways to calculate it, the horsepower conversions, and what an efficiency figure can and cannot be.

Power is how fast work gets done

Power is how fast work gets done

Two hoists that lift the same load to the same height do the same work. The one that does it in half the time needs twice the power.

P = W / t  —  power equals work divided by time.

The two power units and the factors between them.
SystemUnitSymbolDefinition and conversion
SIwattW1 W = 1 J/s; 1 W = 0.001341022 hp
Foot–slug–secondhorsepowerhp1 hp = 550 ft·lbf/s; 1 hp = 745.6999 W

The symbol W does double duty: upright W after a number is the watt, while W standing in a formula for a quantity is usually work. Context separates them, and 1 W = 1 J/s is the sentence where both meanings appear at once.

Worked example: powering the lift

On Work and Energy, lifting 2,000 lbf through 10 ft took 20,000 ft·lbf, or 27.1 kJ. How much power does doing it in 4 s require?

  1. In U.S. units: 20,000 ft·lbf ÷ 4 s = 5,000 ft·lbf/s, and 5,000 ÷ 550 = 9.09 hp.
  2. In SI: 27,116 J ÷ 4 s = 6,779 W, or 6.78 kW.
  3. Cross-check the two: 9.0909 hp × 745.6999 W/hp = 6,779 W. They agree, which is the point of doing both.

That is the power delivered to the load. A motor sized for this job needs more than that, because no drive is perfectly efficient.

P = τω		 (N·m) × (rad/s) = W (lbf·ft × rad/s) ÷ 550 = hp

In the third form the angular velocity must be in rad/s. An rpm figure substituted directly is wrong by a factor of about 9.55. Convert it first — Angular Motion.

The divisor 550 is not a fudge factor. One horsepower is defined as 550 pound-force feet of work per second, so dividing by 550 is simply converting the unit.

Two shortcut formulas, and the conditions attached to them

Two shortcut formulas, and the conditions attached to them

These two save a lot of arithmetic in practice. Both are unit conversions with an assumption hidden inside.

From volts and amps

hp = (volts × amps) ÷ 746

The 746 is exact: NIST defines a separate unit, horsepower (electric), as 746 W. So the formula is really two steps — find the electrical power in watts, then convert watts to horsepower.

The hidden condition is in the first step. Volts times amps gives real power in watts for a direct-current circuit or a purely resistive load. On alternating current it gives apparent power, in volt-amperes; the real power in watts is that multiplied by the power factor, the ratio of real power to apparent power. Use the shortcut on an AC motor without its power factor and the answer comes out high. See Current, Voltage, and Resistance.

From torque and rpm

hp = (torque in lbf·in × speed in rpm) ÷ 63,025

This one is the P = τω row above, with the rpm-to-rad/s conversion and the inch-to-foot conversion already folded into the constant. It only works with the torque in pound-force inches and the speed in rpm; feed it lbf·ft and the answer is twelve times too small.

Supporting detail: Addendum 16 — Where 63,025 comes from.

Efficiency is the fraction that makes it through

Efficiency is the fraction that makes it through

The efficiency of a machine is the ratio of useful output to total input. Its symbol is η (eta), and it can be written with either power or work as long as both sides of the ratio use the same one:

η = Pout / Pin  ·  η = Wout / Win

It is a ratio of two like quantities, so the units cancel: efficiency is a plain number, usually quoted as a percentage.

For any real machine η < 1. You cannot get more power out than you put in, and some of every input always leaves as heat, noise, or friction rather than as useful output. A calculated efficiency above 1 means an error in the numbers, not a remarkable machine.

Worked example: an electric hoist motor

A motor consumes 500 W and applies 1,000 N of force to lift an object at 0.45 m/s. What is the efficiency of the system?

  1. Useful output, using P = Fv: 1,000 N × 0.45 m/s = 450 W.
  2. η = 450 W ÷ 500 W = 0.90, or 90%.

The missing 50 W has not vanished. It has gone into heating the windings, the gearbox, and the bearings.

Efficiency figures are only comparable when the boundary is the same. A motor efficiency, a drive efficiency, and a system efficiency measured from the wall socket to the load are three different numbers for the same machine, and the largest of them is the one a datasheet prefers to print. Say which one you mean.

Engineering Fundamentals/Force and Power

Torque

Turning effort is force times radius - how to calculate it, which unit belongs to it, and why the order of the two words is not decoration.

Torque is force applied at a radius

Torque is force applied at a radius

A force pushes. A torque turns. The same force produces more torque the further it acts from the axis of rotation, which is why a longer wrench loosens a tighter nut with the same pull.

τ = rF  —  torque (τ, tau) equals radius times force.

r is the radius, measured from the axis of rotation to where the force acts. Pulleys, rollers, and wheels are specified by diameter, so halve the number on the drawing before it goes into this formula.

Note: when a turning effort is described about an axis rather than about a shaft, it is often called a moment and given the symbol M. It is the same calculation.

The unit, and the word order that identifies it

The unit, and the word order that identifies it

Torque units and the exact factors between them.
SystemUnitSymbolConversion
SInewton metreN·m1 N·m = 0.7375621 lbf·ft
Foot–slug–secondpound-force footlbf·ft1 lbf·ft = 1.355818 N·m

Case matters, and this one bites. The SI torque unit is N·m with a lower-case m. Written N·M it reads as newton times mega, because M is the prefix for 106 — a million times wrong. The same trap catches Kg for kg and S for s. See Measurement Language.

Torque is lbf·ft; energy is ft·lbf. The same two units, the same numerical factor, written in the opposite order — and NIST prints them that way round on purpose: pound-force foot converts to the newton metre, foot pound-force converts to the joule. They are different physical quantities that happen to share a unit combination, and the order is the only thing on the page telling you which one you are looking at. SI avoids the ambiguity by giving energy its own name, the joule; a torque is never written in joules. See Work and Energy.

Worked example: can the motor hold the arm up?

Worked example: can the motor hold the arm up?

A robot-arm joint motor produces 15 N·m. The arm segment is 0.35 m long and carries a 25 N load at its end. Will the motor hold the arm horizontal?

  1. The load acts straight down, at right angles to a horizontal arm, at the full 0.35 m: τ = 0.35 m × 25 N = 8.75 N·m.
  2. Compare with what the motor produces: 8.75 N·m < 15 N·m.

Yes, with 6.25 N·m to spare — for the load alone. The weight of the arm segment itself also produces a torque about the joint, and no mass was given for it, so this answer is a partial one. In a real selection you would add the arm weight acting at its own centre of mass, then keep margin on top of that.

This is the shape of most torque questions: work out what the load demands, then check it against what the drive can supply, and be explicit about what you have left out.

Worked example: torque from a tangential force

Worked example: torque from a tangential force

A wheel 250 mm in diameter needs 3.5 kN of tangential force to overcome resistance. What torque must the motor supply to the axle?

  1. r = 125 mm = 0.125 m.
  2. 3.5 kN = 3,500 N.
  3. τ = 0.125 m × 3,500 N = 437.5 N·m.

The same formula, rearranged

When the torque is what you know and the force is what you want, F = τ/r. That single rearrangement explains a family of everyday machines: a longer wrench, a bigger steering wheel, and a larger pulley all trade radius against force. Double the radius and the same torque delivers half the force — and, at the same rotational speed, the rim travels twice as far, which is why Power and Efficiency comes out unchanged.

Work and Energy

Force moving through a distance is work - the joule, the foot pound-force, and why an energy unit reads backwards from a torque unit.

Work is a force moving something through a distance

Work is a force moving something through a distance

Holding a heavy box still is exhausting, and in this sense it is zero work. Work needs the force and movement in the direction the force is pushing.

W = Fs  —  work equals force times displacement.

Work is one of the ways energy moves from one place to another, so it is measured in energy units and is often written E when what matters is the energy delivered rather than the act of delivering it.

The two systems, for work and every other form of energy.

Foot–slug–secondfoot pound-forceft·lbf1 ft·lbf = 1.355818 J

Worked example: lifting a load

How much work is required to lift a 2,000 lb load to a height of 10 ft?

  1. The force needed to lift steadily is the load’s weight, 2,000 lbf, and it acts straight up along the 10 ft of travel.
  2. W = 2,000 lbf × 10 ft = 20,000 ft·lbf.
  3. In SI: 20,000 ft·lbf × 1.355818 J per ft·lbf = 27,116 J, or 27.1 kJ.

Two things this calculation assumes, both worth saying out loud: the load is raised at a steady speed, so no extra force goes into accelerating it, and the 2,000 lb figure is a weight in pound-force, not a mass in pound-mass. On Geometry, Mass, and Force those are separate quantities, and here it is the force that belongs in the formula.

Work and torque share a unit - the word order tells them apart

Work and torque share a unit - the word order tells them apart

Multiply a force by a distance and you get work. Multiply a force by a radius and you get torque. Both are a force times a length, so in the foot–pound system both come out as some arrangement of feet and pounds-force.

Torque is written lbf·ft. Energy is written ft·lbf. The numerical factor is identical — 1.355818 of either — so the order of the two words is the only thing distinguishing a turning effort from an amount of energy.

SI removes the ambiguity by naming the energy unit separately. A torque stays N·m; energy becomes the joule. Numerically 1 J = 1 N·m, but a torque is never reported in joules. See Torque.

Engineering Fundamentals/Electrical Quantities

Current, Voltage, and Resistance

The three quantities every schematic and nameplate uses, the units they are measured in, and the two equations that connect them.

Three quantities, three units

Three quantities, three units

Almost every electrical number you will read off a nameplate, a meter, or a schematic is one of three quantities. Each has a name, a quantity symbol used in formulas, and a unit symbol written after a value.

Current, I
The rate at which electric charge flows past a point. Its unit is the ampere, symbol A — one of the seven SI base units. One ampere is one coulomb of charge per second.
Voltage, V
Also called electric potential difference: the difference in electric potential between two points. Its unit is the volt, symbol V, defined as power per unit of current.
Resistance, R
How strongly something opposes the flow of current. Its unit is the ohm, symbol Ω, defined as voltage per unit of current.

Two equations, and everything else follows

Two equations, and everything else follows

The whole set is generated by two relationships:

V = IR  —  P = VI

Rearranging and substituting gives three expressions for each of the four quantities. There is nothing to memorise beyond the two above; the table is a lookup, not a list of new facts.

Worked example: what a lamp draws

Worked example: what a lamp draws

How much current does a 100 W lamp draw on a 120 V supply?

  1. Pick the form with the two quantities you have: I = P/V.
  2. I = 100 W ÷ 120 V = 0.833 A.

Check the units rather than trusting the arithmetic. A volt is a watt per ampere, so watts divided by volts leaves amperes — which is what was asked for.

Worked example: voltage drop along a cord

Worked example: voltage drop along a cord

A 100 ft extension cord has a resistance of 0.1588 Ω. What is the voltage drop across the cord when 10 A flows through it?

  1. V = IR = 10 A × 0.1588 Ω = 1.588 V.

About 1.6 V. That is 1.6 V that never reaches the tool: the supply is divided between the cord and the load, and the cord takes its share first.

    Addendum 16. Where 63,025 comes from

    One horsepower is 550 ft·lbf/s, which is 550 × 12 = 6,600 lbf·in per second. One revolution per minute is 2π/60 rad/s. Putting a torque τ in lbf·in and a speed N in rpm into P = τω gives τN(2π/60) lbf·in per second, and dividing by 6,600 converts that to horsepower:

    hp = τN × (2π/60) ÷ 6,600 = τN ÷ (6,600 × 60 ÷ 2π) = τN ÷ 63,025

    Every constant in the shortcut is a unit conversion. Nothing about the physics is buried in it, which is why the same relationship in coherent SI units needs no constant at all.
