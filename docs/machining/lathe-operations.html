Lathe Operations

What each lathe tool does and cannot do, why most parts start with a facing cut and end with parting, and a simulator to make four parts from bar.

A lathe spins the work and moves the tool on two slides

A lathe spins the work and moves the tool on two slides

In ordinary turning the work rotates while the tool feeds along it or toward its center. This simulator models parts that are round about the spindle’s axis. The shape comes from where the tool is put against the turning work, and in what order.

Chuck
Grips the bar and turns with the spindle. The jaws hold one end; the tool works on the part of the bar that sticks out.
Carriage (Z)
Carries the tool along the bed, parallel to the axis. On this page Z minus is toward the chuck and Z plus toward the tailstock.
Cross slide (X)
Moves the tool straight toward or away from the axis. The simulator’s X readout is the diameter the tool would cut. On a real lathe, find out whether a dial or readout shows diameter or radius before you trust it.
Tailstock
At the far end of the bed, on the centerline. It holds the center drill, the drills and the countersink, which feed straight along the axis.
Z0
Where the drawing’s finished front face belongs. Every length on the drawings is measured from the front face.

How to read the simulator’s picture. It is the lathe seen from the front, with the upper half of the work cut away along its centerline, the way a half-section drawing shows a part. Above the centerline is the cut face: metal is hatched and a hole shows open. Below it is the outside of the part, with its thread and knurl. Tools on the cross slide come in from above, so a tool and the metal it cuts are in the same plane.

The dashed blue outline is the drawing, placed at Z0. Tinted metal is still to come off. A heavy red-orange dashed line marks work that is already smaller than the drawing allows. On each tool, the gold edge is the part that cuts and the small ring is the point the readout follows.

Before using a real lathe, read Machine Hazards and Housekeeping. The chuck key stays in your hand until it is out of the chuck.

Lathe simulator: make the part on the drawing

Lathe simulator: make the part on the drawing

Pick a part and read its drawing. Then make it from bar: choose a tool, start the spindle, and move the tool against the work. Measure the part against the drawing whenever you stop.

  • Move the tool by dragging it in the picture, one slide at a time as if you were turning one handwheel; with the jog buttons, which repeat while held; with the arrow keys once the picture is selected; or by typing a position and pressing Feed to Z or Feed to X.
  • The spindle has to be running for anything to cut, and stopped before you change a tool or measure the part. A caliper or micrometer never goes on turning work.
  • Each tool does only its own kind of work. When a move would ask a tool for something it cannot do, the simulator stops the tool there and says why.
  • Undo takes back one cut at a time. New bar starts the part again, and undo brings the old bar back.
  • Measure the part compares every feature with the drawing. It says what each feature still needs, or whether it has already gone too far to save.
Each tool does one kind of work, and the knurl does not cut at all

Each tool does one kind of work, and the knurl does not cut at all

Facing, turning, drilling, threading and knurling are not the same action with a different shape of tool. Each tool reaches different places and moves in different ways.

Turning tool
Turns a diameter feeding toward the chuck, and faces the end feeding toward the center. Its square left edge leaves a square shoulder; its small nose radius rounds the inside corner.
Chamfer tool
A 90° point. Plunged at an edge, each side cuts a 45° chamfer.
Grooving and parting blade
Cuts only on its front edge. Plunged part of the way it makes a groove; plunged to the center, or into the hole, it parts the work off.
Center drill, drill, countersink
Fed from the tailstock along the axis. The center drill makes an accurate start, the drill makes the hole, and the countersink chamfers its mouth.
Boring bar
Turns the inside of a hole that is already there, the way the turning tool works on the outside. Its bar has to fit in the hole.
Threading tools
Form tools with a 60° point, one for outside threads and one on a bar for inside threads. For a threading pass the carriage is driven by the leadscrew, so the tool moves one thread pitch for every turn of the work.
Knurling tool
Does not cut. Hardened wheels press a diamond pattern into the turning surface. The metal pushed down at the roots rises between the teeth, so the diameter grows.

What makes the simulator stop a move. Each of these is something the real tool cannot do, or should not be asked to:

  • The spindle is stopped, so the tool would only rub.
  • More of the cutting edge would be in the work than the tool takes in one pass. Take it in more passes.
  • A part of the tool that does not cut would touch the work: the holder, the top of an insert, the bar of a boring tool, the side of a blade.
  • The tool would hit the chuck jaws.
  • A threading tool is still in the thread when the carriage moves. Back it out first.
  • A knurl is pressed past full depth, or into a shoulder.
  • A drill is three of its own diameters into a cut without being backed out to clear its chips.

The simulator’s limits are teaching values chosen for these four parts. A real tool’s limits come from its maker and depend on the machine, the material and the setup.

Most parts start with a facing cut and end with parting

Most parts start with a facing cut and end with parting

More than one order can make the same part, and the simulator accepts any order that gets there. Some orders are still better than others, for reasons you can see in the work.

  • Face first. The faced end is the datum: every length on the drawing is measured from it. A sawn end is neither flat nor square to the axis.
  • Rough, then finish. A large step comes off in several passes, and only the last one goes to the size on the drawing.
  • Center drill, drill, then bore. A center-drilled start keeps a drill on center. A boring bar needs a hole its bar fits in, and it brings that hole to size.
  • Heavy work before thin sections. Knurling pushes hard on the work. Knurling before the thin sections next to it are turned keeps the work stiff while it happens.
  • Make room for the threading tool. A thread needs somewhere for the tool to stop: a relief groove, or a stop short of the shoulder. Chamfer the end first so the thread starts on a clean edge.
  • Chamfer the back edge before parting. Once the part is cut off, its back edge is out of reach without a second setup.
  • Part off last. The part falls free, and anything not done to it by then needs another setup.
A thread is cut in several passes along one helix

A thread is cut in several passes along one helix

For a threading pass the carriage is coupled to the leadscrew, so the tool advances exactly one pitch for every turn of the work: 1/16 in for 16 threads per inch. Every pass follows the same helix, so every pass deepens the same groove.

  • A little deeper each pass. The simulator allows 0.015 in of infeed per pass outside and 0.012 in inside. The thread is done when its roots reach the root diameter on the drawing.
  • Back out before you come back. At the end of a pass the tool is still in the groove. Bringing the carriage back without backing the tool out would cut the crests off.
  • Keep the pitch. A pass at another pitch cuts a second helix across the first: a crossed thread.
  • Leave somewhere to stop. The tool runs into a shoulder unless a relief groove gives it room, which is why the thumb screw has one.
How a manual lathe keeps every pass on one helix

The carriage is coupled to the leadscrew by closing the half-nut. On a lathe with an inch leadscrew cutting an inch thread, the thread dial shows when to close it so that each pass drops into the same groove. Many machinists also feed the tool in with the compound rest set near 30°, so that most of the cut is taken by one flank of the tool.

The simulator simplifies all of this. It keeps every pass on one helix for you, feeds the tool straight in, and checks a simplified 60° thread by its crest and root diameters. A real thread is checked against a thread table with thread gauges or measuring wires.

A knurl moves metal instead of cutting it

A knurl moves metal instead of cutting it

The knurling tool makes no chips. Its wheels press a pattern into the turning work, and the metal they push aside has to go somewhere: it rises between their teeth. The diameter over the points ends up larger than the blank was.

Blank
The diameter turned before knurling. On these drawings it is not given: the knurled size is, and you work back to the blank.
Full points
The wheels pressed deep enough that the diamond points are fully formed. Too shallow leaves flat tops; pressing past full depth crushes and flakes the points.
Over the points
The knurled diameter, measured across the crests.

In the simulator the roots sink exactly as far as the crests rise, 0.010 in each at full depth, so a full knurl adds 0.020 in to the diameter. A real knurl’s growth depends on its pitch, the material and how hard it is pressed.

Things to try in the simulator

Things to try in the simulator

  • On the stepped pin, try to turn the body from 1.000 to 0.750 in in one pass. Read why the tool stops, then do it in two.
  • Face the end but stop the cut at X 0.200. Measure: what is left at the center?
  • Face 0.010 in past Z0 and make the rest of the pin to the readout. Measure the lengths. Which ones moved, and why?
  • On the bushing, bring the boring bar to the end before there is a hole. Then drill a 1/4 in hole and try again. Which drill gives the bar room?
  • Drill the bushing without the center drill and read the note under the inspection.
  • On the thumb screw, try a threading pass up to the head before cutting the relief groove.
  • Take one threading pass at 16 per inch, change to 13 and take another. Measure the thread.
  • Knurl the head straight on the 1.000 in bar, without turning a blank first. Measure it against the drawing.
  • On the knurled nut, part the ring off and watch where the blade breaks through.
What this simulator leaves out

What this simulator leaves out

The simulator is about which tool does what, in what order, to what size. It leaves out:

  • Speed and feed. The spindle is on or off. Spindle speed, feed rate and what they do to the cut are on Feeds and Speeds.
  • The cut itself. No tool wear, heat, chatter, deflection or surface finish. A drill cannot wander, so a hole started without a center drill is only noted, not drawn off center.
  • Setups. One setup only: no turning the part around, no second operation, no tailstock center supporting long work.
  • Threads and knurls, simplified. Thread pitch, crests and roots are checked along the required length; pitch diameter and functional thread fit are not verified. The form knurl illustrates displaced material and diameter growth. Its patterned section is schematic, not an exact volume-conservation calculation. Form and cut knurling use different mechanisms.
  • Its numbers. Inch only. The tolerances, the tool limits and the parts are teaching values, not tool-maker data and not a standard.

Tolerances on the drawings mean the same as on Tolerances and Fits: a size on a limit passes.

The starting method for a real hole depends on the drill. For example, Haas distinguishes center drilling from spotting for carbide twist drills; this model does not predict drill wandering.
