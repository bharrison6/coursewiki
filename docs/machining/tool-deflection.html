Tool Stickout and Deflection

A longer tool reaches farther, but bends much more under the same sideways load. Predict a change, test it, and compare the tool with a fixed baseline.

Experiment: how much does a shorter tool help?

Experiment: how much does a shorter tool help?

Predict first: keep the same solid tool, force and material. If its unsupported length drops from 80 to 40 mm, will it bend half as much, one eighth as much, or the same amount?

The length that matters starts at the clamp

The length that matters starts at the clamp

Stickout is unsupported length. Here it is the distance from the rigid clamp face to the point where the sideways force acts. It is not the total tool length, and it is not automatically the flute length.

A force bends the tool and moves its cutting edge away from its unloaded position. The clamp end stays in place and keeps its direction; the bend grows along the unsupported length. In a real cut, force acts over the engaged cutting edges rather than at one perfect point.

Reach is a tradeoff. The tool must reach the feature and clear the work and holder. Within those constraints, reducing unnecessary stickout improves bending stiffness. A larger diameter can also help if the feature has room for it.

Length is cubed; diameter is raised to the fourth power

Length is cubed; diameter is raised to the fourth power

For the ideal solid round tool in this lab, tip bending is proportional to F × L³ ÷ (E × d⁴). Change one input at a time to see which factor is responsible.

½ length → ⅛ bendHalf × half × half. Shortening the unsupported reach is a powerful change.
2× diameter → ¹⁄₁₆ bend2 × 2 × 2 × 2 goes into the denominator. This comparison is for solid round sections.
2× force → 2× bendForce acts linearly. Double E instead, with the other inputs fixed, and bending halves.

Try a combined change: start at L = 40 mm and d = 10 mm, save that baseline, then set L = 80 mm and d = 20 mm. The length contributes 8× and the diameter contributes 1/16×, leaving half the bending at the same load and modulus. The two changes compete.

The calculation and its units

The solid round section has area moment of inertia I = πd⁴/64. With x measured from the clamp, displacement magnitude is y(x) = Fx²(3L − x)/(6EI). At the tip, x = L, so δ = FL³/(3EI). Both displacement and slope are zero at x = 0.

Use N for force, mm for length and diameter, and N/mm² for E. The controls show E in GPa: multiply by 1,000 to get N/mm². The answer is in mm; multiply by 1,000 for µm.

For F = 100 N, L = 40 mm, d = 10 mm and E = 600 GPa, I = 490.874 mm⁴ and δ = 0.007243 mm = 7.243 µm. Halfway along that tool, y(L/2) is 5/16 of the tip value, or 2.264 µm. These are calculated examples, not measured cutter data.

Use the trend; know what this model leaves out

Use the trend; know what this model leaves out

This is a static, linear-elastic, slender-beam model: a uniform solid round tool, a perfectly rigid clamp, a steady sideways point load at the free end and deflections small compared with the length. Elastic modulus E measures stiffness, not hardness or breaking strength.

The lab starts at E = 600 GPa, a chosen teaching value within Hyperion's published 430–690 GPa range for cemented-carbide wear grades. It is not a universal value for carbide cutters. The force is an assumed input, not calculated from a milling operation.

Flutes and necks change stiffness. The holder, spindle and workpiece can move too, and a milling force changes as teeth engage. Static bending alone does not predict chatter thresholds, surface roughness, fracture, safe loads or guaranteed finished-part error.

The lab flags L/d below 4 and bending above 1% of L as prompts to question this simplification. Those are teaching checks, not certification or safety limits.

Primary references
  • MIT OpenCourseWare: review of cantilever beam theory, lecture 2 appendix, equations 10–11 on PDF page 5. Derives the end-loaded shape and tip displacement with fixed-clamp boundary conditions.
  • Hyperion Materials & Technologies: Carbide vs. Steel. Its wear-grade comparison reports the modulus range; it does not specify the stiffness of a particular fluted cutter.
