Surface Texture and Finish

A machined surface has a lay, a waviness and a roughness; a stylus trace and a filter turn them into Ra, Rz and Rt, and a drawing symbol says what is required and how the surface may be made.

A surface has lay, waviness and roughness

A surface has lay, waviness and roughness

Every manufactured surface carries marks from the process that made it. Surface texture describes those marks: which way they run, how far apart they are, and how tall they stand.

Roughness
The finest, closely spaced marks, such as the feed marks a cutting tool leaves at each pass or the scratches of abrasive grit.
Waviness
A longer, gentler undulation that the roughness sits on, for example from vibration or deflection while the part was made.
Lay
The direction of the main surface pattern, set by how the tool or the part moved.
Form
The overall shape, such as a face that is slightly curved. Size and geometric tolerances control form; surface texture is what is left on top of it.
A stylus traces one line, and a filter splits it; Watch out

A stylus traces one line, and a filter splits it

A contact instrument drags a fine stylus along a straight line and records its height. That record is the measured profile. Everything else is worked out from it.

Trace direction
Usually across the lay, so the stylus crosses the marks. If the drawing specifies a measuring direction, that direction governs.
Mean line
A smooth line the filter draws through the measured profile, a weighted average of the heights around each point. This mean line is the waviness.
Cutoff λc
The wavelength where the filter splits the profile. Undulations much longer than λc stay in the mean line; marks much shorter than λc are left over for the roughness. A wave exactly λc long is shared half and half.
Roughness profile
The measured profile minus the mean line.
Evaluation length
The part of the trace the numbers come from. In this lab it is five sections, each λc long. The trace runs one more λc at each end so the filter has heights on both sides of every evaluated point.

The filter decides what counts as roughness, not the size of the undulation. Change λc and the same undulation can move from the waviness into the roughness. That is why a measurement result needs its cutoff, and why a drawing or its standard says which one applies.

Ra, Rz and Rt read the roughness differently

Ra, Rz and Rt read the roughness differently

All three come from the same roughness profile. They answer different questions about it. This lab uses the older ISO 4287 convention with five sections for Rz; the drawing-symbol comparison separately shows older and current graphics.

Ra
The average distance of the roughness profile from its mean line over the evaluation length. Peaks and valleys count alike.
Rz
The average of the sections’ peak-to-valley heights. Each section counts once, whatever is in it.
Rt
The largest peak-to-valley height over the whole evaluation length: the single highest peak to the single deepest valley.

Ra = average of |roughness| over the evaluation length

Rz = (Z1 + Z2 + Z3 + Z4 + Z5) ÷ 5

For the trace above: Ra 1.55 µm; Rz = (6.55 + 6.56 + 6.70 + 6.57 + 6.87) ÷ 5 = 6.65 µm; Rt 7.10 µm.

The values are in micrometres (µm). They can also be given in microinches (µin): one inch is exactly 25.4 mm, so 1 µin is exactly 0.0254 µm, and Ra 1.6 µm is 63.0 µin.

Name the standard. Current documents use the ISO 21920 series. Many existing drawings and instruments use the older ISO 4287 parameters and ISO 1302 symbols. The names Ra, Rz and Rt carried over, but the standards are not identical, so this page says which one each symbol comes from and does not mix them. For a real part, check which standard the drawing invokes.

Same Ra, different surfaces

Same Ra, different surfaces

These three roughness profiles all have Ra 1.6 µm. They are not the same surface. All three plots use the same height scale.

  • Ra alone cannot tell these apart.
  • Rz separates the rounded marks from the other two, but not flat tops from narrow peaks.
  • Where the extremes sit, above or below the mean line, separates those two.

One number does not describe a surface. That is why a drawing names the parameter, not just a value.

Reading a surface texture symbol

Reading a surface texture symbol

The symbol’s point touches the line that represents the surface in a drawing view. Its shape says how the surface may be made; the text beside it says what texture is required.

The three process requirements, in the older and the current graphics. Schematic drawings: the standards dimension the symbols exactly.
SymbolOlder, ISO 1302Current, ISO 21920-1Meaning
BasicAny manufacturing process is permitted. This does not forbid machining; it simply does not require it.
BarMaterial removal is required: the surface must be produced by removing material, for example by machining.
CircleMaterial removal is prohibited: the surface must not be machined or otherwise have material removed to meet this requirement.
Current or older
An ISO 21920-1 symbol carries a short separate dash above the short leg, touching neither leg. The older ISO 1302 graphic has none, so the familiar older marks are not the current symbols unchanged.
The requirement
The parameter and its limit, such as Ra 3.2, sit below the long line, to the right of the long leg, in both.
The lay
In an ISO 21920-1 callout the process and the lay sit above the long line. In the older symbols the lay symbol sits beside the long leg, below the line.

A complete specification can state more than this page draws, such as the measuring direction. The process mark is a requirement about how the surface is made; a profile measurement checks the texture, not whether material was removed.

Lay symbols belong to the drawing view

Lay symbols belong to the drawing view

A lay symbol says which way the marks run. = and ⊥ are judged against the plane of projection of the view the symbol is drawn in, not against the page or the screen.

The first two figures are the same surface. Its marks run front to back: parallel to the right side view’s plane of projection, so = there, and perpendicular to the front view’s, so ⊥ there. The arrow shows which way each view looks.

Lay and trace direction are different things. The lay says how the marks run. The trace direction says how they were measured; it is usually across the lay unless the drawing specifies one.

The lay symbols and meanings here are those of ISO 1302:2002.

Read the callout; Interactive

Read the callout

Ten short questions on the symbols, the lay, the older notations and what the numbers respond to.

  1. The older ISO 1302 symbol with a circle in its V, and Ra 3.2. May this surface be machined to reach Ra 3.2 µm? No: material removal is prohibited. The circle in the V prohibits material removal. Material removal is prohibited: the surface must not be machined or otherwise have material removed to meet this requirement.
  2. A current symbol with an open V, no connecting material-removal crossbar or circle, a separate identifying dash above the short leg, and Ra 6.3. Does this callout forbid machining the surface? No, any process is permitted. The V has no connecting material-removal crossbar or circle: it is the basic symbol. The separate floating dash identifies ISO 21920; it does not require machining. Any manufacturing process is permitted. This does not forbid machining; it simply does not require it.
  3. A symbol with a bar closing its V, a short separate dash above the short leg, and Ra 0.8. Which standard's graphic is this? ISO 21920-1 (current). The short separate dash above the short leg, touching neither leg, marks an ISO 21920-1 symbol. The older ISO 1302 graphic has no dash.
  4. A block of surface whose marks run front to back. An arrow shows the right side view looking at its right side. The marks run front to back. Which lay symbol belongs on this surface in the right side view? =. The right side view looks along the arrow. Marks running front to back are parallel to that view's plane of projection, so the symbol is =. The same surface takes ⊥ in the front view: the symbol depends on the view, not on the screen or page.
  5. A block of surface whose marks run front to back. An arrow shows the front view looking at its front. The marks run front to back. Which lay symbol belongs on this surface in the front view? ⊥. The front view looks along the arrow. Marks running front to back are perpendicular to that view's plane of projection, so the symbol is ⊥. The same surface takes = in the right side view: the symbol depends on the view, not on the screen or page.
  6. The grade number N8. An older drawing gives grade N8. What does that stand for? Ra 3.2 µm. N8 stands for Ra 3.2 µm (126 µin). Each step of the grade number doubles or halves the Ra value, near enough.
  7. Three downward triangles. In the catalog comparison, which Ra values sit beside three triangles? 0.4, 0.8, 1.6 µm. Three triangles sit beside Ra 0.4, 0.8, 1.6 µm. The catalog calls this comparison approximate, not rigorous. Read it as a rough historical guide, not as acceptance limits.
  8. A current symbol with Rz 6.3. Which N grade means the same as this Rz 6.3 µm requirement? None: grades are Ra values. Grade numbers each stand for an Ra value; N9 is Ra 6.3 µm, not Rz 6.3 µm. Ra and Rz measure different things, and how they compare depends on the shape of the profile, as the same-Ra experiment in the lab shows.
  9. One deep scratch crosses this otherwise even trace. Which number has the largest proportional change? Rt. In the lab, a scratch three times the mark height takes Rt from 7.10 to 23.4 µm, Rz from 6.65 to 10.5 µm and Ra from 1.55 to 1.88 µm. Rt spans the highest peak to the deepest valley over the whole length. Rz averages five section heights, diluting localized extremes; filtering can spread a scratch across section boundaries. Ra averages every point.
  10. A block of surface with parallel marks running front to back, and a stylus trace running left to right across them. The trace on this surface crosses the marks. Traced along the marks instead, Ra would be: Much smaller. In the lab, Ra is 1.55 µm across the marks and 0.328 µm along them: the stylus misses the marks when it runs beside them. Tracing across the lay is the usual choice, but a direction the drawing specifies governs.
What this lab leaves out

What this lab leaves out

The lab is a teaching model of one straight trace. A real measurement and a real acceptance decision involve more:

  • The instrument. A real stylus has a tip radius, and instruments apply a short-wavelength filter and remove the part’s form before filtering. The lab’s stylus is sharp and its part is flat.
  • The filter and parameters. This lab follows the older ISO 4287 teaching convention of five sections for Rz, with a Gaussian mean line having 50% transmission at λc. Dense samples resolve the narrow marks; numerical integration on a coarser grid computes the smooth mean line. This is not a compliant instrument or a complete ISO 21920 inspection setup, and a drawing or standard can call for other settings.
  • The decision. The lab compares one trace with one limit. The drawing and its standard say how many traces are taken, where, and how the results are judged.
  • The process. No trace shows whether material was removed. The process requirement is checked against how the part was made.
  • Scratches and pits. Whether an isolated flaw counts against a texture requirement is for the drawing and its standard to say.
  • Symbols. The drawings are schematic. The lay symbols are those of ISO 1302:2002; the lab writes the same symbols in the ISO 21920-1 lay position. Check the current standard before relying on one there. Inch-drawing (ASME) practice is not covered here.
