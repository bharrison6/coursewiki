Saw Blade Selection

Use section shape, teeth in the cut, tooth form, set, and blade material to narrow the choice before consulting the maker's chart.

Pitch spaces the teeth; gullets carry the chips

Pitch spaces the teeth; gullets carry the chips

TPI means teeth per inch along the blade. A higher TPI is a finer pitch. For evenly spaced teeth, pitch in inches is 1 ÷ TPI. The gullet is the hollow between teeth that provides chip space. Set is the sideways displacement of teeth, making clearance for the blade body in the kerf.

Estimate teeth in the cut before choosing TPI; Interactive; Practice

Estimate teeth in the cut before choosing TPI

As a rule of thumb, aim to keep at least about three teeth engaged. For a uniform pitch crossing one continuous thickness of material:

Estimated teeth engaged = contact length in inches × TPI
Minimum TPI for three teeth = 3 ÷ contact length in inches

Use thickness measured along the tooth line through solid material, not automatically the height of the part. For 0.25 in of continuous contact at 12 TPI, the estimate is 0.25 × 12 = 3 teeth. At the same thickness, the three-tooth calculation gives 3 ÷ 0.25 = 12 TPI.

A calculated minimum is a screening value, not a blade specification. If it falls between available pitches, select a suitable finer option from the maker's chart rather than rounding down. Excessively fine teeth can run out of chip space in thick stock.

Why the answer can be a fraction of a tooth

The product counts tooth spacings across the contact length. Actual engaged tooth tips are discrete, and the number changes as the blade moves. Entry, exit, variable pitch, and curved sections also change contact. This estimate explains the selection principle; it does not guarantee a fixed count throughout a real cut.

Predict first: at mid-depth in the thin-wall tube below, would doubling TPI halve, double, or leave the estimated engagement unchanged? It doubles: the same contact length contains twice as many tooth spacings.

Dashed line = about three teeth; shading = below three. Each plot uses its own vertical scale.

Estimated teeth = solid contact length ÷ tooth spacing; mm per tooth = 25.4 ÷ TPI. Readouts round to two decimal places; calculations keep the unrounded, real-valued estimate. Highlighted tooth tips illustrate one blade position, not a guaranteed integer count.

Round-section minima include the exact entry and exit tangencies, where contact is zero. A flat bar has constant contact through its feed height, including the limiting faces. Rotate the flat bar 90° to swap contact thickness and feed height; rotating a round bar or round tube about its axis changes neither. This model is a straight tooth line crossing an ideal section, with uniform pitch and no blade tilt.

The thick-bar, fine-blade preset highlights the full contact region as a reminder: gullets may fill with chips; check the blade maker's guidance. It sets no numeric upper limit. Tooth count alone cannot predict chip filling. For shop practice and hazards, see Cutoff Sawing and Safety collection.

A tube's outside diameter is not its solid thickness; Practice

A tube's outside diameter is not its solid thickness

A solid bar offers continuous material across the blade's path. A tube, angle, or channel presents walls and gaps, and the contact changes as the cut progresses. Select from a chart for tubes or structural sections, using wall thickness, overall dimensions, and orientation as that chart requires.

Thin walls generally need finer teeth than a solid bar of the same outside size. Do not multiply tube outside diameter by TPI and count the empty center as cutting engagement. Nor does simply adding two wall thicknesses describe every stage of a round tube cut.

Variable pitch alternates tooth spacing within a repeating group. It can reduce repeated tooth-impact vibration and suit changing engagement, but it still needs to match the material and section.

Tooth form and set describe different features

Tooth form and set describe different features

Tooth formSelection idea
Regular or standardCommon general-purpose form, typically with a zero rake face. Fine versions suit thinner sections.
SkipWidely spaced teeth with generous gullets, typically zero rake; useful when softer materials and larger chips need clearance.
HookA positive rake face takes a more aggressive bite. Use where the maker recommends it for the material and the machine can support the cut.

Rake describes the angle of the tooth face that meets the material. Exact angles vary by blade; “hook” does not specify one universal angle.

Set patternArrangement and use
RakerA common sequence is left, right, straight. A general-purpose arrangement; the straight tooth is unset.
AlternateSuccessive teeth are set left and right.
WavyGroups of teeth vary their sideways set in a wave. Common on fine-pitch blades for thin sections.

Set pattern does not replace pitch selection. Choose the complete blade specification, not a tooth-form name alone.

Choose blade material and dimensions for the machine

Choose blade material and dimensions for the machine

  • Carbon steel: an economical choice for suitable easy-to-machine materials and lighter service.
  • Bimetal: high-speed-steel cutting teeth joined to a flexible alloy-steel back; widely used for metal cutoff work.
  • Carbide-tipped: carbide cutting tips for applications needing greater wear resistance, with a compatible machine and cutting conditions.

Match blade length and thickness to the machine. Blade width, measured from tooth tip to back, also affects contouring; see Vertical Band Machining. Material name alone does not establish a blade's hardness limit or speed. Use the maker's product chart for the actual alloy and condition.

Manufacturer resources for the next selection step

Consult the chart supplied with the blade. Public starting points include Starrett's carbon blade range and DoALL's blade FAQ. Product-specific recommendations govern; the estimates here are teaching calculations.
