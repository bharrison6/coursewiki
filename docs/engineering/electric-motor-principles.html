Electric Motor Principles

Find the stator and rotor, then follow the changing magnetic field that keeps a motor turning.

The stator stays still; the rotor turns

The stator stays still; the rotor turns

An electric motor turns electrical input into mechanical motion. In a rotary motor, the stator is the stationary part and the rotor is the rotating part. An air gap separates them. Bearings support the rotating assembly; the shaft carries motion to the load.

The stator is often outside the rotor, but it does not have to be. The names describe motion, not inside versus outside. See Electric Motor Types for both arrangements.

Keep the magnetic target moving: carrot on a stick

Keep the magnetic target moving: carrot on a stick

Picture the rotor turning to align its magnetic field with the stator's field. If the fields simply settled into alignment, that turning effect would disappear. To keep turning, the motor switches a field or keeps the stator field rotating. Think carrot on a stick: keep moving the target ahead.

Commutation means switching current between windings at the right time. Brushes and a commutator can do it mechanically, or a drive can do it electronically. A winding is a coil of wire that creates a magnetic field when current flows through it.

Where the alignment picture needs care

This is a first picture, not a description of every motor's internal timing. A synchronous motor can rotate at the same speed as the stator field while keeping an angular offset that produces torque. An induction rotor runs slower than the field during normal motoring; that relative motion induces rotor current. A stepper can deliberately stop and hold a commanded position.

Why a DC motor keeps turning: coil, field and commutator; Interactive

Why a DC motor keeps turning: coil, field and commutator

The simplest brushed DC motor is one rectangular coil on a shaft between two magnet poles. Turn the coil, find where the field stops pushing it, and see how a split-ring commutator keeps the push going one way.

Predict first: a coil carrying current sits between a north and a south pole. At which angles does the field give it no turning push at all, and where is the push largest?

Torque on the coil. For an ideal rectangular coil in a uniform field, the torque about the shaft is τ = N·B·I·A·sin θ. N is the number of turns, B the field (T), I the current (A) and A the coil's area (m²), so τ is in N·m. θ is the angle from the field B to the coil's normal n, the line perpendicular to the coil's face. The coil's magnetic moment lies along n or against it, depending on which way the current circulates. That sets the direction of the torque, not its size.

Why the angle matters. Whenever current flows, each long side of the coil feels a force F = BIL, the same size at every angle. What changes is its lever arm about the shaft, r·sin θ, where r is half the coil's width. As the coil approaches θ = 0° or 180°, with its face across the field in the neutral plane, the forces point almost straight through the shaft. The lever arm shrinks to zero, and so does the torque. With slip rings the current still flows at those angles and the forces point straight through the shaft. With an ideal split ring the brushes sit on the gaps at that instant, so there is momentarily no current and no force at all. Either way the torque there is zero. At θ = 90° and 270° the lever arm, and the torque, are largest.

Why a commutator. If the coil were fed through slip rings, its current would never reverse. With the polarity drawn here, past 180° the torque would reverse too, pushing the coil back toward that neutral-plane position, and over a whole turn it would average zero. Reverse the supply or the magnets and the coil's resting position moves half a turn. A split-ring commutator turns with the coil. Its brushes change segments exactly as the coil crosses the neutral plane, which reverses the coil current. The torque becomes N·B·I·A·|sin θ|: it never points backward, but it still falls to zero twice a turn.

Two coils. Add a second coil 90° ahead of the first, each with its own split ring. When one coil is in the neutral plane, the other is at its peak. The total, |sin θ| + |cos θ| in units of one coil's peak, runs from 1 to √2. Its smallest value is 1/√2 ≈ 0.71 of its largest, so the ripple shrinks and the zero-torque dead spot disappears.

Worked example, with illustrative numbers rather than a real motor rating: N = 50 turns, B = 0.20 T, I = 2.0 A, and a coil 40 mm × 30 mm, so A = 1.2 × 10⁻³ m². The peak torque is N·B·I·A = 50 × 0.20 × 2.0 × 0.0012 = 0.024 N·m. At θ = 30°, τ = 0.024 × sin 30° = 0.012 N·m.

Direction. Reversing the current reverses every force (F = I L × B), so the motor turns the other way. Swapping the magnets reverses B and does the same. Reversing both restores the original direction.

Model limits. This is an ideal teaching model: one or two rectangular coils, a uniform field, and current switched exactly at the neutral plane. Torque is shown as a fraction of one coil's peak, N·B·I·A. It does not model back EMF, speed, magnetic saturation or brush sparking.

For how a DC motor's speed falls as its load torque rises, see DC Motor Torque, Speed, and Power.

Sources for the torque formula and the commutator

OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, section 11.5, Force and Torque on a Current Loop, derives the torque on a rectangular loop in a uniform field (equations 11.19 to 11.22). It defines the magnetic moment of an N-turn loop as N·I·A along the loop's normal and describes brushes and a commutator reversing the current. HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), Commutator and Brushes on DC Motor, describes a split-ring device called a commutator that reverses the current as the coil passes the plane perpendicular to the field. Its Torque on a Current Loop page gives the same torque in terms of the magnetic moment.

Separate turning effort, speed, and power

Separate turning effort, speed, and power

Torque is turning effort. Angular speed tells how fast the shaft turns. Mechanical power combines the two. A stopped shaft can have torque and still deliver zero mechanical power.

Start with Current, Voltage, and Resistance for the electrical quantities, then use DC Motor Torque, Speed, and Power to see what happens when a load asks a motor for more torque.
