Gear Ratios

Change the teeth, follow the turns, and trade speed for turning effort. Compare a gear pair, an idler and a compound train.

Experiment: trade speed for turning effort

Experiment: trade speed for turning effort

Predict first: a 20-tooth gear turns at 120 rpm clockwise and drives a 40-tooth gear. Will the larger gear turn faster or slower? What changes if you add an idler between them?

All three examples use the same ideal input: 120 rpm and 2 N·m. Input power is 25.13 W, and ideal output power is also 25.13 W. More output torque comes with less speed.

Count the teeth to find the speed

Count the teeth to find the speed

The driver supplies motion; the driven gear receives it. For one external gear mesh, each tooth passing on the driver advances the driven gear by one tooth space. A gear with twice as many teeth needs twice as many passing teeth to complete a turn.

Output speed = input speed × driver teeth ÷ driven teeth. A 20-tooth driver at 120 rpm driving 40 teeth gives 120 × 20 ÷ 40 = 60 rpm. External meshing reverses the direction.

This lesson uses reduction ratio i = input speed ÷ output speed, with speed magnitudes. A 2:1 reduction means two input turns for one output turn. Ratios above 1 reduce speed; ratios below 1 increase it. At zero speed, use tooth counts to find the ratio; do not divide 0 by 0.

Why tooth counts work, and what the dashed circles mean

The gears here share one tooth size, called module, and a 20° pressure angle. Their pitch diameters are proportional to tooth count. The dashed pitch circles roll together at equal tangential speed, so nA × NA = −nB × NB when clockwise is positive on both shafts. Here n is signed rotational speed and N is tooth count.

Tooth flanks are involute curves. Actual tooth surfaces can slide during contact even though the pitch circles roll. The drawing samples those curves, simplifies the tooth roots and adds a small visual clearance. It is a motion model, not a manufacturing drawing.

An idler and a compound shaft do different jobs

An idler and a compound shaft do different jobs

One mesh reverses

A → B turns the output opposite to the input, viewed from the same end of each shaft.

An idler cancels

A → I → B reverses twice. I affects its own speed and the spacing, but its tooth count cancels from the end-to-end ratio.

Compound stages multiply

A → B, then C → D, with B and C fixed together: i = (NB/NA) × (ND/NC).

Same shaft means the same angular speed. A large B and a small C fixed to one shaft make the same number of turns in the same direction. Their rim speeds differ because their radii differ. They do not mesh with each other.

In the compound example, 20 → 40 teeth halves the speed. A second 20 → 40 stage halves it again: 120 → 60 → 30 rpm. The two external meshes make the final output turn in the same direction as the input.

More torque costs speed; it does not create power

More torque costs speed; it does not create power

Torque is turning effort. In this ideal, lossless train at steady load, output torque magnitude = input torque magnitude × reduction ratio. A 2:1 reduction changes 120 rpm and 2 N·m into 60 rpm and 4 N·m. The 4:1 compound example gives 30 rpm and 8 N·m.

Rotational power is torque × angular speed: P = T × 2πn/60 when T is in N·m and n is in rpm. Both sides of either example carry 25.13 W ideally. Real friction and other losses leave less output power. Tooth strength, bearings, shafts, lubrication and the motor all limit usable torque.

At zero speed, mechanical power is zero. A loaded, stationary gear train can still carry torque. The lab shows ideal static torque balance in that case; it does not model motor stall behavior or promise a safe load.

Primary references and model boundaries
  • KHK: Idler gear. The intermediate tooth count cancels from the overall speed ratio.
  • KHK: Calculation of Gear Dimensions, section 4.1 and Table 4.1. Standard spur-gear pitch, base, tip and root dimensions and center spacing.
  • MIT OpenCourseWare: Gear Trains. Gear bodies, shared shafts, and force and torque balance.

The lab assumes rigid external spur gears, fixed shaft centers, matched tooth size, steady transmitted load and no power loss. Acceleration, elasticity, backlash dynamics, friction, noise and tooth strength are omitted. Changing tooth counts moves the centers to maintain the correct mesh; it does not represent swapping a gear into a fixed gearbox housing.
