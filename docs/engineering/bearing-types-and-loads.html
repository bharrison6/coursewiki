Bearing Types and Loads

Choose the motion to allow, identify the load, then compare plain and rolling-element bearings.

A bearing allows useful motion and restrains unwanted motion

A bearing allows useful motion and restrains unwanted motion

A loose rigid part can move in six independent ways: translate along three axes and rotate about those three axes. These are its degrees of freedom. A bearing transfers load to a support while allowing the motion the machine needs, with low friction.

For a rotating shaft, the goal is usually one remaining freedom: rotation about its own axis. Two spaced radial supports resist sideways movement and tilting; axial location stops the shaft walking lengthwise. A single short bearing does not automatically provide that whole constraint system.

Bearings guide motion; motors and drives supply it. Belt, chain and gear drives load their supporting shafts. See Torque for turning effort and Shaft Support and Axial Adjustment for the complete support arrangement.

Radial load crosses the shaft; thrust acts along it

Radial load crosses the shaft; thrust acts along it

Radial load acts perpendicular to the shaft axis. Axial load, or thrust, acts parallel to it. Combined load has both components. A bearing's orientation and internal geometry determine which it can carry.

First selection filter, not a load rating. Check the exact catalog design.
TypeRadial loadAxial loadCombined load

Do not turn “roller” into “radial only,” or “thrust” into a rule for every product bearing that name. Tapered rollers carry combined loads; some spherical roller thrust designs also accept radial load under specified conditions.

Plain and rolling bearings trade friction, upkeep and cost

Plain and rolling bearings trade friction, upkeep and cost

A plain bearing supports sliding surfaces, often with a replaceable sleeve called a bushing. A rolling-element bearing places balls or rollers between rings. “Anti-friction” is a traditional name, not a promise of zero friction.

Compare the operating conditions as well as the purchase price.
QuestionPlain bearingRolling-element bearing
Starting versus running resistance?Breakaway friction can exceed running friction. A hydrodynamic film is not fully established at rest.Often low starting resistance and a smaller start/run difference; seals, grease and preload still matter.
Lubrication and maintenance?Dry grades may need no added lubricant; oil-fed designs require the specified supply and cleanliness.Usually grease or oil lubricated. A sealed, prelubricated bearing still has a finite service life.
Speed?Dry bushings have material-specific speed and pressure limits. Fluid-film bearings can serve high-speed machinery.Many suit moderate to high speeds; type, size, lubrication and heat limit speed.
Load and cost?Simple bushings often cost little initially. Capacity depends on size, material, speed and heat removal.Precision rings and elements add cost. Roller designs often carry more load than ball designs of similar overall size.

Rolling contact generally reduces resistance compared with sliding contact. It does not guarantee higher efficiency than every fluid-film bearing, nor does a cheap bushing guarantee lower lifetime cost. Include lubrication equipment, replacement, downtime and power loss. Bearing losses contribute to Power and Efficiency and the stage efficiencies discussed in Gear Ratios.

Plain bearings differ in both lubrication and load direction

Plain bearings differ in both lubrication and load direction

Dry or self-lubricating
A suitable polymer, composite or lubricant-containing material supplies a low-friction sliding surface. Follow that material's instructions; adding grease is not automatically an improvement.
Hydrodynamic
Relative motion draws lubricant into a converging gap, forming a pressure-supported film. Starts and stops require attention because the running film depends on motion.
Hydrostatic
An external pressure supply supports the load on a fluid film, including at rest while the supply operates. The pump, flow control and loss-of-pressure behavior are part of the system.

Those are lubrication descriptions. Shape answers a different question: a journal surrounds a shaft to carry radial load; a footstep supports axial load at a shaft end; a thrust collar presents an annular face to carry axial load. An assembly can combine a journal and a collar to handle both components.

Rings carry the load; the cage spaces the rolling elements

Rings carry the load; the cage spaces the rolling elements

The inner ring fits around the shaft; the outer ring fits in the housing. The rolling elements transfer load between their raceways. The cage maintains spacing and guides the elements.

In a common arrangement the inner ring turns with the shaft while the outer ring stays still. The balls spin about their own axes and orbit around the shaft. The rings do not have to rotate in opposite directions.

Balls have idealized point contact; rollers have idealized line contact. Under load these become finite contact patches. The longer roller contact generally supports more load in a similar overall size. Contact angle, not just element shape, determines thrust capability. Thrust ball and roller bearings place their contact geometry to support axial force.

Manufacturer reading for bearing selection

Use the exact product's current catalog to check its load, speed and lubrication limits. These references introduce the design choices.

  • NSK: Introduction to Bearings identifies rings, elements and cage.
  • NSK: Roller Bearings compares line and point contact.
  • Kingsbury: Hydrodynamic Bearings describes oil-film support and high-speed applications.
  • igus: self-lubricating plain bearings describes embedded solid lubricants.
  • SKF: Rolling Bearings, bearing type and arrangement, for product-specific selection.

Practice identifying the load

Double a ball bearing's load and its basic rating life falls to one eighth

Double a ball bearing's load and its basic rating life falls to one eighth

L10 is the life that 90% of a large group of identical bearings are expected to reach or exceed. It is neither a guaranteed life nor an average. Change one input below and watch the life curve.

Example bearing: C = 14.0 kN, given for this exercise. A real bearing's C comes from its manufacturer's catalog; this example does not represent a catalog part.

Basic rating life: L10 = (C/P)p in millions of revolutions, with p = 3 for balls and p = 10/3 for rollers. C is the basic dynamic load rating; P is the equivalent dynamic load, taken as given here (for pure radial loading in this exercise, P = Fr). Use the same force units for C and P.

At constant speed n in r/min, L10h = L10 × 10⁶/(60 × n) hours. The years conversion assumes a duty schedule you can change; it does not predict calendar aging.

Static worked example (also used for printing).

Example ball bearing: C = 14 kN (given), P = 2 kN, n = 1500 r/min
C/P7
L10 (million revolutions)343
L10h (hours)3811
Years at assumed duty1.906

Duty assumption: 8 h/day × 250 days/year. Years = hours ÷ annual operating hours.

C/P = 14/2 = 7; L10 = 7³ = 343 million revolutions. L10h = 343 × 10⁶/(60 × 1500) = 3811 h, or 1.906 years at 2000 h/year. At P = 4 kN, L10 = 42.875 million revolutions and L10h = 476.4 h.

Adjusted life factors (a1, aISO), contamination, lubrication and combined-load X/Y factors are beyond this page.
