Who is asking?

What changes in the answer when it knows who you are?

Before you start. Demo 2 showed that a language model predicts the next words from its context — the text sitting in front of it. Here you build that context yourself, one block at a time. Lever 1 adds the context block: who is asking, and what they are working with.

Predict

Adding context changes one dimension of the answer. Which one?

Try

Leave all three levers off and press Ask. Then turn on 1 — CONTEXT and press Ask again.

Takeaway

With no context the answer is aimed at everybody, which is another way of saying nobody. Context changes who it is for — length and vocabulary follow the card dealt, not this lever.

Demo 5 of 17 · Part three · Stage 2

What does finished look like?

If you never say what a good answer looks like, who decides instead?

Before you start. Every answer has a shape — a length, an audience, a format. Somebody decides it, always. A success specification is the block where you decide it yourself. Leave it out and the decision still gets made, just not by you.

Predict

Lever 2 is off, so the target is unspecified. What happens to it?

Try

With lever 2 off, press Ask and watch the card it deals. Then turn on 2 — SUCCESS SPEC and ask again.

Takeaway

An unspecified target does not stay unspecified — something else fills it in, and does not tell you. Saying what finished looks like takes that decision back.

In whose words?

What do you actually get for asking in the vocabulary of the field?

Before you start. Lever 3 adds expert keywords — the vocabulary to answer in. Under every answer is a counter: how many specialist words the answer used, and how many of those you never typed. Watch both numbers, and then ask what they are worth.

Predict

With keywords off you see at most four expert terms. With keywords on, how many?

Try

Turn on 3 — EXPERT KEYWORDS, press Ask, then press Show me which ones.

Takeaway

Asking in a field's vocabulary gets that vocabulary back — words nobody in the room supplied. That is real. It is also not the same thing as a better answer, and telling those apart is the skill.

Guidehow to use this demo

A student asks a question. You decide how much you tell it before it answers. That is the whole demo.

Where this sits. Demo 5 of 17. Demo 2 showed that a model reads its context; here you control that context, one lever at a time.

  1. Context. Pick a question, leave every lever off, press Ask. Then turn on 1 — CONTEXT and ask again.
  2. Success spec. With lever 2 off, press Ask and watch the card it deals itself. Turn on 2 — SUCCESS SPEC and the deck is struck out.
  3. Expert keywords. Turn on 3 — EXPERT KEYWORDS, ask, then press Show me which ones and count the words nobody typed.

Every answer here was written by hand before the file was saved. Nothing is generated live and nothing touches the internet.

Scriptedall 64, written before the file was saved

Every answer is a hand-written fixture looked up from a table. There is no model in this file, no API key and no request of any kind — the build refuses to produce the page if it contains one. The typing animation is decoration at a fixed rate; it is not a model's speed.

How it works

answer = table[ question + which levers are on ]

Four questions × eight lever states, plus three alternate target-deck answers for each of the four states where the success spec is off: 64 authored answers in total.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • These answers were written to make the levers legible. Each single-lever answer was authored to improve in that lever's dimension and nothing else. A real model is messier: it may improve two things at once, or nothing, or get worse. This shows what a better prompt tends to buy you — it is not a measurement of what any model did.
  • The bare answers are bland, not broken — and we wrote them that way so the room cannot dismiss the demo as rigged. Bare prompts in the wild also produce confident errors and refusals.
  • A bare prompt is not information-free. The wording, register and topic of "help me get ready for my test" already carry a lot about the asker.
  • Four questions, one kind of asker. All four are school or first-job tasks for a US high-school or early-college room. Levers behave differently on tasks with a single correct answer.
  • Three levers are not the whole of prompting. Worked examples, step-by-step reasoning, output formats, tool access and simply iterating on a reply are all absent, and all matter.

Sources

  • Nothing on this stage is a published figure, so there is nothing to cite. The counts above were measured from this file's own content by its build gates — see stage 3.
Boundary: this demo does not claim to show what any particular AI system does. It shows what changes when a request carries more of what the asker knows — using answers a person wrote to make that change visible.

Live the card draw

With lever 2 off, the target card is chosen at random from three, in front of you, each time you ask.

Scripteda teaching device, not a mechanism

A real model does not deal from a deck of three. When you do not say what a finished answer should look like, it falls back on habits learned in training: more stable than a card draw, not confined to three options, and varying continuously rather than across three named archetypes. The deck makes a real and normally invisible thing visible. It is not what the machine does.

How it works

card = random of three  →  answer written to that card

Each question carries its own three cards, drawn from a printed deck of eight. In presentation mode a picker fixes the draw so a beat can be rehearsed; otherwise it is always random.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The deck is the simplification, and it is the biggest one in the demo. Three discrete named cards stand in for a continuous distribution of defaults. Keep the insight — an unstated target gets filled in by something else — and drop the dice.
  • The card is shown to you. A real system does not announce the target it assumed. That courtesy is the demo's, not the machine's.
  • Showing you the card is itself a teaching choice that makes the effect look tidier than it is.

Sources

  • No published figure is used on this stage. The claim about where real defaults come from is stated as the authors' reading, not as a citation — which is why it is not dressed as one.
Boundary: the deck is a prop for an argument, not a model of an algorithm. This demo does not claim that any system selects among a fixed menu of answer shapes.

What this page actually computes

Scriptedthe count is an author's tag list, not a detector

The big number under an answer counts the specialist terms the author tagged in that answer. Nothing on this page detects specialist vocabulary; it reads a list.

Live "words you never typed"

The second number is computed, now, against the prompt you assembled: each tagged term is looked for in your own text. It matches plain text, so a term that appears in your prompt only in another form — "mitosis" in the prompt against "mitotic" as a term — still counts as new.

Measuredthis file's own content, by its build gates

Across all 64 answers, keyword-off states carry 0 to 4 tagged terms and keyword-on states carry 15 to 20. Those are measured ranges, enforced by the content gates, not estimates.

How it works

novel = term not found in your assembled prompt

Terms are matched on word boundaries and only the first occurrence of each is marked, which is what makes the final count equal the authored list exactly.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • More expert words is not the same as a better answer. The counter measures vocabulary, and vocabulary is the easiest thing to mistake for understanding. The demo's own walkthrough asks you to check whether the answer got more useful or just more impressive; that question is the point of this stage.
  • The 1–10 curve measures this room, not AI. It plots the scores you give against how many levers were pulled. The answers were written to get better as levers are pulled, so a rising line means the room agrees with the author. It is a good way to make a room commit out loud; it is not evidence about models.
  • The lever colours include one non-brand hue. Lever 3's violet is not part of the Murray State palette. It is kept deliberately: the three lever colours are a code printed on the physical cut-out target cards a presenter hands round the room, so the screen and the paper have to agree. It is the only sanctioned exception in this demo.
  • Some bad answers are not a prompting problem at all. A lever can fix an answer that is vague, mis-aimed or the wrong shape. It cannot fix one that is confidently wrong, out of date, refusing, or past what the system can do. Those need checking, not a better prompt.
