Guidehow to use this demo

Has this happened before? AI has contracted twice. The histories rhyme, but neither is a script for the next one.

Where this sits: Demo 3 measured a benchmark under stated conditions; this demo asks you to read a historical claim the same way — as a forecast with a deadline, or an assessment of a moment — before judging it.

  1. Claims. Guess the year and verdict for ten sourced claims, then press Lock it in to reveal the answer and its source.
  2. History. Drag the timeline slider or step with ‘ and ’ to inspect landmark results and five comparison lenses across 1950–2026.
  3. Compare. Choose a lens, then open each era's Details for the full explanation and sources.
  4. Today. Expand a similarity and a difference, then decide what evidence supports your own analogy.

Getting around

The four tabs switch stages. Details opens the assumptions and sources for the current stage. Guide reopens this card; Settings holds Open Presenter Notes, Presentation mode, Reset, and Run the self-test.

Time

Fourteen to nineteen minutes for all four stages; six minutes for the short version described in Presenter Notes.

Settings

Presenter notes

The printable session plan, on screen: what to say at each stage, which predictions to keep if you are short of time, the questions you will get, and a six-minute cut. This is the printable guide itself, not a summary of it.

Presentation mode

Scales the whole page up one notch for projecting to a room, and puts a Notes button in the header.

Reset

Back to the start: every answer cleared, stage 1, the timeline rewound to the full span, every opened card closed, the self-test output cleared. The page does not reload. Presentation mode stays as you set it.

Proof

Checks that every claim on screen resolves to a declared source, that no source is cited and never used, that every source URL points at a specific page rather than a bare host, that the timeline runs forward in time, that each winter contains at least one funding withdrawal, that the counts quoted on the scorecard match the cards actually in the deck, and that the scoring refuses to mark an unresolved prediction. Results appear here and in the browser console.

Live your miss distance and verdict correctness

The moment you press Lock it in, this page computes how many years your guess missed by and whether your verdict matches what happened, from the year and verdict you chose. Nothing is looked up for that comparison.

Sourced the ten claims themselves

Each quotation, its speaker, date and outcome are drawn from a named, checkable source and marked primary (checked against the document itself) or reported (a specific secondary source that names its own underlying scholarship) — for example Turing's 1950 paper (primary) versus Minsky's 1967 line, routed here through Quote Investigator's page references (reported).

How it works

A claim's kind (promise, assessment, or warning) decides whether it can be scored at all: only a time-bounded promise or warning receives an outcome verdict. An assessment or untimed ambition is classified, not graded against a later result, because it never named a deadline to miss.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The kind (promise / assessment / warning) is this activity's own classification, made from what each quotation actually says, not a category the original speaker used.
  • This is a curated teaching deck of ten claims, not a representative accuracy sample of optimists, pessimists, or the field.
  • Two forecasts are left deliberately open (unresolved as of this build) and are never scored as failures.

How it works

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Winter date ranges are conventional and soft-edged, not a single dated event; the supporting notes below the timeline name exactly where historians disagree.

Learning goals

Not stated objectives — what the activity itself demonstrably shows, one per stage:

  1. A forecast needs an outcome and a deadline; an assessment needs evidence about its own moment — grading one as the other makes history look more certain than it is.
  2. The five lanes organize evidence for comparison; they do not prove that every AI contraction follows one fixed order.
  3. An open cell is evidence only that a limit or a withdrawal has not (yet) been established — not a prediction that one is coming.
  4. A historical rhyme is a reason to ask a question, not a reason to expect the same ending — each claim about today needs its own evidence.

Where this sits on the path

Demo 3 leaves the room reading a benchmark result under its own stated conditions rather than extrapolating from two points. Fast progress like that is exactly what raises the question this demo answers: is this a bubble? History answers with evidence, not a script — and this demo hands forward the habit of classifying a claim, forecast or assessment, before judging it.

One question for the session

Has this happened before? AI has contracted twice, through different combinations of technical limits, institutional choices and markets. The histories rhyme; neither is a script for the present.

The one sentence to land: a prediction needs a deadline and an outcome; an assessment needs evidence about its own moment. Do not grade a historical claim until you know which kind it is.

Stage 1 — Claims

Question. Is this a forecast you can grade, or an assessment you can only judge by its own moment?

Predict prompt. Guess the year, then say whether the claim is a scoreable forecast or an assessment to classify — before Lock it in reveals anything.

Activity. Do the first two cards yourself, out loud, so the room sees the mechanic. Then hand it over — ask for a show of hands on the year before you drag the slider, and a verbal verdict or classification before you press Lock it in. For the four context cards, choose Assessment or untimed ambition when the claim supplies no scoreable future window. The room being wrong together is the experience; a silent room reading a screen is not.

Expected observation. Three of the five time-bounded promises missed outright, one arrived thirty years late, and the deliberately unscored ones (the 1958 press claim, ALPAC, Perceptrons, Lighthill, and Amodei 2024) pull the room toward wanting to grade them anyway — that pull is the misconception to name.

Check-yourself answer key. "Should an open forecast be marked wrong?" — No: an unresolved forecast stays unscored rather than counted as a failure.

Teaching limit. This is a curated ten-claim deck, not a representative accuracy sample of optimists, pessimists, or the field.

Stage 2 — History

Question. Do the two AI winters share one fixed cause-and-effect sequence, or different pathways that happen to rhyme?

Predict prompt. Scrubbing from 1950, does an institutional bet or a named constraint fire first in each winter?

Activity. Drag the slider slowly, or step with ‘ and ’ to stop and read one event. Tap any dot for its full text and source. Use the rows as comparison lenses — claims, institutional bets, constraints, reassessment, withdrawal — and ask which causes matter in each episode and which do not carry across. Read the current entries as evidence about today, not a third historical run.

Expected observation. The order is not identical between the two winters: the first winter's reassessment (ALPAC, then Lighthill) came from commissioned reports years apart, while the second winter's reassessment was the market itself — the LISP hardware collapse, with no equivalent report. Two histories, not one template.

Check-yourself answer key. "Does a named bet before a withdrawal in both winters prove a rule?" — No: two episodes sharing an order is not a proven causal sequence.

Teaching limit. No continuous, sourceable AI funding series exists across these years; four discrete funding examples with different currencies and accounting bases are shown instead of one invented total.

Do not let the lenses turn into a prophecy. The correct line is: “these comparisons tell us what to investigate. They do not establish that a withdrawal will occur.”

Stage 3 — Compare

Question. Which of the five comparison lenses still shows a real similarity once you separate description from prediction?

Predict prompt. On the "Constraints" lens, is "Today" an established limit, or an open question?

Activity. Choose one of the five lens buttons; its navy fill marks the selection. Compare the three era headings, then open each era’s Details for the full explanation and named sources. Escape closes the panel and returns focus to its button. Compare causes, not step numbers.

Expected observation. Two cells in the "Today" column stay open — no single binding technical constraint is established, and no comparable broad contraction is established either — while the claims, money and reassessment lenses do resolve to sourced present-day evidence.

Check-yourself answer key. "Does an open cell mean a winter is currently impossible?" — No: open means not yet established either way, not ruled out.

Teaching limit. Calling a present-day cell "open" is this page's own reading of the evidence available at build time, not a citation that says "unresolved."

“Hindsight makes old constraints easier to see. It does not prove that today has the same constraint or that it must end the same way. Ask what evidence would support the claim.”

Stage 4 — Today

Question. Which rhyme still holds once you weigh it against a genuine difference?

Predict prompt. Pick one rhyme and one difference — does the rhyme survive once you weigh the difference against it?

Activity. Expand a similarity and a difference, giving both columns roughly equal time; the supporting statements start closed so the room investigates one claim at a time. Close on the checklist, not a verdict: is it a forecast or an assessment, what evidence supports it, and what evidence would move it.

Expected observation. The strongest rhyme (an evaluation warning about SWE-bench Verified) and the strongest difference (measured scaling behaviour and reported consumer reach) do not cancel each other — each still needs its own evidence, which is the point.

Check-yourself answer key. "Does a bigger user base rule out a contraction?" — No: user count answers a different question than whether funding, evaluation trust, or returns hold up.

Teaching limit. Historical comparison is not a prediction; this curated set of near-term forecasts that missed does not establish a representative error rate for optimists, pessimists, or AI research generally.

Misconceptions

"An unresolved forecast should count as a failure." No. Only a forecast whose window has closed one way or the other gets a verdict; an open claim stays unscored (Stage 1).
"The same order appearing in both winters proves a fixed sequence." No. Two historical cases sharing a pattern is a reason to look closer, not a general law about every contraction (Stage 2).
"An open cell in the 'Today' column means a winter cannot happen." No. Open means the evidence has not established a limit or withdrawal yet — not that one is ruled out (Stage 3).
"More users than any earlier boom means this one avoids a contraction." No. Scale of adoption is a genuine, sourced difference, but it answers a different question than whether funding, evaluation trust, or returns hold up (Stage 4).

Discussion questions

  • If someone states a confident AI timeline today, what would you need to see before you could score it — and by when could you score it?
  • The first winter's reassessment came from a commissioned report; the second came from a market collapse with no report at all. Does the absence of a report today mean nothing is wrong, or only that the evidence would look different if something were?
  • Pick one "Today" claim from Stage 4. What single piece of evidence would move your confidence in it the most, in either direction?

Questions you will get

“So is it a bubble or not?”

Refuse the binary, in one sentence, then give the useful answer: current funding, reported usage, technical reliability, and returns are distinct questions. Earlier eras also had deployed systems and organisational users, though current consumer reach is much larger. A financial correction and a technological dead end are different claims and need different evidence.

“Aren’t the winter dates made up?”

Partly, and the demo says so. The bands are conventions for broader contractions, not dates stamped by a single event. The 1987 hardware collapse is a useful second-winter anchor; the first has more distributed causes and withdrawals, including but not limited to ALPAC. The ends are also conventional and historians disagree; that is why the bands have soft edges and explanatory notes.

“Where did you get this?”

Open Settings → Run the self-test in front of them. It re-derives, live, that every claim resolves to a declared source, that no source is cited and never used, and that the counts quoted on screen match the data. Then open Stage 4, which lists every source under Every source and, separately, everything researched and cut for lack of one. Both supporting sections open on request.

“Why is that quote from a quotation-checking website?”

Because the Simon and Minsky lines are routed through a quotation-checking source that cites printed books and page references. This build has not inspected those original pages, so it labels the claims reported rather than primary. That distinction is part of the lesson: a plausible citation route is not the same as direct verification.
