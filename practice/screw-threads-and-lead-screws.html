Pitch, travel and force

1. Lead: A triple-start screw has pitch 1.5 mm. What is its lead in mm per revolution?

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

L = pitch × starts = 1.5 × 3 = 4.5 mm/rev. The 1.5 mm spacing is pitch; it is not the travel per turn for this screw.

2. Actuator speed: A single-start ACME actuator has 0.20 in pitch and turns at an actual 900 rpm. Its given efficiency is 60%. Find linear speed in in/min during steady engaged travel; the nut cannot rotate.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

L = 0.20 × 1 = 0.20 in/rev. Then v = 900 × 0.20 = 180 in/min (3 in/s). Applying 60% to speed would give 108 in/min; efficiency affects force, not the engaged motion ratio.

3. Actuator force: A single-start ACME actuator with 0.20 in pitch receives 12 lb-in (lbf·in) of torque. Given overall efficiency 60%, find actual axial force during steady motion against a resisting load. Round to the nearest lbf.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

F = 0.60 × 2π × 12 / 0.20 = 226.1947 ≈ 226 lbf. The theoretical force is 376.9911 lbf. Omitting 2π would give only 36 lbf.

4. Handle torque: A 120 N hand force acts perpendicular to a vise handle, 180 mm from the screw axis. Find screw input torque in N·m.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

From [[torque]], T = rF = 0.180 × 120 = 21.6 N·m. The supplied 180 mm is already the axis-to-hand radius; do not halve it.

5. Clamping estimate: An illustrative single-start square-thread vise has 4 mm pitch and receives 21.6 N·m. Assume a given overall tightening efficiency of 30%, not a measured typical vise value. Estimate force while tightening, in kN, to two decimal places.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

L = 0.004 m/rev. F = 0.30 × 2π × 21.6 / 0.004 = 10,178.7602 N = 10.18 kN. Using π = 3.14 gives 10.1736 kN, or 10.17 kN rounded; both are accepted. This is not a rated vise capacity or a residual static-force prediction. Keeping 4 mm beside N·m without conversion makes the result 1,000 times too small.

6. Free jaw travel: Before contacting the work, a vise's single-start 4 mm pitch screw turns at 24 rpm. Find jaw speed in mm/s. The nut is restrained from rotation and backlash has been taken up.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

v = 24 × 4 = 96 mm/min. Divide by 60: 1.6 mm/s. The jaw cannot keep travelling at that speed through a rigid clamped workpiece.

7. Table feed: A CNC positioning slide uses a triple-start screw of 1.5 mm pitch, rotating at an actual 480 rpm. Given efficiency is 55%. Find steady feed in mm/s with the nut restrained from rotating and clearance taken up.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

L = 1.5 × 3 = 4.5 mm/rev. v = 480 × 4.5 / 60 = 36 mm/s. Pitch instead of lead gives 12 mm/s; applying efficiency gives 19.8 mm/s; leaving minutes unconverted gives 2,160 with the wrong unit.

8. Available thrust: A triple-start screw of 1.5 mm pitch receives 0.75 N·m at steady speed against a resisting load. Given assembly efficiency is 55%. Find actual axial force to the nearest N. This is available screw thrust, before allocating force to guide friction or other loads; it is not a cutting-capacity rating.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

L = 1.5 × 3 / 1,000 = 0.0045 m/rev. F = 0.55 × 2π × 0.75 / 0.0045 = 575.9587 ≈ 576 N. Using pitch instead of lead gives 1,727.876 N; omitting 2π gives 91.667 N.

9. Required torque: A two-start screw with 2.5 mm pitch must deliver 800 N at steady speed. Its given efficiency is 50%. Find the input torque in N·m, to two decimal places.

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

L = 2.5 × 2 = 5 mm = 0.005 m. T = FL/(2πη) = 800 × 0.005/(2π × 0.50) = 1.27324 ≈ 1.27 N·m. Divide by efficiency when solving for the required input.

Callouts and thread selection

10. Inch coarse callout: What pitch in inches is specified by 1/4-20 UNC? This checked designation appears in NBS Circular 479; the source entry and link are in [[screw-threads]].

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

20 is threads per inch: pitch = 1/20 = 0.050 in. The 1/4 is nominal diameter. UNC identifies the coarse series.

11. Inch fine callout: Find the pitch of 1/4-28 UNF in inches, to four decimal places. The NBS Circular 479 designation is checked and linked in [[screw-threads]].

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

pitch = 1/28 = 0.035714… ≈ 0.0357 in. At the same nominal diameter, the fine series has a smaller pitch than the coarse series.

12. Metric callout: What pitch in mm does M6 × 1.0 specify? The NAAMS standard entry is quoted and linked in [[screw-threads]].

Enter a number, then check it.
Show worked solution

The pitch is written directly: 1.0 mm. M6 gives the metric family and nominal 6 mm diameter. Do not invert the pitch as though it were a threads-per-inch count.

13. One-way thrust: Which form deliberately uses an asymmetric working flank to favor heavy axial thrust in one direction? Choose the form, not a rated capacity: 1 = V, 2 = square, 3 = ACME/trapezoidal, 4 = buttress.

Choose a form, then check it.
Show worked solution

4 — buttress. Its working flank and sloping back are intentionally different. Reverse motion is possible, but heavy loading on the other flank does not keep the same strength and efficiency advantages.

14. Repeated travel: A slide needs a power-screw form suited to repeated travel in both directions, easier machining than square, and a split-nut arrangement to take up wear clearance. Which conventional form best matches? 1 = V, 2 = square, 3 = ACME/trapezoidal, 4 = buttress.

Choose a form, then check it.
Show worked solution

3 — ACME/trapezoidal. This trades some square-thread efficiency for practical manufacturing and nut-adjustment advantages. Actual wear life, efficiency and load rating still require assembly data.
